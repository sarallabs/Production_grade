# Podcast Audio Script: Critical Reading as Looking for Ways of Thinking (Short-Form)

**Subject:** Reading and Writing Skills | **Topic:** Critical Reading as Ways of Thinking  
**Format:** Dual-Host Academic Dialogue (Host & Guest)  
**Target Duration:** ~5 Minutes (~900 words)  
**Institution:** De La Salle University - Senior High School Department  

---

Host: Welcome to SaralVidhya's Rapid Review series for Senior High School Reading and Writing Skills. Today, we're diving into Week Two: "Critical Reading as Looking for Ways of Thinking." I'm joined by Dr. Elena Ramos, our reading specialist. Elena, most students think reading is just scanning words off a page. But critical reading is completely different, isn't it?

Guest: Absolutely! Hirschberg and Hirschberg remind us that there are four kinds of reading. First is casual reading—glancing at phone notifications or street signs because you have to. Second is pleasure reading—curling up with a romance or thriller where you surrender to the author's world. Third is reading for information—memorizing dates or terms for a test. But the fourth kind—critical reading—is an active, intellectual duel. You're not a passive sponge soaking up claims. You enter into a lively mental dialogue with the author: "You say this is true, but what about the counter-evidence?"

Host: It's about shedding gullibility! And Metherell and Winkler point out something crucial in their guidelines: we must demystify the author. 

Guest: Yes! Kicking the writer off the throne of public esteem! Just because someone is published doesn't make them an infallible oracle. Every author carries personal biases, demographic assumptions, and potential blind spots. Critical readers treat an essay not as absolute truth, but as what Michel Montaigne called an "essai"—an attempt, an exploration of an inquiring mind trying to make sense of opposing impulses.

Host: Let's talk about the cognitive engine behind this. Hirschberg outlines a powerful triad: Analysis, Synthesis, and Evaluation. Walk us through how a student uses these three steps.

Guest: Think of Analysis as taking a clock apart. You dissect how the author builds their argument, what premises they rely on, and what hidden warrants they take for granted. Next is Synthesis—putting the gears back together, but incorporating pieces from other clocks! You blend multiple viewpoints from psychologists, historians, or sociologists, and forge an original insight that belongs to you. Synthesis tampers down intellectual arrogance because you realize that on complex issues, multiple legitimate perspectives exist.

Host: And the final step is Evaluation?

Guest: Exactly. That's where you place your own weight on the seesaw. You judge whether the author's evidence is sufficient, recent, and logically sound, avoiding classic fallacies like ad hominem or either-or black-and-white thinking.

Host: In Chapter 2 of *Writing and Reading Across the Curriculum*, Behrens and Rosen provide two master questions that turn evaluation into a formal critique.

Guest: Yes! Question 1: "To what extent does the author succeed in their purpose?" Whether that purpose is to inform, persuade, or entertain. If it's informative, is it accurate, significant, and fairly interpreted? If it's persuasive, are the claims logically supported? And Question 2: "To what extent do you agree with the author?" This requires evaluating the author's hidden assumptions. Behrens and Rosen examine Charles Krauthammer's op-ed advocating a return to the Moon. Krauthammer admits robotic probes are cheaper and provide better scientific data, yet he insists living humans must go. Why? Because of his hidden romantic assumption that true greatness requires human physical presence! Notice too that Krauthammer, an outspoken conservative small-government columnist, is demanding massive taxpayer government spending—an ideological tension that sharp critical readers immediately spot!

Host: Now, our curriculum pairs critical reading with our diagnostic essay topic: Intertextuality and Hypertext. Many students get intimidated by the word "intertextuality," but Dharwadker and his co-authors give us a crystal-clear framework.

Guest: In simple terms, intertextuality is when a literary, media, or social text appears inside another text. It comes in two primary forms: "Book in a Book"—like when a novelist references Shakespeare's Hamlet or borrows a famous mythological archetype—and "Other Text in a Book," where an author references a social narrative, like Rosa Parks's bus ride or the JFK assassination.

Host: And it's not just casual namedropping, right?

Guest: Not at all! It serves three dynamic functions: Comparison—inviting you to compare the original context with how it's used now; Dialogue—creating an ongoing conversation between two narrative worldviews; and Destabilization—shaking up what you thought you knew about the original story! 

Host: And Dharwadker notes this produces transformative effects!

Guest: Yes! It transforms our understanding of the primary book, it reflexively transforms our memory of the prior text, and it sparks a simultaneous re-reading of both. Meanwhile, digital Hypertext gives us clickable, physical hyperlinks connecting web nodes, but intertextuality provides the cognitive links that connect human culture across centuries.

Host: That is powerful. To read critically is to look for ways of thinking—to become an active participant in human thought rather than an obedient consumer. Thank you, Elena!

Guest: My pleasure! Remember: underline the thesis, hunt for premise and conclusion signals, interrogate the author, evaluate hidden assumptions, and trace the intertextual web!
