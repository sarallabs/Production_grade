# Lesson 2: Functions of Communication and Functional Theories of Language

---

## Course & Lesson Overview

Human communication is rarely aimless; it is purposeful, goal-directed, and intrinsically woven into human social life. Whether managing behavior, building deep personal relationships, motivating collective action, expressing inner psychological states, or transmitting technical knowledge, communication serves distinct functions. 

At a deeper linguistic level, language itself has evolved functionally: its internal grammatical and semantic architecture directly mirrors the social purposes for which humans use it.

### Learning Competencies & Objectives
By the end of this lesson, learners are expected to:
1. Explain the biological, psychological, and social imperatives underlying the human need to interact.
2. Identify, differentiate, and analyze the five primary functions of communication: **Control/Regulation**, **Social Interaction**, **Motivation**, **Emotional Expression**, and **Information Dissemination** (Sipacio & Balgos, 2016).
3. Evaluate communicative scenarios to determine primary and overlapping communicative functions.
4. Trace the historical evolution of functional language theories, comparing extrinsic models by Malinowski, Bühler, Jakobson, Britton, and Morris (Halliday & Hasan, 1989).
5. Understand and apply M. A. K. Halliday’s Systemic Functional Linguistics (SFL), specifically the three overarching **Metafunctions** (Ideational, Interpersonal, Textual).
6. Connect the **Context of Situation** (Field, Tenor, Mode) with the semantic components of natural language.
7. Apply functional communication analysis to practical real-world contexts, including the **Philippine Linguistic Landscape** and video-based behavioral analysis.

## 1. The Human Need to Interact

Communication is not merely an optional activity; it is a fundamental human drive. Anthropologists, psychologists, and sociologists identify several core reasons why human beings must interact:

```
                          ┌──────────────────────────┐
                          │   THE HUMAN IMPERATIVE   │
                          │        TO INTERACT       │
                          └─────────────┬────────────┘
         ┌──────────────────────────────┼──────────────────────────────┐
         ▼                              ▼                              ▼
    Biological &                   Psychological &                 Societal &
  Survival Needs                  Relational Needs              Cultural Needs
• Physical cooperation         • Emotional validation         • Establishing laws
• Coordinated labor            • Affiliation and belonging    • Transmitting heritage
• Warning of hazards           • Self-concept formation       • Collective problem-solving
```

1. **Biological and Survival Needs**: Early humans survived through collaborative hunting, gathering, and mutual protection. Clear signaling of danger and coordination of tasks were vital to physical survival.
2. **Psychological and Emotional Well-being**: Prolonged social isolation leads to cognitive decline and emotional distress. Interaction provides validation, emotional warmth, belonging, and identity affirmation.
3. **Social Cohesion and Order**: Societies establish laws, norms, moral frameworks, and cultural traditions through symbolic interaction. Without communication, collective social institutions cannot exist.

## 2. The Five Core Functions of Communication (Sipacio & Balgos, 2016)

In oral communication pedagogy, communicative acts are categorized into five fundamental functional types:

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                       FIVE CORE FUNCTIONS OF COMMUNICATION                  │
├─────────────────────┬───────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ 1. Control /        │ Managing, directing, and regulating human behavior    │
│    Regulation       │ and social conduct.                                   │
├─────────────────────┼───────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ 2. Social           │ Establishing, maintaining, and strengthening social   │
│    Interaction      │ bonds and interpersonal relationships.                │
├─────────────────────┼───────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ 3. Motivation       │ Inspiring, encouraging, and mobilizing individuals to │
│                     │ pursue goals or improve performance.                  │
├─────────────────────┼───────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ 4. Emotional        │ Venting, conveying, and expressing personal feelings, │
│    Expression       │ affections, passions, and inner states.               │
├─────────────────────┼───────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ 5. Information      │ Conveying factual data, objective knowledge, news,    │
│    Dissemination    │ and educational instructions.                         │
└─────────────────────┴───────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

---

### 2.1 Control / Regulation
**Definition**: Communication functions to control, direct, or regulate the behavior of individuals, groups, or organizations.
* **Purpose**: To enforce obedience, maintain social order, align collective activities, or restrict actions according to rules and legal standards.
* **Verbal Features**: Use of imperative sentences, explicit regulations, authoritative instructions, warnings, and contractual conditions (e.g., *"Wear face masks at all times," "Submit your requirements on Friday," "Keep off the grass"*).
* **Nonverbal Features**: Direct eye contact, firm vocal tone, raised authoritative hand, upright posture, wearing uniforms, or physical boundary markers (e.g., stop signs).
* **Concrete Examples**:
  * A teacher reading and enforcing classroom policies on the first day of class.
  * A police officer directing traffic at a crowded intersection.
  * A medical doctor providing a strict prescription dosage to a patient.

---

### 2.2 Social Interaction
**Definition**: Communication allows individuals to interact with one another to build camaraderie, maintain goodwill, and form relationships.
* **Purpose**: To satisfy the social need for belonging, nurture intimacy, develop friendships, and sustain community ties.
* **Verbal Features**: Casual greetings, inquiries about personal life, informal expressions, invitations, compliments, and playful teasing (e.g., *"Hello! How was your weekend?", "Would you like to have lunch together?", "Congratulations on your new project!"*).
* **Nonverbal Features**: Warm smiles, handshakes, open body language, gentle nods, pleasant vocal modulation, and proximate physical distance.
* **Concrete Examples**:
  * Two friends greeting each other in the hallway and chatting about holiday vacation plans.
  * Welcoming a new neighbor with a batch of cookies and introducing oneself.
  * Participating in an informal family reunion dinner.

---

### 2.3 Motivation
**Definition**: Communication motivates, encourages, or inspires individuals to achieve their potential, persevere through adversity, or accomplish specific goals.
* **Purpose**: To stimulate positive energy, uplift moral resolution, foster confidence, and empower listeners to take constructive action.
* **Verbal Features**: Persuasive exhortations, words of encouragement, inspiring metaphors, positive reinforcement, and aspirational goal-setting (e.g., *"You have worked hard and you can overcome this challenge," "Believe in your vision and take the first step today"*).
* **Nonverbal Features**: Dynamic, energetic vocal delivery, firm and passionate hand gestures, direct and encouraging eye contact, and forward-leaning posture.
* **Concrete Examples**:
  * A basketball coach delivering a passionate halftime pep talk to rally a trailing team.
  * A valedictorian delivering a commencement address urging classmates to serve their nation.
  * A mentor guiding an anxious student to overcome fear of failure.

---

### 2.4 Emotional Expression
**Definition**: Communication facilitates the release and sharing of personal emotions, feelings, vulnerabilities, passions, and psychological states.
* **Purpose**: To express sorrow, joy, affection, frustration, awe, or anger, thereby reducing psychological pressure and inviting empathy.
* **Verbal Features**: Emotive adjectives, heartfelt confessions, expressions of gratitude or grief, and exclamation phrases (e.g., *"I feel completely overwhelmed by this workload," "I am deeply grateful for your support," "I love you with all my heart"*).
* **Nonverbal Features**: Crying, trembling voice, joyful laughter, passionate hugs, sighs, facial expressions of pain, sorrow, or delight.
* **Concrete Examples**:
  * A student confiding personal frustrations and family anxieties to a trusted friend.
  * A bereaved family member giving a tearful, heartfelt tribute at a memorial service.
  * A person expressing exuberant excitement upon passing a board licensing exam.

---

### 2.5 Information Dissemination
**Definition**: Communication functions to convey, distribute, or exchange objective facts, news, scientific data, and educational information.
* **Purpose**: To enlighten, instruct, clarify, update, and resolve ambiguities by providing accurate knowledge.
* **Verbal Features**: Declarative sentences, objective terminology, clear explanations, logical sequences, statistics, and verifiable citations (e.g., *"Typhoon signal number 2 has been raised over Northern Luzon," "The human heart possesses four chambers," "Turn left at the next traffic light"*).
* **Nonverbal Features**: Measured speech rate, visual aids (diagrams, maps, slides), neutral vocal tone, and clear, distinct articulation.
* **Concrete Examples**:
  * A mathematics professor lecturing on the principles of integral calculus.
  * A public health official presenting daily epidemic infection statistics.
  * A museum curator or tourist guide explaining the historical background of a heritage site.

---

### 2.6 Differentiating and Classifying Communicative Situations
In everyday human interaction, communicative acts often integrate multiple functions simultaneously. For example:
* A **State of the Nation Address (SONA)** by a national President primarily serves **Information Dissemination** (reporting government achievements), while also incorporating **Motivation** (inspiring civic unity) and **Control/Regulation** (ordering agencies to execute key legislative mandates).
* A **Physician's Consultation** conveys **Information** (diagnosing an illness), exerts **Control/Regulation** (prescribing medicines and diet restrictions), and offers **Motivation** or **Emotional Expression** (reassuring an anxious patient).

#### Analysis of Practical Situations (Sipacio & Balgos, 2016 Exercises)

| Communicative Situation | Primary Function | Secondary / Overlapping Function |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1. A teacher explains classroom attendance policies and grading rules. | **Control / Regulation** | Information Dissemination |
| 2. Ross greets Rachel and they discuss plans for their upcoming vacation. | **Social Interaction** | Emotional Expression |
| 3. A life coach shares insights on how to maintain inner peace amid chaos. | **Motivation** | Information Dissemination |
| 4. Monica tearfully confides her deepest career anxieties to Chandler. | **Emotional Expression** | Social Interaction |
| 5. A geometry instructor lectures on Euclidean postulates and proofs. | **Information Dissemination** | Control / Regulation |
| 6. A high school valedictorian delivers an uplifting graduation speech. | **Motivation** | Emotional Expression |
| 7. The President delivers the annual address outlining state policy directives. | **Information Dissemination** | Motivation & Control |
| 8. An actress thanks her supportive fans and crew during an interview. | **Emotional Expression** | Social Interaction |
| 9. A city mayor presents concrete flood-control strategies in a public forum. | **Information Dissemination** | Control / Regulation |
| 10. A tourist guide orients a group of international visitors at Intramuros. | **Information Dissemination** | Social Interaction |

## 3. Functional Theories of Language — Extrinsic Perspectives (Halliday & Hasan, 1989, Ch. 2)

Before examining the internal grammar of language, scholars analyzed the functions of language from an **extrinsic** perspective—viewing **"function as synonymous with use"** (the purposes people aim to achieve through speaking, listening, reading, and writing).

```
                     EXTRINSIC THEORIES: FUNCTION EQUALS USE
  ┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │ Malinowski (1923): Pragmatic (Active/Narrative) vs. Magical/Ritual     │
  │ Bühler (1934):     Expressive (1st P) - Conative (2nd P) - Repr (3rd P)│
  │ Jakobson (1960):   Emotive, Conative, Referential, Poetic, Phatic, Meta│
  │ Britton (1970):    Expressive, Transactional (Participant), Poetic     │
  │ Morris (1967):     Information, Mood, Exploratory, Grooming Talking    │
  └────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

### 3.1 Bronislaw Malinowski (1923)
Studying the Trobriand Islanders, anthropologist Bronislaw Malinowski divided language into two broad functional categories:
1. **Pragmatic Functions**: Practical uses of language directly tied to social action and physical survival:
   * *Active*: Directing and coordinating immediate physical activity (e.g., commands during fishing or building).
   * *Narrative*: Recounting past events to inform, plan, and pass down practical knowledge.
2. **Magical / Ritual Functions**: Language used in ceremonial, religious, and sacred rites to influence spiritual forces or affirm collective cultural myths.
3. **Phatic Communion**: Malinowski coined this famous term to describe **language used to establish ties of union by mere exchange of words**, rather than to transmit substantive technical information. Examples include inquiries like *"How do you do?"* or remarks about the weather (*"Nice day, isn't it?"*). Its purpose is to **"oil the social wheels"**, dissolve awkward silence, and establish mutual goodwill.

### 3.2 Karl Bühler (1934) — The Organon Model
Psychologist Karl Bühler viewed language from the standpoint of the individual, deriving his framework from Plato's classic grammatical distinctions of person:
1. **Expressive Language (First Person / Speaker-Oriented)**: Language that reveals the inner emotional state, mood, and subjective perspective of the speaker.
2. **Conative Language (Second Person / Addressee-Oriented)**: Language designed to influence, command, direct, or persuade the listener to act or believe something.
3. **Representational Language (Third Person / Context-Oriented)**: Language directed toward the external world, portraying facts, phenomena, and states of affairs.

### 3.3 Roman Jakobson (1960) — Six Communicative Factors & Functions
Jakobson (Prague Linguistic Circle) extended Bühler’s model by linking six communicative functions to six fundamental factors of communication:

```
                          CONTEXT (Referential)
                                     │
   ADDRESSER (Emotive) ──►  MESSAGE (Poetic)  ──►  ADDRESSEE (Conative)
                                     │
                            CONTACT (Phatic)
                                     │
                             CODE (Metalingual)
```

1. **Referential / Representational Function** (Orientation: *Context*): Communicating cognitive, factual, or objective information about reality.
2. **Emotive / Expressive Function** (Orientation: *Addresser*): Expressing the speaker's immediate attitude, feelings, and emotions.
3. **Conative Function** (Orientation: *Addressee*): Aimed at commanding, persuading, or producing an effect on the listener.
4. **Poetic Function** (Orientation: *Message*): Highlighting the aesthetic qualities, rhythm, rhyme, and linguistic form of the message itself.
5. **Phatic Function** (Orientation: *Contact / Channel*): Establishing, prolonging, verifying, or terminating the communicative channel (e.g., *"Can you hear me?", "Hello?", "Uh-huh"*).
6. **Metalingual / Metalinguistic Function** (Orientation: *Code*): Language used to discuss, explain, clarify, or define language itself (e.g., *"What do you mean by that term?", "'Exigence' means an urgent situational demand"*).

### 3.4 James Britton (1970)
Focusing on language and cognitive development in children's education, Britton established three functional categories:
1. **Expressive Function**: The natural starting point where an individual writes or speaks to externalize their immediate thoughts and feelings.
2. **Transactional Function**: The writer or speaker assumes a **participant role**, using language as an instrument to get things done (informing, explaining, debating, instructing).
3. **Poetic Function**: The writer or speaker assumes a **spectator role**, creating artistic, literary, or contemplated texts for aesthetic contemplation.

### 3.5 Desmond Morris (1967)
In *The Naked Ape*, ethologist Desmond Morris classified human vocal interaction from an animal behaviorist perspective:
1. **Information Talking**: Cooperative, rational exchange of factual data.
2. **Mood Talking**: Communicating feelings, internal moods, and physical states.
3. **Exploratory Talking**: Talking for talking's sake; playful, recreational, and aesthetic wordplay.
4. **Grooming Talking**: The human equivalent of primates picking fleas—meaningless, polite social chatter (phatic communion) used to establish peaceful coexistence.

### 3.6 Comparative Synthesis of Extrinsic Functional Theories
Halliday & Hasan (1989, Figure 2.1) consolidated these theories into a comprehensive matrix, illustrating their underlying commonality:

| Functional Domain | Informative Uses (*Orientation to Content*) | Interactive / Regulatory (*Orientation to Other*) | Expressive Uses (*Orientation to Self*) | Imaginative / Aesthetic Uses | Social Ritual / Channel Uses |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Malinowski (1923)** | Pragmatic (Narrative) | Pragmatic (Active) | — | — | Magical / Ritual; Phatic Communion |
| **Bühler (1934)** | Representational | Conative | Expressive | — | — |
| **Jakobson (1960)** | Referential | Conative | Emotive | Poetic | Phatic; Metalingual |
| **Britton (1970)** | Transactional | Transactional | Expressive | Poetic | — |
| **Morris (1967)** | Information Talking | — | Mood Talking | Exploratory Talking | Grooming Talking |

## 4. Systemic Functional Linguistics & The Metafunctions of Language (M. A. K. Halliday)

While earlier scholars viewed function merely as the external *use* of language, **M. A. K. Halliday** proposed a profound linguistic insight:

> **Function is not simply an external use; it is a fundamental property built into the very architecture of grammar and the semantic system of every natural language.**

Natural languages are inherently **multifunctional**. Every natural clause does not serve just one isolated function; rather, it weaves together three distinct strands of meaning simultaneously. These strands are known as **Metafunctions**.

```
                           THE THREE METAFUNCTIONS OF LANGUAGE
                                   (M. A. K. Halliday)
 ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
 │ 1. IDEATIONAL METAFUNCTION                                                      │
 │    • Experiential: Language representing the world of experience (transitivity, │
 │      processes, participants, circumstances).                                   │
 │    • Logical: Language expressing logical relations between clauses (parataxis, │
 │      hypotaxis, condition, causality).                                          │
 ├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
 │ 2. INTERPERSONAL METAFUNCTION                                                   │
 │    Language as social action, enacting interpersonal relationships, roles,      │
 │    attitudes, and speech functions (offers, commands, statements, questions;    │
 │    mood and modality).                                                          │
 ├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
 │ 3. TEXTUAL METAFUNCTION                                                         │
 │    Language creating texture, coherence, and flow, organizing discourse into    │
 │    a unified message relevant to its environment (theme, information, cohesion).│
 └─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

---

### 4.1 The Three Linguistic Metafunctions

#### 1. The Ideational Metafunction
Language serves as the instrument through which speakers understand, represent, and reflect upon their inner and outer reality:
* **Experiential Meaning**: Expresses what is happening in the real or imagined world. It is realized through the grammatical system of **Transitivity**:
  * *Processes*: Actions, states, mental occurrences, or relations (verbs).
  * *Participants*: Entities carrying out or affected by actions (e.g., **Actor**, **Goal**, **Sayer**, **Sensor**).
  * *Circumstances*: Temporal, spatial, or conditional settings (e.g., Locative, Manner, Time).
* **Logical Meaning**: Expresses abstract logical-semantic relationships connecting multiple ideas or clauses:
  * *Parataxis*: Linking independent clauses of equal grammatical status (coordination: *"and", "or", "but"*).
  * *Hypotaxis*: Subordinating one clause to another to convey dependence (e.g., conditions: *"if... then..."*; causality: *"because"*).

#### 2. The Interpersonal Metafunction
Language serves as the instrument of social action and human interaction:
* Enacts social relationships, establishes speaker-listener roles, and negotiates power, intimacy, and solidarity.
* Realized through the grammatical system of **Mood and Modality**:
  * *Speech Functions*: Giving information (**Statement**), demanding information (**Question**), giving goods-and-services (**Offer**), or demanding goods-and-services (**Command**).
  * *Mood Structure*: Choice of Subject, Finite verbs, imperatives, declaratives, or interrogatives.
  * *Modality*: Expressing probability, necessity, obligation, or willingness (e.g., *"might", "must", "will"*).

#### 3. The Textual Metafunction
Language serves as the operational instrument that creates **texture**—weaving experiential and interpersonal strands into a coherent, organized text:
* Distinguishes a cohesive, living text from a random assortment of disconnected words.
* Realized through:
  * *Thematic Structure*: **Theme** (the departure point of the clause; what the clause is about) and **Rheme** (the remainder of the message providing new information).
  * *Information Structure*: The flow of Given (familiar) information and New (prominent) information.
  * *Cohesive Devices*: Reference (pronouns, demonstratives), lexical cohesion, conjunctions, and ellipsis.

---

### 4.2 Systematic Relation Between Context of Situation and Semantic Metafunctions

Halliday and Hasan demonstrated that there is an exact, systematic correlation between the external **Context of Situation** and the internal **Semantic Metafunctions** of text:

```
    SITUATION: Feature of Context                     TEXT: Functional Semantic Component
 ┌──────────────────────────────────┐               ┌─────────────────────────────────────┐
 │       FIELD OF DISCOURSE         │  realized by  │        EXPERIENTIAL MEANING         │
 │     (What is taking place)       ├──────────────►│    (Transitivity, processes,        │
 │                                  │               │     participants, circumstances)    │
 ├──────────────────────────────────┤               ├─────────────────────────────────────┤
 │       TENOR OF DISCOURSE         │  realized by  │        INTERPERSONAL MEANING        │
 │  (Who is taking part; relations  ├──────────────►│     (Mood, modality, speech roles,  │
 │   of power, status, & solidarity)│               │      attitudes, person choices)     │
 ├──────────────────────────────────┤               ├─────────────────────────────────────┤
 │       MODE OF DISCOURSE          │  realized by  │          TEXTUAL MEANING            │
 │ (Role assigned to language;      ├──────────────►│     (Theme, information focus,      │
 │  channel, medium, spoken/written)│               │      cohesive relations, rhythm)    │
 └──────────────────────────────────┘               └─────────────────────────────────────┘
```

#### Halliday's Classic Demonstration: Ben Jonson’s "Song: To Celia"
Halliday analyzes the poetic line:
> *"Or leave a kiss within the cup, and I'll not ask for wine."*

* **Experiential Strand (Field)**: The clause represents human physical processes: an action of leaving an entity (**kiss**) in a physical place (**within the cup**), contrasted with a verbal process of demanding a substance (**wine**).
* **Interpersonal Strand (Tenor)**: The clause executes two distinct speech functions: a polite command/request (*"leave a kiss..."*) followed by an offer (*"and I will not ask..."*). The poet sets up an intimate, reciprocal exchange: *"If you do this for me, then I undertake to do that for you."*
* **Textual Strand (Mode)**: The clause is patterned poetically and rhythmically to be spoken or sung, with parallel grammatical clauses connected paratactically by *"and"*, using thematic markers (*"Or", "I"*) to balance the line.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Key Takeaway**: We cannot divide a sentence into separate physical pieces and say, *"This third is ideational, this third is interpersonal, and this third is textual."* Rather, the entire sentence is **multifunctional**; it simultaneously communicates ideas (Ideational), manages relationships (Interpersonal), and creates cohesive discourse (Textual).

## 5. Practical Applications & Applied Analytical Frameworks

### 5.1 Analyzing the Philippine Linguistic Landscape
The **Linguistic Landscape** refers to the visibility and salience of languages on public signs, commercial billboards, government notices, street names, and directional posters within a given territory or community (Landry & Bourhis, 1997).

In the Philippines, the linguistic landscape displays rich multilingual diversity (English, Filipino, and regional languages like Tagalog, Cebuano, or Ilokano). Applying communication functions illuminates the social purpose of public signage:

```
│ Official Regulatory Warning     │ "BAWAL TUMAWID: NAKAMAMATAY"    │ Control / Regulation              │
│                                 │ ("Do Not Cross: Deadly")        │ (Strict behavioral restriction;   │
│                                 │                                 │  enforces public pedestrian safety)│
├─────────────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────────────┼───────────────────────────────────┤
│ Public Health Advisory          │ "Magsuot ng Face Mask at        │ Control / Regulation &            │
│                                 │ Mag-sanitize ng Kamay"          │ Information Dissemination         │
├─────────────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────────────┼───────────────────────────────────┤
│ Commercial Billboard            │ "Iba ang May Pinagsamahan"      │ Motivation & Social Interaction   │
│ (Brand Advertising)             │ (San Miguel Beer slogan)        │ (Invoking cultural camaraderie,   │
│                                 │                                 │  solidarity, and belonging)       │

```

* **Field**: Indicates the civic, commercial, or environmental activity taking place (e.g., waste management, pedestrian transit, public health).
* **Tenor**: Establishes the relationship of authority between the state/sign maker and the general public (commanding vs. inviting).
* **Mode**: Written, highly concise public visual display designed for rapid comprehension by commuters.

### 5.2 Short Video Analysis Framework: *A Guide to Effective Communication*
*(Video Resource: `https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JwjAAgGi-90`)*

The training resource *A Guide to Effective Communication* examines how interpersonal friction, passive-aggressive habits, and communication breakdowns develop in professional and everyday environments. Key insights include:

#### 1. Communication Styles
* **Passive Communication**: Failing to express genuine needs, feelings, or boundaries; allowing others to infringe upon one's rights; breeding resentment.
* **Aggressive Communication**: Demanding one's desires through intimidation, shouting, interruption, or humiliation; destroying psychological safety.
* **Assertive Communication (Optimal)**: Expressing thoughts, needs, and boundaries directly, honestly, and respectfully without violating the rights of others.

#### 2. Causes of Communication Breakdowns
* **Selective Listening**: Hearing only parts of a message that confirm existing biases while tuning out critical clarifications.
* **Defensive Reaction**: Assuming personal attack when receiving constructive feedback; counter-attacking instead of listening.
* **Ambiguous Messaging**: Using vague expressions, contradictory body language, or sarcastic intonations that obscure the true communicative intent.

#### 3. Strategies for Restoring Communication
* **Active Listening**: Focusing undivided attention on the speaker, observing nonverbal cues, and validating their perspective before responding.
* **Reflective Feedback**: Paraphrasing what was heard (*"What I understand you saying is... Is that correct?"*) to verify shared meaning.
* **Constructive "I" Statements**: Framing issues using *"I feel [emotion] when [action] occurs because [reason]"* rather than accusatory *"You"* statements (*"You always mess up"*).

## 6. Synthesis: Integrating Communication Functions with Metafunctions

To master oral communication, students should recognize how the practical functional categories of speech map directly onto underlying linguistic systems:

| Everyday Communication Function (SHS Framework) | Corresponding Metafunction (Halliday's SFL) | Context of Situation Dimension | Grammatical & Behavioral Realization |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Control / Regulation** | **Interpersonal Metafunction** | **Tenor** (Authority, power disparity) | Imperative mood, commands, strict vocal tone, authoritative posture. |
| **Social Interaction** | **Interpersonal & Textual** | **Tenor & Mode** (Solidarity, contact) | Phatic inquiries, greetings, informal pronouns, open body language. |
| **Motivation** | **Interpersonal & Ideational** | **Tenor & Field** (Leadership, aspiration) | Persuasive modal verbs (*"we can", "we must"*), inspirational metaphors, energetic delivery. |
| **Emotional Expression** | **Interpersonal Metafunction** | **Tenor** (Intimacy, vulnerability) | Exclamations, emotive adjectives, self-oriented first person (*"I"*), tears, laughter. |
| **Information Dissemination** | **Ideational (Experiential)** | **Field** (Facts, processes, states) | Declarative mood, transitivity structures (actors, processes), neutral rate, visual aids. |
