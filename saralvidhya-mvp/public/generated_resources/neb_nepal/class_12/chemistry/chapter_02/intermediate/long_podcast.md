Exactly. And the solutions that conduct electricity? Those are called electrolytic solutions. The properties of these solutions are determined by the ions present, and there’s often an equilibrium between these ions and the undissociated molecules of the substance in the solution. This is what we call ionic equilibrium.

So, to start unpacking this, let's look at the foundational ideas. The Arrhenius Theory of Ionization, proposed by Svante Arrhenius in 1887, was one of the first major steps in understanding electrolytes in solution.

Right. The modern form of Arrhenius's theory has several key postulates. First, when an electrolyte – that's an acid, base, or salt – dissolves in a polar solvent like water, it breaks down into electrically charged particles called ions. Positive ions are cations, and negative ions are anions. The general equation for this is AB dissociating into A⁺ and B⁻.

And it's not just in solution that these ions exist. Arrhenius also proposed that even solid electrolytes are composed of ions held together by electrostatic forces. When they dissolve, these forces are overcome, and the ions separate. So, you can have A⁺B⁻ in solid form, and then it dissociates into A⁺ and B⁻ in solution.

This leads to a very important point: in solution, these ions can also recombine to form the original undissociated molecules. This creates a dynamic equilibrium between the ions and the undissociated molecules. This equilibrium is described by something called the ionization constant. For our example, AB ⇌ A⁺ + B⁻, the ionization constant, K, is [A⁺][B⁻] / [AB].

And a crucial aspect of any solution is its electrical neutrality. Arrhenius pointed out that in any electrolyte solution, the total positive charge from cations must equal the total negative charge from anions.

This electrical neutrality is also why these solutions conduct electricity. When you pass an electric current, the ions move towards oppositely charged electrodes. So, only substances that ionize in solution are conductors of electricity.

The theory also states that the properties of an electrolyte in solution are due to the ions it produces. And interestingly, each ion formed contributes to osmotic pressure and other colligative properties as if it were a whole molecule. So, an ion behaves osmotically like a molecule.

Finally, Arrhenius's theory introduced the concept of the degree of ionization, denoted by alpha (α). This is the fraction of the total molecules that exist as free ions in the solution. It’s calculated as the number of molecules ionized into ions divided by the total number of molecules dissolved.

That’s a great overview of the Arrhenius postulates. Now, let's move on to what influences this degree of ionization. Several factors play a role.

The first, and perhaps most obvious, is the nature of the electrolyte itself. Strong electrolytes ionize almost completely, while weak ones ionize only feebly.

The nature of the solvent is also critical. A solvent's ability to weaken the forces holding ions together is key. This is measured by its dielectric constant. A higher dielectric constant means the solvent is better at separating ions.

Then there's concentration. Ionization is inversely proportional to concentration. If a solution is less concentrated, the solute will ionize to a greater extent. This makes sense because more solvent molecules are available to interact with and separate solute molecules. So, dilution generally increases ionization.

And temperature also plays a role. Higher temperatures generally lead to greater ionization because increased molecular velocity reduces interionic forces of attraction.

This dynamic equilibrium between ions and unionized molecules is the basis for Ostwald's Dilution Law, developed by Ostwald in 1888. It's essentially an application of the law of mass action to ionic equilibrium.

Let's walk through that. Suppose we have a binary electrolyte AB, and one gram molecule is dissolved in V liters. If 'α' is its degree of dissociation, initially we have 1 mole of AB and 0 moles of ions. At equilibrium, we have (1 - α) moles of AB, and α moles each of A⁺ and B⁻.

The active masses, or concentrations, at equilibrium are then [AB] = (1 - α)/V, [A⁺] = α/V, and [B⁻] = α/V. Applying the law of mass action, the rate of ionization is proportional to (1 - α)/V, and the rate of combination is proportional to (α/V) × (α/V).

At equilibrium, these rates are equal. So, k₁(1 - α)/V = k₂α²/V². Rearranging this, we get the ionization constant, Kc = α² / [(1 - α)V]. This equation is Ostwald's Dilution Law, showing how the degree of dissociation changes with dilution.

For weak electrolytes, where 'α' is very small, we can approximate (1 - α) as 1. This simplifies the equation to Kc = α²/V, or α = √(Kc × V). This means the degree of dissociation is proportional to the square root of dilution.

For strong electrolytes, however, 'α' is large and cannot be neglected. We have to use the original equation, Kc = α² / [(1 - α)V], which leads to a quadratic equation for 'α'.

Ostwald's Dilution Law is experimentally verified by measuring 'α' at different dilutions and checking if Kc remains constant. This works well for weak electrolytes, but it fails completely for strong electrolytes.

The reason it fails for strong electrolytes is that they are essentially completely ionized at all dilutions, meaning 'α' is close to 1. The law assumes a dynamic equilibrium between ions and undissociated molecules, which isn't really the case for strong electrolytes.

Moving on from ionization, let's talk about acids and bases. Historically, the concept of acids was linked to sour taste, and alkalis to ashes. Lavoisier initially thought all acids contained oxygen, but Davy showed HCl didn't. The first widely accepted generalization came from Arrhenius.

Arrhenius defined an acid as a substance that ionizes in aqueous solution to produce hydrogen ions (H⁺), and a base as one that produces hydroxyl ions (OH⁻). For example, HCl is an acid because it gives H⁺ and Cl⁻, and NaOH is a base because it gives Na⁺ and OH⁻.

An acid-base reaction, according to Arrhenius, is the combination of H⁺ from the acid and OH⁻ from the base to form water: H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l). The strength of an acid or base depends on its degree of ionization. Strong acids like HCl are almost completely ionized, while weak acids like acetic acid are only partially ionized. The same applies to bases.

However, the Arrhenius concept has limitations. It's only applicable in aqueous solutions, doesn't explain the acidic nature of oxides like CO₂ or the basic nature of oxides like CaO, and it struggles with the concept of a bare proton (H⁺) in solution, which is actually highly hydrated as H₃O⁺ (hydronium ion).

This leads us to the more general Bronsted-Lowry concept, proposed independently by Bronsted and Lowry in 1923. Here, an acid is defined as a proton donor, and a base as a proton acceptor.

This concept is much broader. An acid-base reaction is simply a proton transfer. For instance, HCl donating a proton to water: HCl + H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + Cl⁻. Here, HCl is the acid and H₂O acts as a base. Conversely, NH₃ accepting a proton from water: NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻. Here, NH₃ is the base and H₂O acts as an acid.

A key feature is that acids and bases are interdependent. You can't have an acid without a base to accept its proton, and vice versa. Substances that can act as both acids and bases, like water, are called amphoteric.

And this concept introduces conjugate acid-base pairs. When an acid donates a proton, it forms its conjugate base. When a base accepts a proton, it forms its conjugate acid. For example, in HCl + NH₃ ⇌ NH₄⁺ + Cl⁻, Cl⁻ is the conjugate base of HCl, and NH₄⁺ is the conjugate acid of NH₃.

A crucial insight here is the relative strength of conjugate pairs. A strong acid has a weak conjugate base, and a weak acid has a strong conjugate base. For example, HCl (strong acid) forms Cl⁻ (weak base), while acetic acid (weak acid) forms acetate ion (strong base).

The Bronsted-Lowry concept is powerful, but it also has limitations. It doesn't explain the behavior of some non-metallic oxides like CO₂ as acids, or metallic oxides like Na₂O as bases, nor does it explain the acidity of salts like AlCl₃.

That's where the Lewis concept, proposed by Gilbert N. Lewis in 1923, comes in. This is the most general concept. A Lewis acid is an electron-pair acceptor, and a Lewis base is an electron-pair donor.

So, a Lewis base has an unshared pair of electrons, like ammonia (NH₃) or hydroxide ions (OH⁻). A Lewis acid has a vacant orbital to accept that electron pair, like BF₃ or cations like Ag⁺. A Lewis acid-base reaction involves the formation of a coordinate covalent bond. For example, BF₃ + NH₃ → F₃B←NH₃.

Lewis acids include cations, electron-deficient molecules like BF₃ and AlCl₃, and molecules with vacant d-orbitals. The Lewis concept is broader, not dependent on specific elements, and helps interpret reaction mechanisms. However, it doesn't easily explain relative acid-base strengths, and it includes all reactions forming coordinate bonds, which might be too general.

Let's touch upon the autoionization of water. Pure water conducts electricity very slightly, indicating the presence of ions. This is due to water molecules acting as both acids and bases, transferring a proton between them.

This process, 2H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + OH⁻, is called autoionization. For simplicity, we often write it as H₂O ⇌ H⁺ + OH⁻. This autoionization is fundamental to understanding pH.

The equilibrium constant for this is Kc = [H⁺][OH⁻] / [H₂O]. Since the concentration of undissociated water is essentially constant, we define the ionic product of water, Kw = [H⁺][OH⁻]. At 25°C, Kw is 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴.

In pure water, [H⁺] equals [OH⁻]. So, [H⁺]² = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴, meaning [H⁺] = [OH⁻] = 1.0 × 10⁻⁷ moles per liter. Any solution where these concentrations are equal is neutral.

If [H⁺] > 1.0 × 10⁻⁷, the solution is acidic. If [OH⁻] > 1.0 × 10⁻⁷ (meaning [H⁺] < 1.0 × 10⁻⁷), the solution is basic.

To simplify expressing these concentrations, Sorensen introduced the pH scale. pH is defined as the negative logarithm (base 10) of the hydrogen ion concentration: pH = -log₁₀[H⁺]. So, [H⁺] = 10⁻pH.

Similarly, pOH = -log₁₀[OH⁻]. And there's a direct relationship: pH + pOH = pKw. At 25°C, since Kw = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴, pKw = 14. So, pH + pOH = 14.

The pH scale typically ranges from 0 to 14. Solutions with pH 0-2 are strongly acidic, 2-4 moderately acidic, 4-7 weakly acidic. pH 7 is neutral. pH 7-10 is weakly basic, 10-12 moderately basic, and 12-14 strongly basic.

Now, let's consider the dissociation of weak electrolytes, like weak acids and bases. They exist in equilibrium with their ions. For a weak monobasic acid HA, the dissociation is HA ⇌ H⁺ + A⁻.

The dissociation constant, Ka, is given by Ka = [H⁺][A⁻] / [HA]. A higher Ka value indicates a stronger acid. The strength of an acid is directly related to its Ka.

If C is the molar concentration and α is the degree of dissociation, then at equilibrium, [HA] = C(1-α), [H⁺] = Cα, and [A⁻] = Cα. Substituting this into the Ka expression gives Ka = Cα² / (1 - α).

For very weak acids where α is small, this simplifies to Ka = Cα², or α = √(Ka / C). This means the H⁺ concentration, [H⁺] = Cα = C√(Ka/C) = √(KaC). The H⁺ concentration is proportional to the square root of the dissociation constant and the square root of the concentration.

The relative strength of two weak acids, with the same concentration, is the square root of the ratio of their dissociation constants: α₁/α₂ = √(Ka₁/Ka₂).

The same principles apply to weak bases. For a base BOH ⇌ B⁺ + OH⁻, the dissociation constant is Kb = [B⁺][OH⁻] / [BOH]. A higher Kb means a stronger base.

Similar to acids, Kb = Cα² / (1 - α), simplifying to Kb = Cα² for weak bases. The degree of ionization is α = √(Kb / C), and the hydroxide ion concentration is [OH⁻] = Cα = √(KbC).

The relative strength of two weak bases at the same concentration is α₁/α₂ = √(Kb₁/Kb₂).

To make dealing with these small dissociation constants easier, we use pKa and pKb. pKa = -log₁₀Ka, and pKb = -log₁₀Kb. A smaller pKa means a stronger acid, and a smaller pKb means a stronger base.

Many acids and bases are polyprotic, meaning they have more than one ionizable hydrogen. They dissociate in stages, each with its own dissociation constant. For example, carbonic acid (H₂CO₃) dissociates in two stages:

H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻ (Ka₁)

HCO₃⁻ ⇌ H⁺ + CO₃²⁻ (Ka₂)

Ka₁ is always greater than Ka₂, because it's harder to remove a proton from a negatively charged ion.

Similarly, orthophosphoric acid (H₃PO₄) is tribasic and dissociates in three stages, with Ka₁ >> Ka₂ >> Ka₃. The H⁺ ions from the first dissociation also suppress subsequent dissociations due to the common ion effect.

Let's talk about solubility product. Some salts, like BaSO₄ and AgCl, are sparingly soluble. In a saturated solution, there's an equilibrium between the solid salt and its ions.

For AgCl(s) ⇌ Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq), the solubility product, Ksp, is Ksp = [Ag⁺][Cl⁻]. For a general sparingly soluble salt AxBy, Ksp = [Aʸ⁺]ˣ [Bˣ⁻]ʸ. This Ksp is constant at a given temperature.

The solubility product principle states that the ionic product in a saturated solution cannot exceed the Ksp. If the ionic product exceeds Ksp, precipitation occurs.

The relationship between solubility (s) and Ksp is direct. For a binary electrolyte AB, Ksp = s², so s = √Ksp. For AxBy, Ksp = (xs)ʸ(ys)ˣ, and so on.

Applications of solubility product include calculating solubility, predicting precipitation, and in qualitative analysis. For example, adding HCl to a saturated NaCl solution causes NaCl to precipitate because the increased Cl⁻ concentration (common ion effect) makes the ionic product exceed Ksp.

The common ion effect is the suppression of the ionization of a weak electrolyte by the addition of a strong electrolyte that has an ion in common. For example, adding NH₄Cl to NH₄OH solution suppresses the ionization of NH₄OH.

Buffer solutions are crucial. They resist changes in pH upon addition of small amounts of acid or base. They typically consist of a weak acid and its conjugate base, or a weak base and its conjugate acid.

An acidic buffer, like acetic acid and sodium acetate, works because the acetate ions can neutralize added H⁺ ions, forming weakly ionized acetic acid. Added OH⁻ ions are neutralized by H⁺ ions, which are then replenished by further ionization of acetic acid.

The Henderson-Hasselbalch equation relates pH, pKa, and the concentrations of the acid and its salt: pH = pKa + log([Salt]/[Acid]). If concentrations are equal, pH = pKa.

Buffer solutions are vital in biological systems, like blood, maintaining a stable pH. They are also used in industries for manufacturing, in agriculture, and in chemical analysis.

pH changes during acid-base titrations are also important. The titration curve shows how pH changes as titrant is added. The sharp change in pH around the equivalence point helps in choosing an appropriate indicator.

For strong acid-strong base titrations, the pH at the equivalence point is 7. For weak acid-strong base, it's above 7, and for strong acid-weak base, it's below 7. Weak acid-weak base titrations have no sharp pH change.

Salts can be classified as simple, double, complex, or mixed salts based on their formation and behavior in solution.

Hydrolysis is the reaction of ions of a salt with water, which can make the solution acidic, basic, or neutral. Salts of strong acid and strong base don't hydrolyze. Salts of weak acid and strong base are basic due to anion hydrolysis. Salts of strong acid and weak base are acidic due to cation hydrolysis. Salts of weak acid and weak base can be acidic, basic, or neutral depending on the relative strengths of the acid and base.
