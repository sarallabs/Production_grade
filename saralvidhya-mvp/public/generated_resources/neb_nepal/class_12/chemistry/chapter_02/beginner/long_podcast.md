Let's start with a foundational concept: 2.1 The Postulates of Arrhenius Theory of Ionization. Back in 1887, Svante Arrhenius came up with a theory to explain how electrolytes behave in solution. It's often called Arrhenius theory of ionization or electrolytic dissociation.

He laid out some fundamental ideas. First, when an electrolyte – that's an acid, base, or salt – dissolves in water or another polar solvent, it produces two types of electrically charged particles: ions. Positively charged ones are cations, and negatively charged ones are anions. Think of a simple reaction like AB breaking down into A⁺ and B⁻.

His modern theory also assumes that even a solid electrolyte is made of ions held together by electrostatic forces. When it dissolves, these forces are overcome, and the ions separate, like A⁺B⁻ becoming A⁺ and B⁻.

Interestingly, Arrhenius also proposed that in solution, these ions don't just stay separate. They also try to recombine to form the original unionized molecules. This creates a dynamic equilibrium between the ions and the undissociated molecules. This balance is described by an "ionization constant," which for an electrolyte AB ionizing into A⁺ and B⁻, is expressed as K = [A⁺][B⁻] / [AB].

And a very important point is that an electrolytic solution is always electrically neutral. This means the total number of positive charges from the cations always equals the total number of negative charges from the anions.

Right. Also, if you pass an electric current through the solution, these ions start moving towards the oppositely charged electrodes. This movement of ions is precisely why these solutions conduct electricity. So, only substances that ionize in solution will be electrical conductors.

And the properties of an electrolyte solution are directly attributable to the ions it produces. Each ion, once formed, behaves osmotically like a complete molecule, affecting properties like osmotic pressure and other colligative properties.

Finally, Arrhenius noted that at moderate concentrations, only a fraction of the total molecules actually ionize. This fraction is called the "degree of ionization," symbolized by alpha (α). It’s calculated as the number of molecules ionized into ions divided by the total number of molecules dissolved.

That gives us a solid foundation for understanding ionization. Now, let's talk about 2.2 Factors Affecting the Degree of Ionization. Alpha isn't constant; several things can influence it.

The first is the nature of the electrolyte itself. Strong electrolytes, like strong acids or salts, ionize almost completely. Weak ones, however, like weak acids or bases, only ionize slightly.

Then there's the nature of the solvent. The solvent plays a huge role in separating ions. Its ability to weaken the attractive forces between electrical charges is measured by its "dielectric constant." Water, for example, has a high dielectric constant, making it excellent at dissolving and ionizing many substances. Vacuum has the lowest, serving as the standard.

Third is concentration. The degree of ionization is inversely proportional to the solution's concentration. This means that if a solution is less concentrated, the solute will ionize to a greater extent. Why? Because with more solvent molecules relative to solute molecules, more solute molecules can be ionized. So, dilution generally increases ionization.

And finally, temperature. Higher temperatures generally lead to greater ionization. This is because increased temperature boosts the velocity of molecules, which in turn reduces the interionic forces of attraction, making it easier for ions to separate.

To recap, Arrhenius gave us the initial framework for ionization, explaining how electrolytes create ions, establish equilibrium, and conduct electricity. And we just learned that factors like the electrolyte's strength, the solvent's dielectric constant, concentration, and temperature all play a role in how much a substance ionizes.

Excellent recap! Now, let's move on to 2.3 Ostwald's Dilution Law. This law builds directly on Arrhenius's theory, applying the law of mass action to the dynamic equilibrium between ions and unionized molecules. Wilhelm Ostwald first formulated this in 1888.

Let's walk through the derivation for a simple binary electrolyte, AB. Imagine we dissolve one gram molecule of AB in V litres of water, and 'α' is its degree of dissociation.

Initially, we have 1 molecule of AB and no A⁺ or B⁻ ions. At equilibrium, (1-α) molecules of AB remain, and we have α molecules of A⁺ and α molecules of B⁻.

So, the active masses, or concentrations, at equilibrium would be [AB] = (1-α)/V, [A⁺] = α/V, and [B⁻] = α/V.

Applying the law of mass action, the rate of ionization is proportional to the concentration of AB, so it's k₁(1-α)/V. The rate of recombination is proportional to the product of the ion concentrations, so k₂ (α/V) × (α/V) = k₂α²/V².

At equilibrium, these rates are equal. So, k₁(1-α)/V = k₂α²/V². If we rearrange this to get the ratio of the velocity constants, Kc = k₁/k₂, we find that Kc = α² / [(1-α)V]. This Kc is the ionization or dissociation constant, and it's constant at a given temperature. This equation, showing how the degree of dissociation varies with dilution, is Ostwald's Dilution Law.

Now, for weak electrolytes, the degree of ionization 'α' is very small, much less than 1. So, we can approximate (1-α) as simply 1. This simplifies the equation to Kc = α²/V. Rearranging, α² = Kc × V, or α = √(Kc × V). This tells us that for weak electrolytes, the degree of dissociation is proportional to the square root of the dilution. In other words, diluting a weak electrolyte increases its dissociation.

But for strong electrolytes, 'α' is large – close to 1 – and cannot be neglected. So, we have to use the original, more complex expression, Kc = α² / [(1-α)V], which leads to a quadratic equation to solve for 'α'.

How do we know this law is valid? 2.3.1 Experimental Verification of Ostwald's Dilution Law involves determining 'α' for a binary electrolyte at different dilutions. If we substitute these values back into the equation, and the calculated Kc remains constant, then the law is verified. This holds true for weak electrolytes within reasonable limits.

The degree of dissociation 'α' is often measured using conductivity measurements. We compare the equivalent conductivity at a given dilution (Λv) with the equivalent conductivity at infinite dilution (Λ∞) using the formula α = Λv / Λ∞. Λ∞ can be found using Kohlrausch's law of ionic mobilities.

However, this law isn't without its problems. Let's look at 2.3.2 Limitations of Dilution Law. While it's quite good for weak electrolytes, it fails completely for strong electrolytes.

So, Ostwald's Dilution Law is a powerful tool for understanding weak electrolytes, but its assumptions don't hold up for strong ones.

Next up, we shift gears slightly to 2.4 Concepts of Acids and Bases. The word "acid" comes from the Latin "acidus," meaning sour, which was a key characteristic. "Alkali" comes from the Arabic "al-quili," meaning plant ashes.

Historically, acids and bases have been recognized by certain properties. Acids taste sour, are corrosive, change indicator colors, react with metals to liberate hydrogen, and produce carbon dioxide from carbonates. Bases, or alkaline substances, feel soapy and neutralize acids.

Early ideas, like Lavoisier's in 1787, suggested acids were oxygen compounds. But Humphry Davy proved in 1810 that hydrochloric acid, for example, contained hydrogen and chlorine, not oxygen. The most widely accepted early generalization about acids and bases came from Arrhenius in 1887, the same Arrhenius we just talked about!

Let's dive into 2.4.1 Arrhenius Concept of Acids and Bases. According to him, an acid is a substance that ionizes in aqueous solution to give hydrogen ions (H⁺). Examples include HCl, HNO₃, H₂SO₄, and CH₃COOH. For instance, HCl in water gives H⁺(aq) and Cl⁻(aq).

And a base, in Arrhenius's view, is a substance that ionizes in aqueous solution to give hydroxyl ions (OH⁻). Examples are NaOH, KOH, Ca(OH)₂, and NH₄OH. So, NaOH in water yields Na⁺(aq) and OH⁻(aq).

An acid-base reaction, by Arrhenius's definition, is simply the union of H⁺ ions from the acid with OH⁻ ions from the base to form unionized water molecules: H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l).

The strength of an acid or base depends on how readily it ionizes to produce H⁺ or OH⁻ ions. Strong acids like HCl, HNO₃, and H₂SO₄ are almost completely ionized. Weak acids like CH₃COOH and HCN are only weakly ionized. Similarly, strong bases like NaOH and KOH are almost completely ionized, while weak bases like NH₄OH and Mg(OH)₂ are only slightly ionized.

However, the Arrhenius concept has its Limitations:

(i) It's only applicable to acid-base behavior in aqueous solutions. It couldn't explain reactions in non-aqueous mediums.

(ii) It fails to explain the acidic behavior of oxides like CO₂, SO₂, SO₃, or the basic behavior of oxides like CaO, MgO, Na₂O, even in aqueous solutions.

(iii) It doesn't account for the acidic character of salts like AlCl₃, FeCl₃, or BF₃.

(iv) Finally, the idea of a bare H⁺ proton in water is questionable due to its high hydration energy. In reality, H⁺ ions are associated with water molecules, forming H₃O⁺, the hydronium ion.

These limitations led to a more general theory: 2.4.2 Bronsted-Lowry Concept of Acids and Bases, also known as the Proton Transfer Theory. Proposed independently by J.N. Bronsted and T.M. Lowry in 1923, this concept applies to both aqueous and non-aqueous solutions and is very useful for ionic equilibria.

Here's the core idea: an acid is a substance that donates one or more protons, and a base is a substance that accepts one or more protons. So, an acid is a proton-donor, and a base is a proton-acceptor. An acid-base reaction is simply the transfer of a proton from an acid to a base.

A crucial feature of this concept is that no single substance can act as an acid or a base in isolation. An acid needs a base to donate its proton to, and a base needs an acid to accept a proton from. They are interdependent. The extent of the reaction depends on both the acid's proton-donating ability and the base's proton-accepting tendency.

Let's look at some examples. In HCl(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ H₃O⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq), HCl donates a proton to water, making HCl the acid and water the base. Water accepts the proton to become H₃O⁺. In NH₃(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ NH₄⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq), water donates a proton to ammonia, so water is the acid and ammonia is the base.

This brings us to amphoteric substances, which can act as both an acid and a base. Water (H₂O), bicarbonate ion (HCO₃⁻), and bisulphite ion (HSO₄⁻) are examples. Water acts as a base with HCl, accepting a proton. But it acts as an acid with NH₃, donating a proton.

Now, let's talk about Conjugate Acid-Base Pairs. In the reaction HCl(aq) + NH₃(aq) ⇌ NH₄⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq), HCl donates a proton, acting as an acid, and forms Cl⁻. This Cl⁻ ion can now accept a proton, so it's a base. We call Cl⁻ the conjugate base of HCl.

Similarly, NH₃ accepts a proton, acting as a base, and forms NH₄⁺. This NH₄⁺ ion can now donate a proton, so it's an acid. We call NH₄⁺ the conjugate acid of NH₃. A conjugate acid-base pair consists of two substances that differ by just one proton. Every acid-base reaction involves two such pairs.

The Relative Strengths of Conjugate Acid-Base Pairs is an important aspect. A strong acid, like HCl, has a strong tendency to donate a proton. This means the equilibrium HCl + H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + Cl⁻ lies far to the right. Its conjugate base, Cl⁻, has a very weak tendency to accept a proton, making it a weak base. So, a strong acid has a weak conjugate base.

Conversely, a weak acid like CH₃COOH has a small tendency to donate a proton. The equilibrium CH₃COOH + H₂O ⇌ CH₃COO⁻ + H₃O⁺ lies mostly to the left. Its conjugate base, CH₃COO⁻ (acetate ion), therefore has a strong tendency to accept a proton, making it a strong base. So, a weak acid has a strong conjugate base.

Bronsted-Lowry is more comprehensive than Arrhenius, covering more species and solvents. But it still has Limitations:

(i) It fails to explain the acidic character of non-metallic oxides (CO₂, SO₂, SO₃) and basic character of metallic oxides (Na₂O, MgO, CaO), as these don't involve proton transfer.

(ii) It also fails to explain the acidic character of salts like AlCl₃, FeCl₃, or BF₃, which lack donatable protons.

These limitations bring us to the most general concept: 2.4.3 Lewis Concept of Acids and Bases. Gilbert N. Lewis proposed this in 1923. It's fundamental because it doesn't even require the presence of protons.

According to Lewis, a base is a substance that can donate a pair of electrons, and an acid is a substance that can accept a pair of electrons. So, an acid is an electron pair acceptor, and a base is an electron pair donor.

This definition of a base aligns well with the Bronsted concept. Substances like hydroxide ion, ammonia, and water are Bronsted bases because they accept protons, but they are also Lewis bases because they possess unshared electron pairs available for donation.

A Lewis acid-base reaction involves the sharing of an electron pair from a base by an acid, forming a coordinate covalent bond. For example, in the reaction between BF₃ and NH₃, the nitrogen in ammonia donates its lone pair of electrons to the boron in BF₃.

Lewis acids are diverse and include:

(a) All cations, as they can accept electron pairs. Examples include Ag⁺.

(b) Electron-deficient molecules, where the central atom has fewer than eight valence electrons, like BF₃, AlCl₃, and FeCl₃. Boron in BF₃ only has six electrons around it.

(c) Molecules where the central atom has vacant d-orbitals, allowing them to expand their valency shell beyond an octet, like SiF₄ or SnCl₄.

(d) Molecules where the central atom is bonded to highly electronegative atoms via multiple bonds, like CO₂ or SO₂. When a Lewis base approaches, the pi-electron density shifts away from the less electronegative atom, creating a site for electron acceptance.

The Success of the Lewis Theory is notable:

(i) It's a much broader interpretation of acid-base behavior.

(ii) It's not dependent on the presence of any particular element, like hydrogen.

(iii) It's frequently used to interpret reaction mechanisms in organic chemistry and beyond.

Despite its generality, the Lewis concept also has Limitations:

(i) It's too general, encompassing all reactions that form coordinate bonds, which might dilute its specific utility for "acid-base" definitions.

(ii) It cannot explain the relative strengths of acids and bases quantitatively, unlike the Bronsted-Lowry concept with its conjugate pairs.

So, we've covered three major acid-base theories: Arrhenius, Bronsted-Lowry, and Lewis, each building upon the last to offer a more comprehensive understanding.

Moving on, let's discuss 2.5 Auto Ionization of Water. Friedrich Kohlrausch discovered that even the purest water conducts electricity, albeit very slightly. This indicates the presence of ions in water.

Water is an amphoteric substance, meaning it can act as both an acid and a base. So, one water molecule can transfer a proton to another water molecule. This process, called auto ionization, or autoprotolysis, of water, produces a hydronium ion (H₃O⁺) and a hydroxide ion (OH⁻).

We can write this as 2H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + OH⁻. For simplicity, since H₃O⁺ is just a hydrated H⁺ ion, we often write it as H₂O ⇌ H⁺ + OH⁻. This auto ionization of water is fundamental to understanding acids and bases.

This leads us directly to 2.6 pH Value of Solution. Since water auto-ionizes to a very small extent, we have the equilibrium H₂O ⇌ H⁺ + OH⁻. The equilibrium constant for this is Kc = [H⁺][OH⁻] / [H₂O].

Because water is so poorly ionized, the concentration of undissociated water molecules, [H₂O], is essentially constant. So, we can combine Kc and [H₂O] into a new constant, Kw, which is called the ionic product of water. Kw = [H⁺][OH⁻].

Experimentally, at 25°C, the value of Kw is 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴. So, [H⁺][OH⁻] = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴. In pure water, one H⁺ ion and one OH⁻ ion are produced for every water molecule that dissociates, meaning [H⁺] = [OH⁻].

Using the Kw value, this means [H⁺]² = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴, so [H⁺] = 1.0 × 10⁻⁷ M. Therefore, in pure, neutral water at 25°C, [H⁺] = [OH⁻] = 1.0 × 10⁻⁷ mole per litre. Any solution with these concentrations is neutral.

For an acidic solution, the [H⁺] must be greater than 1.0 × 10⁻⁷ M. For a basic solution, the [OH⁻] must be greater than 1.0 × 10⁻⁷ M, which means [H⁺] must be less than 1.0 × 10⁻⁷ M.

To express these concentrations more conveniently, Sorensen introduced the pH-value in 1909. pH is defined as the negative logarithm of the hydrogen ion concentration on base 10. Mathematically, pH = -log₁₀[H⁺]. This means [H⁺] = 10⁻pH.

Similarly, we have pOH for hydroxyl ion concentration: pOH = -log₁₀[OH⁻].

There's a useful 2.6.1 Relation between pH and pOH. Since Kw = [H⁺][OH⁻], if we take the logarithm of both sides, we get log₁₀Kw = log₁₀[H⁺] + log₁₀[OH⁻]. Then, taking the negative of this, -log₁₀Kw = -log₁₀[H⁺] - log₁₀[OH⁻].

This simplifies to pKw = pH + pOH. Since Kw = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ at 25°C, pKw = 14. So, at 25°C, 14 = pH + pOH. This is a very important relationship.

The 2.6.2 pH-Scale is a numerical scale from 0 to 14.

Solutions with pH between 0 and 2 are strongly acidic.

Between 2 and 4 are moderately acidic.

Between 4 and 7 are weakly acidic.

A pH of 7 is neutral.

Between 7 and 10 are weakly basic.

Between 10 and 12 are moderately basic.

And between 12 and 14 are strongly basic.

So, a lower pH indicates higher acidity, and a higher pH indicates higher basicity.

Let's move on to 2.7 Dissociation Equilibria of Weak Electrolytes. Remember, weak electrolytes, like weak acids and bases, only partially ionize in water, establishing an equilibrium.

First, consider the 1. Dissociation of weak acids. Let's take a weak monobasic acid, HA. In water, it dissociates as HA + H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + A⁻. If we simplify and treat water's concentration as constant, we can write the dissociation constant, Ka, as Ka = [H⁺][A⁻] / [HA].

This constant, Ka, is characteristic of the acid and measures its strength. A higher Ka means a stronger acid. We can also express Ka in terms of the degree of dissociation (α) and total molar concentration (C).

If C is the initial concentration and α is the degree of dissociation, at equilibrium, [HA] = C(1-α), [H⁺] = Cα, and [A⁻] = Cα. Substituting these into the Ka expression gives Ka = (Cα × Cα) / C(1-α) = Cα² / (1-α).

For weak acids, since α is very small, we can often approximate (1-α) as 1. This simplifies to Ka = Cα², or α = √(Ka/C). From this, we can find the hydrogen ion concentration: [H⁺] = Cα = C√(Ka/C) = √(KaC). This shows that [H⁺] is directly proportional to the square root of the acid's dissociation constant.

We can also compare the Relative strength of acids. For two weak acids at the same concentration, the ratio of their strengths (which is proportional to their degrees of dissociation) is equal to the square root of the ratio of their dissociation constants: Strength₁/Strength₂ = √(Ka₁/Ka₂).

Similarly, for 2. Dissociation of weak bases. A weak base BOH dissociates as BOH ⇌ B⁺ + OH⁻. Its dissociation constant, Kb, is given by Kb = [B⁺][OH⁻] / [BOH]. Kb measures the base's strength.

In terms of C and α, Kb = Cα² / (1-α). Again, for weak bases, α is small, so Kb = Cα², or α = √(Kb/C). The hydroxyl ion concentration is then [OH⁻] = Cα = √(KbC).

And like acids, the Relative strength of bases can be compared. For two weak bases at the same concentration, Strength₁/Strength₂ = √(Kb₁/Kb₂).

Now, let's talk about 2.8 Strength of Acids and Bases in Terms of pKa and pKb. Dissociation constants (Ka and Kb) are often very small numbers, expressed as negative powers of 10, which can be inconvenient.

To make them easier to work with, we use pKa and pKb values, similar to pH.

pKa of an acid is defined as the negative logarithm of the dissociation constant of the acid on base 10: pKa = -log₁₀Ka. The smaller the pKa value, the stronger the acid. For example, lactic acid with a pKa of 3.8 is stronger than acetic acid with a pKa of 4.8.

And pKb of a base is the negative logarithm of the dissociation constant of the base on base 10: pKb = -log₁₀Kb. A smaller pKb value indicates a stronger base. For instance, methylamine (pKb 3.3) is stronger than ammonia (pKb 4.7).

That's a good way to simplify and compare acid and base strengths.

Moving on to 2.9 Multistep Ionic Equilibrium, specifically the Ionization of Polyprotic Acids. These are acids that contain two or more ionizable hydrogens, and they always dissociate in stages. Examples include carbonic acid (H₂CO₃), orthophosphoric acid (H₃PO₄), and sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄).

Let's take Example 1: Carbonic acid (H₂CO₃). It has two dissociable hydrogens, so it ionizes in two stages:

At 25°C, Ka₁ is 4.5 × 10⁻⁷, and Ka₂ is 4.7 × 10⁻¹¹. Notice that Ka₁ is significantly larger than Ka₂. This means the secondary dissociation occurs to a much smaller extent.

This trend makes sense because it's harder to remove a proton from a negatively charged ion (HCO₃⁻) than from a neutral molecule (H₂CO₃). Also, the H⁺ ions from the first dissociation suppress the subsequent dissociations due to the common ion effect.

Example 2: Orthophosphoric acid (H₃PO₄) is a tribasic acid, so it dissociates in three stages:

The dissociation constants are Ka₁ = 7.52 × 10⁻³, Ka₂ = 6.23 × 10⁻⁸, and Ka₃ = 4.8 × 10⁻¹³. Again, we see a clear trend: Ka₁ >> Ka₂ >> Ka₃. The primary dissociation is strongest, and each subsequent step is much weaker.

The reasoning is the same: it becomes progressively harder to remove a proton from an increasingly negatively charged ion. As a result, most of the H⁺ ions in the solution come from the primary dissociation, and the common ion effect from the first dissociation suppresses the later stages.

Next, we explore 2.10 Solubility Product. This concept applies to sparingly soluble salts, like BaSO₄ or AgCl, which dissolve only to a very small extent in water. In their saturated solutions, there's an equilibrium between the solid salt, its undissociated molecules, and its ions.

So, you have Solid ⇌ Undissociated electrolyte ⇌ Ions in solution. Let's take silver chloride, AgCl, as an example: AgCl(s) ⇌ AgCl(aq) ⇌ Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq).

Applying the law of chemical equilibrium to the ionic part, K = [Ag⁺][Cl⁻] / [AgCl]. Crucially, the concentration of the undissociated solid salt, [AgCl], is constant at a given temperature, regardless of how much solid is present.

So, we can combine K and this constant [AgCl] into a new constant, Ksp, which is called the solubility product. Ksp = [Ag⁺][Cl⁻]. It's the product of the ionic concentrations in a saturated solution.

In general, for any sparingly soluble salt AxBy that dissociates as AxBy ⇌ xAʸ⁺ + yBˣ⁻, the solubility product constant is Ksp = [Aʸ⁺]ˣ [Bˣ⁻]ʸ. The exponents 'x' and 'y' represent the stoichiometric coefficients of the ions in the dissociation equation.

So, the solubility product of a sparingly soluble salt at a given temperature is defined as the product of the concentrations of its ions in the saturated solution, each concentration term raised to the power equal to its number in the dissociation equation.

Let's look at some examples:

For PbCl₂: Ksp = [Pb²⁺][Cl⁻]²

For Mg(OH)₂: Ksp = [Mg²⁺][OH⁻]²

For Ca₃(PO₄)₂: Ksp = [Ca²⁺]³[PO₄³⁻]²

The solubility product principle states that the value of Ksp for a given salt remains constant at a given temperature, regardless of the source of the ions. This is important when we consider the common ion effect.

For example, if we add a soluble chloride, like potassium chloride (KCl), to a saturated solution of silver chloride (AgCl), the concentration of Cl⁻ ions increases. According to Le Chatelier's principle, to keep the product [Ag⁺][Cl⁻] constant (the Ksp value), the equilibrium AgCl(s) ⇌ Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) shifts to the left.

This means chloride ions combine with silver ions to form solid silver chloride, causing it to precipitate. This is a classic example of the common ion effect, where adding a common ion reduces the solubility of a sparingly soluble salt.

Now, for 2.11 Relationship between Solubility and Solubility Product. While "solubility" applies to all solutes, "solubility product" is specifically for electrolytes.

Let's consider a sparingly soluble binary electrolyte AB, which ionizes as AB ⇌ A⁺ + B⁻. If its solubility is 's' moles per litre, then in a saturated solution, assuming complete dissociation, the concentrations of [A⁺] and [B⁻] ions will both be 's' moles per litre.

So, Ksp = [A⁺][B⁻] = s × s = s². Therefore, the solubility 's' = √Ksp. For a binary electrolyte, the solubility in moles per litre is equal to the square root of its solubility product.

This leads us to the Condition of Precipitation. Ksp is constant for a given electrolyte at a given temperature. However, the "ionic product" – the product of the concentrations of ions at any given moment – can vary.

The ionic product of an electrolyte cannot exceed its Ksp value. So, if the ionic product temporarily becomes greater than the Ksp due to an increase in the concentration of one or both types of ions, the excess ions will combine and precipitate out as a solid salt.

We can classify solutions based on the ionic product:

(i) If ionic product = Ksp, the solution is just saturated, and no precipitation occurs.

(ii) If ionic product > Ksp, the solution is supersaturated, and precipitation will occur.

(iii) If ionic product < Ksp, the solution is unsaturated, and more solute can dissolve.

This is very practical! Let's explore the 2.12 Applications of Solubility Product.

1. Calculation of solubility of sparingly soluble salts: If you know the Ksp at a certain temperature, you can calculate its solubility. For AgCl, s = √Ksp.

2. Predicting the precipitation of a salt: By comparing the calculated ionic product with the known Ksp, we can predict if a salt will precipitate. If ionic product > Ksp, precipitation occurs. If ionic product < Ksp, no precipitation.

3. Precipitation of soluble salts: This concept is used in "salting out," for example, purifying sodium chloride. Passing HCl gas through a saturated NaCl solution drastically increases the Cl⁻ concentration. The ionic product of NaCl then exceeds its Ksp, causing pure NaCl to precipitate out, leaving impurities in solution.

4. In inorganic qualitative analysis: The classification of basic radicals into groups is based on the Ksp values of their salts. For instance, Group I radicals like Hg₂²⁺, Pb²⁺, and Ag⁺ have very low Ksp for their chlorides. Adding dilute HCl precipitates them as chlorides. The common ion effect is often used here to precisely adjust precipitation conditions.

That brings us to a closer look at 2.13 Common Ion Effect. As we mentioned, weak electrolytes ionize only slightly. Adding a strong electrolyte that provides an ion common to the weak electrolyte's dissociation will suppress the weak electrolyte's ionization.

Let's use an example: ammonium hydroxide (NH₄OH), a weak base. Its ionization is NH₄OH ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻. If we add ammonium chloride (NH₄Cl), a strong electrolyte, it dissociates completely into NH₄⁺ and Cl⁻.

The increased concentration of NH₄⁺ ions from NH₄Cl shifts the NH₄OH equilibrium to the left, favoring the unionized NH₄OH. This suppresses the degree of ionization of NH₄OH, significantly reducing the concentration of OH⁻ ions. The weak base becomes even weaker.

So, the suppression of the degree of ionization of a weak electrolyte by adding a strong electrolyte with a common ion is the definition of the common ion effect. This principle, combined with the solubility product, is extremely important in qualitative analysis.

Let's see how it applies to group separations:

* Group II cations (Hg²⁺, Pb²⁺, Bi³⁺, Cu²⁺, Cd²⁺, As³⁺, Sb³⁺, Sn²⁺) are precipitated as sulphides. Their sulphides have very low Ksp values. To precipitate them, we need a low concentration of S²⁻ ions. We achieve this by passing H₂S gas through the solution in the presence of dilute HCl. The H⁺ from HCl suppresses the ionization of H₂S (H₂S ⇌ 2H⁺ + S²⁻) via the common ion effect, keeping [S²⁻] low enough to precipitate only Group II sulphides, not Group IV.

Group IV cations (Ni²⁺, Co²⁺, Mn²⁺, Zn²⁺) are also precipitated as sulphides, but their sulphides have much higher Ksp values. To precipitate them, we need a high concentration of S²⁻ ions. This is achieved by passing H₂S gas through the solution in the presence of NH₄OH.

NH₄OH (NH₄OH ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻) provides OH⁻ ions. These OH⁻ ions react with the H⁺ ions from H₂S (H₂S ⇌ 2H⁺ + S²⁻, and H⁺ + OH⁻ ⇌ H₂O). By removing H⁺ ions, the H₂S equilibrium shifts to the right, increasing the concentration of S²⁻ ions. This higher [S²⁻] allows the ionic products of Group IV sulphides to exceed their Ksp values, causing them to precipitate.

Group III radicals (Fe³⁺, Al³⁺, Cr³⁺) are precipitated as hydroxides by NH₄OH in the presence of NH₄Cl. Here, NH₄Cl suppresses the ionization of NH₄OH (NH₄OH ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻) by the common ion effect, decreasing the [OH⁻] concentration.

The hydroxides of Group III radicals have low Ksp values. So, even with this suppressed [OH⁻], their ionic products exceed Ksp, and they precipitate. If [OH⁻] weren't suppressed, radicals from Groups IV, V, and Mg²⁺ would also precipitate.

Finally, Group V radicals (Ba²⁺, Sr²⁺, Ca²⁺) are precipitated as carbonates by adding (NH₄)₂CO₃ in the presence of NH₄Cl and NH₄OH. NH₄Cl suppresses the ionization of (NH₄)₂CO₃, decreasing the [CO₃²⁻] concentration.

But the Ksp values for Group V carbonates are low enough that even with reduced [CO₃²⁻], their ionic products exceed Ksp, causing them to precipitate. Mg²⁺ and Group VI radicals, with higher Ksp values, remain in solution.

So, the concepts of solubility product and the common ion effect are powerful tools for selective precipitation in qualitative analysis.

Let's shift our focus to 2.14 Buffer Solutions. Generally, adding small amounts of acid or base to a solution changes its pH. But a special kind of solution, called a buffer solution, can resist these pH changes.

A buffer solution contains a weak acid and its conjugate base, or a weak base and its conjugate acid. Its ability to resist pH change is called buffer action.

Buffer solutions are classified by their pH. If the pH is less than 7, it's an acidic buffer, typically a mixture of a weak acid and its salt with a strong base (e.g., acetic acid and sodium acetate). If the pH is greater than 7, it's a basic buffer, a mixture of a weak base and its salt with a strong acid (e.g., NH₄OH and NH₄Cl).

Let's understand the 2.14.1 Mechanism of Buffer Action with an acidic buffer like acetic acid (CH₃COOH) and sodium acetate (CH₃COONa).

CH₃COOH(aq) ⇌ CH₃COO⁻(aq) + H⁺(aq)

CH₃COONa(aq) → CH₃COO⁻(aq) + Na⁺(aq)

This solution has lots of CH₃COO⁻ ions and undissociated CH₃COOH.

If you add a few drops of HCl (H⁺ ions), these H⁺ ions combine with the abundant CH₃COO⁻ ions to form weakly ionized acetic acid molecules: CH₃COO⁻(aq) + H⁺ ⇌ CH₃COOH(aq). Since the added H⁺ ions are consumed, the pH doesn't change significantly.

On the other hand, if you add a few drops of NaOH (OH⁻ ions), these OH⁻ ions combine with the small amount of H⁺ ions present in the buffer to form water: H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) ⇌ H₂O(l). To restore the H⁺ concentration, more acetic acid ionizes. Again, the pH remains relatively stable.

For a basic buffer, like NH₄OH and NH₄Cl:

NH₄OH(aq) ⇌ NH₄⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq)

NH₄Cl(aq) → NH₄⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq)

This solution has lots of NH₄⁺ ions and undissociated NH₄OH.

If HCl (H⁺ ions) is added, the H⁺ ions are neutralized by the OH⁻ ions from the buffer: OH⁻(aq) + H⁺(aq) ⇌ H₂O(l). To restore the OH⁻ concentration, more NH₄OH ionizes.

If NaOH (OH⁻ ions) is added, the additional OH⁻ ions combine with the abundant NH₄⁺ ions to form weakly ionized NH₄OH: NH₄⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) ⇌ NH₄OH(aq). In both cases, the pH change is resisted.

The pH of a buffer solution can be calculated using 2.14.2 Henderson's Equation. For an acidic buffer, pH = pKa + log([Salt]/[Acid]).

If the concentrations of the salt and the acid are equal, then log(1) is 0, so pH = pKa. This means that by measuring the pH of an equimolar acidic buffer, you can determine the pKa of the weak acid. A similar equation exists for basic buffers.

Buffer solutions have many 2.14.3 Applications of Buffer Solutions:

1. In biological systems: Human blood is a natural buffer system (H₂CO₃ / HCO₃⁻) that maintains a pH of 7.4. Excess H⁺ reacts with HCO₃⁻ to form H₂CO₃. Excess OH⁻ reacts with H₂CO₃ to form HCO₃⁻ and H₂O. A pH change of even ±0.5 can be fatal.

2. In industry: They are vital in manufacturing paper, dyes, inks, paints, and drugs, as well as in processes like tanning leather, laundry, and fermentation, where specific pH control is critical.

3. In agriculture: Soil pH needs to be maintained for healthy crop growth, using suitable buffer solutions. Buffers are also used in dairy products, processed foods, and drinks to prevent excessive acidity and for preservation.

4. In chemical analysis: Buffer solutions are routinely used in both qualitative and quantitative analytical procedures.

That's an impressive range of applications! Buffers are truly essential.

Let's move on to 2.15 pH Change in Acid Base Titration and Choice of Indicator. In an acid-base titration, an acid neutralizes a base, or vice-versa, essentially forming water from H⁺ and OH⁻ ions. As the titration proceeds, the pH of the solution changes.

There are different types of titrations:

(a) Strong acid-strong base titration (e.g., HCl and NaOH): The pH starts low, gradually rises, then shows a very sharp increase around the equivalence point, which is at pH 7. The sharp change ranges from about pH 3.3 to 10.7. Indicators like phenolphthalein (effective range pH 8.3-10.0) or methyl orange (effective range pH 3.1-4.4) can be used.

(b) Weak acid-strong base titration (e.g., acetic acid and sodium hydroxide): The pH increases slowly at first. At half-neutralization, the pH equals the pKa of the weak acid (e.g., 4.73 for acetic acid). The equivalence point is shifted towards the alkaline side, typically around pH 8.7, due to salt hydrolysis. The sharp pH change occurs from about 7.7 to 9.7. Phenolphthalein is a suitable indicator here.

(c) Strong acid-weak base titration (e.g., HCl and NH₄OH): The pH progressively increases. The equivalence point is shifted towards the acidic side, typically around pH 5.3, because the salt formed (like ammonium chloride) hydrolyzes to produce H⁺ ions. The sharp pH change occurs from about 3.5 to 7.0. Methyl orange is a suitable indicator for this type of titration.

(d) Weak acid-weak base titration (e.g., acetic acid and ammonium hydroxide): The titration curve for this type of reaction typically does not show any sharp pH change. This makes it very difficult to accurately determine the end point using pH measurements, so indicators are generally not effective.

The choice of indicator is critical. Indicators like phenolphthalein and methyl orange change color over specific pH ranges, which must coincide with the sharp pH jump at the equivalence point of the titration.

Let's move on to 2.16 Salts. Salts are ionic compounds composed of positive ions, which come from a base, and negative ions, which come from an acid. They can taste salty, sour, bitter, sweet, or be tasteless. Their solutions can be acidic, basic, or neutral, and both fused salts and aqueous solutions conduct electricity and undergo electrolysis. The properties of salts in aqueous solutions are essentially the properties of their constituent ions.

Salts are classified into several types:

1. Simple Salts: Formed by acid-base neutralization.

(a) Normal Salts: All replaceable hydrogens of an acid are replaced by metal or ammonium ions. They contain no replaceable hydrogen or hydroxyl groups. Examples: NaCl, KNO₃, (NH₄)₂SO₄.

(b) Acidic Salts: Formed by incomplete neutralization of polybasic acids, retaining one or more replaceable hydrogens. Examples: NaHSO₄, NaHCO₃, NaH₂PO₄. These can be further neutralized by bases.

(c) Basic Salts: Formed by incomplete neutralization of polyacidic bases, retaining one or more hydroxyl groups. Examples: Zn(OH)Cl, Mg(OH)Cl. These can be further neutralized by acids.

2. Double Salts: These are addition compounds formed by combining two simple salts. They are stable only in the solid state and dissociate completely into their constituent ions when dissolved in solution. They give reactions for all ions present in both original simple salts. Examples include ferrous ammonium sulphate (Mohr's salt, FeSO₄(NH₄)₂SO₄·6H₂O) and potash alum (K₂SO₄·Al₂(SO₄)₃·24H₂O).

3. Complex Salts: These salts contain a "complex ion" or "complex molecule" where a central metal ion is surrounded by neutral molecules or negative ions. They are stable in both solid state and in solution, retaining their identity and furnishing complex ions when dissolved. Their properties differ from those of their individual components. Examples: [Cu(NH₃)₄]SO₄, K₃[Fe(CN)₆].

4. Mixed Salts: These salts contain more than one basic radical or more than one acid radical. They dissociate completely in aqueous solutions, giving reactions of all the ions produced. Examples: CaOCl₂ (bleaching powder, containing Ca²⁺, OCl⁻, and Cl⁻), NaKCO₃.

That's a comprehensive overview of salt classification!

Finally, let's delve into 2.17 Hydrolysis. The term "hydrolysis" comes from "hydro" (water) and "lysis" (breaking). It refers to reactions involving water.

We know pure water dissociates slightly into H⁺ and OH⁻ ions, with their concentrations being equal (1.0 × 10⁻⁷ M each), making pure water neutral.

Salts are strong electrolytes and dissociate almost completely in water. But sometimes, the ions from the salt can react with the H⁺ or OH⁻ ions furnished by water.

If the salt's anions react with H⁺ ions from water, the [H⁺] decreases. Since Kw = [H⁺][OH⁻] is constant, the [OH⁻] must increase, making the solution alkaline.

If the salt's cations react with OH⁻ ions from water, the [OH⁻] decreases. This causes [H⁺] to increase, making the solution acidic.

This phenomenon – the interaction of salt anions or cations with water's H⁺ and OH⁻ ions to produce acidic, alkaline, or even neutral solutions – is called salt hydrolysis. It can be seen as the reverse of neutralization, which consumes H⁺ and OH⁻ to form water. Hydrolysis, in contrast, leads to the formation of H⁺ or OH⁻ ions.

To study salt hydrolysis, we categorize salts based on the strength of their parent acid and base:

1. Salts of strong acid and strong base (e.g., NaCl, KNO₃): These salts do not undergo hydrolysis. When dissolved, their ions (like Na⁺ and Cl⁻) have no tendency to react with H⁺ or OH⁻ from water because the possible products (NaOH and HCl) are themselves strong and completely dissociated. So, the H⁺ and OH⁻ concentrations remain unchanged, and the solution stays neutral.

2. Salts of weak acid and strong base (e.g., CH₃COONa, KCN): These undergo anionic hydrolysis. When CH₃COONa dissolves, it gives CH₃COO⁻ and Na⁺. The acetate ion (CH₃COO⁻) reacts with H⁺ from water to form weakly dissociated acetic acid: CH₃COO⁻ + H⁺ ⇌ CH₃COOH. This consumes H⁺, so water dissociates further to restore Kw, increasing [OH⁻]. The solution becomes alkaline.

3. Salts of strong acid and weak base (e.g., NH₄Cl, FeCl₃): These undergo cationic hydrolysis. When NH₄Cl dissolves, it gives NH₄⁺ and Cl⁻. The ammonium ion (NH₄⁺) interacts with OH⁻ from water to form feebly ionized NH₄OH: NH₄⁺ + OH⁻ ⇌ NH₄OH. This consumes OH⁻, causing water to dissociate further, increasing [H⁺]. The solution becomes acidic.

4. Salts of weak acid and weak base (e.g., CH₃COONH₄, (NH₄)₂CO₃): In this case, both the cation and the anion hydrolyze. For CH₃COONH₄, CH₃COO⁻ reacts with H⁺, and NH₄⁺ reacts with OH⁻. Both H⁺ and OH⁻ ions are removed. The resulting solution's pH depends on the relative strengths of the weak acid (CH₃COOH) and weak base (NH₄OH) formed. If they are removed in equivalent amounts, the solution remains neutral, even though hydrolysis has occurred.
