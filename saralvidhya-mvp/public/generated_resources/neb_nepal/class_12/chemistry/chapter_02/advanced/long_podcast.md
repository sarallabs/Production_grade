Exactly. And within these solutions, there's this fascinating dynamic equilibrium happening between the ions and the undissociated molecules of the substance. This is what we call ionic equilibrium.

Let's break down the key postulates of the Arrhenius Theory of Ionization, proposed back in 1887 by Svante Arrhenius.

The first major point is that when an electrolyte, like an acid, base, or salt, dissolves in a polar solvent like water, it splits into electrically charged particles called ions. Positive ones are cations, and negative ones are anions.

The general equation for this is AB dissociating into A⁺ and B⁻.

He also proposed that even in solid form, electrolytes are made of ions held together by electrostatic forces. Dissolving them simply breaks these forces, allowing the ions to separate. So, A⁺B⁻ in solid form becomes A⁺ and B⁻ in solution.

And here’s a key concept: in solution, these ions don't just stay separated. They can recombine to form unionized molecules. This creates a dynamic equilibrium between the ions and the undissociated molecules.

This dynamic equilibrium is described by an ionization constant. For our AB example, the equilibrium is AB ⇌ A⁺ + B⁻. The ionization constant, K, would be [A⁺][B⁻] divided by [AB].

An important consequence of this is electrical neutrality. In any solution, the total positive charge must always equal the total negative charge.

And, of course, the movement of these ions is what allows the electric current to flow. So, only substances that ionize in solution can conduct electricity.

The properties of an electrolyte solution are entirely due to these ions. And interestingly, each ion acts osmotically as if it were a single molecule, affecting colligative properties in a specific way.

A crucial concept here is the degree of ionization, often denoted by 'alpha' (α). It's the fraction of the total molecules that have ionized into free ions in the solution.

So, α is calculated as the number of molecules ionized into ions divided by the total number of molecules dissolved.

Now, what influences this degree of ionization? Several factors come into play. First, the nature of the electrolyte itself. Strong electrolytes, like strong acids and bases, ionize almost completely, while weak ones ionize only slightly.

Then there's the nature of the solvent. The solvent's role is to weaken the forces holding the ions together. This is measured by its dielectric constant. A higher dielectric constant means a greater capacity to weaken those forces.

Concentration is another big factor. Ionization is inversely proportional to concentration. Diluting a solution generally increases the degree of ionization because there are more solvent molecules available to interact with the solute molecules.

And finally, temperature. Higher temperatures usually lead to greater ionization because the molecules move faster, reducing the interionic forces of attraction.

Building on Arrhenius's idea of dynamic equilibrium, Ostwald developed the Dilution Law in 1888. It's essentially the application of the law of mass action to ionic equilibrium.

Let's imagine one gram molecule of a binary electrolyte, AB, dissolved in V litres of water, with a degree of dissociation 'α'. Initially, we have 1 mole of AB and no ions.

At equilibrium, we'll have (1 - α) moles of AB, and α moles each of A⁺ and B⁻ ions. The active masses, or concentrations, at equilibrium are then (1 - α)/V for AB, and α/V for A⁺ and B⁻.

Applying the law of mass action, the rate of ionization is proportional to (1 - α)/V, and the rate of combination is proportional to (α/V) * (α/V), or α²/V².

At equilibrium, these rates are equal. So, k₁ (1 - α)/V = k₂ α²/V², where k₁ and k₂ are velocity constants.

Rearranging this, we get the ionization constant, K<0xE1><0xB5><0x84>, which is k₁/k₂. This gives us K<0xE1><0xB5><0x84> = α² / [(1 - α)V]. This is Ostwald's Dilution Law.

Now, for weak electrolytes, 'α' is very small compared to 1. So, we can approximate (1 - α) as 1. The equation simplifies to K<0xE1><0xB5><0x84> = α²/V, or α = √(K<0xE1><0xB5><0x84> * V). This means the degree of dissociation is proportional to the square root of dilution.

For strong electrolytes, 'α' is large and cannot be neglected. We have to use the original equation, K<0xE1><0xB5><0x84> = α² / [(1 - α)V], which leads to a quadratic equation for 'α'.

Ostwald's Dilution Law can be experimentally verified by determining 'α' at different dilutions and checking if K<0xE1><0xB5><0x84> remains constant. The degree of dissociation 'α' is often found using conductivity measurements, specifically by comparing the equivalent conductivity at a given dilution (Λv) with the equivalent conductivity at infinite dilution (Λ∞), where α = Λv / Λ∞.

However, this law has limitations. It works well for weak electrolytes but fails completely for strong electrolytes. For strong electrolytes, 'α' is close to 1 at all dilutions, and the law doesn't hold true in practice.

Let's move on to the fundamental concepts of acids and bases. The terms themselves have interesting origins – 'acid' from the Latin 'acidus' meaning sour, and 'alkali' from the Arabic 'al-quili' meaning ashes of a plant.

Early generalizations were made, like Lavoisier's idea that acids contain oxygen, which Davy disproved with hydrochloric acid. But the most widely accepted early generalization came from Arrhenius in 1887.

The Arrhenius concept defines an acid as a substance that ionizes in aqueous solution to produce hydrogen ions (H⁺), and a base as a substance that produces hydroxyl ions (OH⁻).

So, HCl in water gives H⁺ and Cl⁻. NaOH in water gives Na⁺ and OH⁻. An acid-base reaction, according to Arrhenius, is the union of H⁺ and OH⁻ to form water: H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l).

The strength of an acid or base depends on its degree of ionization. Strong acids like HCl ionize almost completely, while weak acids like acetic acid ionize only partially. The same applies to bases.

However, the Arrhenius concept has limitations. It's strictly for aqueous solutions and can't explain acid-base behavior in non-aqueous media. It also fails to explain the acidic nature of oxides like CO₂ or the basic nature of oxides like CaO.

Furthermore, the idea of a bare proton (H⁺) in aqueous solution is questionable due to its high hydration energy. In reality, it's hydrated, often represented as H₃O⁺, the hydronium ion.

To overcome these limitations, the Bronsted-Lowry concept was proposed independently by Brønsted and Lowry in 1923. This is a more general definition applicable to both aqueous and non-aqueous solutions.

According to Bronsted-Lowry, an acid is a proton-donor, and a base is a proton-accepter. An acid-base reaction is simply the transfer of a proton from an acid to a base.

This concept highlights that acids and bases are interdependent. A substance can only act as an acid if there's a base to accept its proton, and vice-versa.

Some substances, like water, can act as both an acid and a base. These are called amphoteric substances. For example, water can donate a proton to HCl, acting as a base, or accept a proton from NH₃, acting as an acid.

A crucial part of this concept is conjugate acid-base pairs. When an acid donates a proton, it forms its conjugate base. When a base accepts a proton, it forms its conjugate acid.

For instance, in HCl + NH₃ ⇌ NH₄⁺ + Cl⁻, Cl⁻ is the conjugate base of HCl, and NH₄⁺ is the conjugate acid of NH₃.

The strengths of conjugate pairs are inversely related. A strong acid has a weak conjugate base, and a weak acid has a strong conjugate base.

The Bronsted-Lowry concept is more comprehensive but still has limitations. It can't explain the acidity of certain non-metallic oxides like CO₂ or the basicity of metallic oxides like Na₂O.

This brings us to the Lewis concept, proposed by Gilbert N. Lewis in 1923. This is the most general concept. A Lewis base is an electron pair donor, and a Lewis acid is an electron pair acceptor.

So, a Lewis base has an available pair of electrons to share, like ammonia or hydroxide ions. A Lewis acid has an empty orbital to accept that electron pair.

Examples of Lewis acids include cations like Ag⁺, electron-deficient molecules like BF₃, and even molecules with vacant d-orbitals.

The Lewis concept is broader, not dependent on specific elements, and is very useful for understanding reaction mechanisms. However, it's so general that it can include reactions not traditionally considered acid-base reactions, and it doesn't easily explain relative acid-base strengths.

Let's touch upon the autoionization of water. Even pure water conducts electricity very slightly, indicating the presence of ions. This happens because one water molecule can donate a proton to another, forming a hydronium ion (H₃O⁺) and a hydroxide ion (OH⁻).

The overall reaction is 2H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + OH⁻. For simplicity, we often write this as H₂O ⇌ H⁺ + OH⁻. This autoionization is fundamental to understanding pH.

The pH of a solution is a measure of its hydrogen ion concentration. It's defined as the negative logarithm (base 10) of the hydrogen ion concentration: pH = -log₁₀[H⁺].

In pure water at 25°C, the concentration of H⁺ and OH⁻ ions is equal, 1.0 x 10⁻⁷ moles per litre. This gives a neutral pH of 7.

The product of these concentrations is the ionic product of water, Kw = [H⁺][OH⁻], which is 1.0 x 10⁻¹⁴ at 25°C.

For acidic solutions, [H⁺] > 10⁻⁷, so pH < 7. For basic solutions, [OH⁻] > 10⁻⁷, meaning [H⁺] < 10⁻⁷, and pH > 7.

We also have pOH, defined as pOH = -log₁₀[OH⁻]. And there's a direct relationship: pH + pOH = pKw. At 25°C, this means pH + pOH = 14.

The p-scale generally ranges from 0 to 14. Solutions with pH 0-2 are strongly acidic, 2-4 moderately acidic, 4-7 weakly acidic. Conversely, 7-10 are weakly basic, 10-12 moderately basic, and 12-14 strongly basic.

Moving on to dissociation equilibria of weak electrolytes. Weak acids and bases only partially ionize, establishing an equilibrium. For a weak acid HA, the dissociation constant Ka is given by Ka = [H⁺][A⁻] / [HA].

Ka is a measure of the acid's strength. A higher Ka means a stronger acid. If the initial concentration of the acid is C and its degree of dissociation is α, then Ka = Cα² / (1 - α). For very weak acids, this simplifies to Ka ≈ Cα², making α ≈ √(Ka/C).

Similarly, for a weak base BOH, the dissociation constant is Kb = [B⁺][OH⁻] / [BOH]. Kb is a measure of the base's strength. It can also be expressed as Kb ≈ Cα², leading to α ≈ √(Kb/C).

The relative strengths of acids or bases are often compared using their dissociation constants. If we have two acids with concentrations C, their strengths are proportional to the square root of their Ka values: Strength₁/Strength₂ = √(Ka₁/Ka₂).

To make handling these small numbers easier, we use pKa and pKb values. pKa = -log₁₀Ka, and pKb = -log₁₀Kb. A smaller pKa indicates a stronger acid, and a smaller pKb indicates a stronger base.

Polyprotic acids, like H₂CO₃ or H₃PO₄, have more than one ionizable hydrogen and dissociate in multiple stages, each with its own dissociation constant (Ka₁, Ka₂, etc.).

The subsequent dissociation constants are always smaller than the preceding ones. For example, Ka₁ >> Ka₂ >> Ka₃. This is because it's harder to remove a proton from an already negatively charged ion.

This leads us to solubility product. For sparingly soluble salts like AgCl, there's an equilibrium between the solid salt and its ions in a saturated solution.

The solubility product, Ksp, is the product of the concentrations of the ions in a saturated solution, each raised to the power of its stoichiometric coefficient. For AxBy, Ksp = [Aʸ⁺]ˣ [Bˣ⁻]ʸ.

The solubility product principle states that for a given salt at a given temperature, Ksp is constant. If the ionic product in a solution exceeds Ksp, precipitation occurs. If it's less than Ksp, more solute can dissolve. If it equals Ksp, the solution is saturated.

The relationship between solubility (s) and solubility product is direct. For a binary salt AB, Ksp = s². For AxBy, Ksp = xˣyʸs⁽ˣ⁺ʸ⁾.

Applications of solubility product are numerous: calculating solubility, predicting precipitation, and even in qualitative analysis for separating ions.

The common ion effect is crucial here. It's the suppression of the ionization of a weak electrolyte when a strong electrolyte containing a common ion is added. For example, adding NH₄Cl to NH₄OH reduces the concentration of OH⁻ ions.

This effect is vital in qualitative analysis. For instance, it helps control the concentration of S²⁻ ions when precipitating sulfides of different groups.

Buffer solutions are special. They resist changes in pH upon addition of small amounts of acid or base. They typically consist of a weak acid and its conjugate base, or a weak base and its conjugate acid.

The mechanism involves the buffer components reacting with added H⁺ or OH⁻ ions, maintaining a relatively stable pH. For an acidic buffer like acetic acid and sodium acetate, added H⁺ reacts with acetate ions to form acetic acid, and added OH⁻ reacts with H⁺ ions, which are then replenished by the weak acid's dissociation.

Henderson's equation, pH = pKa + log([Salt]/[Acid]), is key for calculating buffer pH. If the concentrations of the salt and acid are equal, pH = pKa.

Buffer solutions are essential in biological systems, like blood, maintaining a stable pH for vital functions. They are also widely used in industry, agriculture, and chemical analysis.

pH changes during acid-base titrations are also important. Titration curves show how pH changes as one reactant is added. The steep change around the equivalence point helps select the appropriate indicator.

For strong acid-strong base titrations, the equivalence point is at pH 7. For weak acid-strong base, it's above 7, and for strong acid-weak base, it's below 7. Weak acid-weak base titrations have no sharp pH change, making indicator selection difficult.

Salts are ionic compounds formed from acids and bases. They can be classified as simple salts (normal, acidic, basic), double salts, complex salts, and mixed salts, each with distinct properties.

Hydrolysis is the reaction of ions of a salt with water, altering the pH. Salts of strong acid-strong base don't hydrolyze. Salts of weak acid-strong base give alkaline solutions due to anion hydrolysis. Salts of strong acid-weak base give acidic solutions due to cation hydrolysis. Salts of weak acid-weak base can be acidic, basic, or neutral depending on the relative strengths of the acid and base.
