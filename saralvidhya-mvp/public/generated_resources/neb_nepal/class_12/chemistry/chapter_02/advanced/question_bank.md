Ionic equilibrium governs reactions in aqueous solutions, crucial for analysis and industrial processes. Electrolytic solutions conduct electricity due to the presence of ions. The ionization of electrolytes is not an absolute phenomenon but exists as a dynamic equilibrium.     *   **Arrhenius Theory Postulates:**
        1.  **Dissociation into Ions:** Acids, bases, and salts, when dissolved in water (or polar solvents), dissociate into electrically charged particles called ions. Positive ions are cations, and negative ions are anions. (e.g., $AB \rightarrow A^+ + B^-$).
        2.  **Ionic Nature of Solids:** Even in the solid state, electrolytes consist of ions held by electrostatic forces. Dissolution breaks these forces, separating the ions. (e.g., $A^+B^- \rightarrow A^+ + B^-$).
        3.  **Dynamic Equilibrium:** In solution, a dynamic equilibrium exists between the ions and the undissociated molecules of the electrolyte. This equilibrium is characterized by an ionization constant ($K$).
        4.  **Electrical Neutrality:** The total positive charge in an electrolytic solution is always equal to the total negative charge, making the solution electrically neutral.
        5.  **Conduction:** The movement of ions towards oppositely charged electrodes (electrolysis) is responsible for the flow of electric current through the solution. Only substances that ionize conduct electricity.
        6.  **Properties due to Ions:** The properties of an electrolytic solution are attributed to the ions present in it.
        7.  **Degree of Ionization ($\alpha$):** The fraction of the total number of dissolved molecules that exist as free ions is called the degree of ionization ($\alpha$).
    *   **Limitations of Arrhenius Theory:**
        *   **Solvent Specificity:** It is primarily applicable to aqueous solutions, though it acknowledges other polar solvents. It doesn't fully explain ionization in non-polar solvents.
        *   **Incomplete Dissociation:** It suggested that all electrolytes dissociate, but didn't adequately explain why some are strong (nearly complete dissociation) and others are weak (partial dissociation).
        *   **Strong Electrolytes:** The theory struggles to explain the behavior of strong electrolytes at higher concentrations, where deviations from ideal behavior are significant due to interionic attractions.
        *   **Bronsted-Lowry and Lewis Acids/Bases:** It defines acids and bases solely based on their ability to produce $H^+$ and $OH^-$ ions in water, failing to explain acid-base reactions in non-aqueous media or reactions that don't involve proton transfer (like Lewis acid-base reactions).

        *   **Application of Law of Mass Action:** Ostwald extended Arrhenius theory by applying the law of mass action to the dynamic equilibrium of weak electrolytes.             $K \approx \frac{1}{V}\alpha^2 \implies \alpha^2 \approx KV \implies \alpha \approx \sqrt{KV}$.

            Given: $C_2 = 0.0025$ M.
            Using the quadratic equation $C_2\alpha_2^2 + K\alpha_2 - K = 0$:
            $0.0025 \alpha_2^2 + (4.08 \times 10^{-5}) \alpha_2 - (4.08 \times 10^{-5}) = 0$.

            $\alpha_2 = \frac{-K + \sqrt{K^2 + 4C_2K}}{2C_2}$
            $\alpha_2 = \frac{-(4.08 \times 10^{-5}) + \sqrt{(4.08 \times 10^{-5})^2 + 4(0.0025)(4.08 \times 10^{-5})}}{2(0.0025)}$
            $\alpha_2 = \frac{-4.08 \times 10^{-5} + \sqrt{1.66 \times 10^{-9} + 4.08 \times 10^{-7}}}{0.005}$
            $\alpha_2 = \frac{-4.08 \times 10^{-5} + \sqrt{0.00000000166 + 0.000000408}}{0.005}$

            $\alpha_2 \approx \sqrt{KV} = \sqrt{K/C_2} = \sqrt{4.08 \times 10^{-5} / 0.0025} = \sqrt{0.01632} \approx 0.1277$.

4.  **Detailed Answer:**
    (a) **Derivation of Ionization Constant:**
        For the equilibrium $AB \rightleftharpoons A^+ + B^-$:
        Let the initial molar concentration of $AB$ be $C$.
        Let the degree of ionization be $\alpha$.
        At equilibrium:
        Concentration of undissociated $AB = [AB] = C(1-\alpha)$.
        Concentration of $A^+$ ions $= [A^+] = C\alpha$.
        Concentration of $B^-$ ions $= [B^-] = C\alpha$.
        Applying the law of mass action (law of chemical equilibrium):
        The ionization constant $K$ is given by:
        $K = \frac{\text{Product of concentrations of ions}}{\text{Concentration of undissociated molecule}}$
        $K = \frac{[A^+][B^-]}{[AB]}$
        Substituting the equilibrium concentrations:
        $K = \frac{(C\alpha)(C\alpha)}{C(1-\alpha)} = $\frac{C^2\alpha^2}{C(1-\alpha)} = $\frac{C\alpha^2}{1-\alpha}$.

    (b) **Effect of Common Ion:**
        The equilibrium is $AB \rightleftharpoons A^+ + B^-$.
        If a salt containing a common ion, such as $NaA$ (which provides $A^+$ ions), is added to the solution, the concentration of $A^+$ ions will increase.
        According to **Le Chatelier's principle**, the system will try to counteract this increase. To reduce the concentration of $A^+$ ions, the equilibrium will shift to the **left**. This means that $A^+$ ions and $B^-$ ions will recombine to form more undissociated $AB$ molecules.
        **Consequences:**
        *   The concentration of $AB$ will increase.
        *   The concentrations of $A^+$ and $B^-$ will decrease (relative to what they would have been without the added salt).
        *   The **degree of ionization ($\alpha$) of $AB$ will decrease**. This phenomenon is known as the **common ion effect**.

    (c) **Quantifying the Effect:**
        *   **Initial State (without common ion):**
            Given: $K = 10^{-5}$, Initial $[AB] = C = 0.1$ M.
            Since $\alpha$ is likely small for a $K$ value of $10^{-5}$, we can use the approximation $K \approx C\alpha^2$.
            $10^{-5} = 0.1 \times \alpha^2$
            $\alpha^2 = $\frac{10^{-5}}{0.1} = 10^{-4}$
            $\alpha = \sqrt{10^{-4}} = 0.01$ or $1\%$.
            (Let's check the exact formula: $K = \frac{C\alpha^2}{1-\alpha}$. If $\alpha=0.01$, $K = \frac{0.1 \times (0.01)^2}{1-0.01} = $\frac{0.1 \times 10^{-4}}{0.99} \approx 1.01 \times 10^{-5}$. The approximation is good).
            So, initial degree of ionization $\alpha = 0.01$.

        *   **With Common Ion:**
            Added $0.05$ M of $NaA$, so the concentration of $A^+$ from $NaA$ is $0.05$ M.
            The total concentration of $A^+$ at equilibrium will be $[A^+]_{total} = [A^+]_{from AB} + [A^+]_{from NaA}$.
            Let the new degree of ionization of $AB$ be $\alpha'$.
            At equilibrium:
            $[AB] = 0.1(1-\alpha')$
            $[A^+]_{from AB} = 0.1\alpha'$
            $[B^-] = 0.1\alpha'$
            Total $[A^+]_{total} = 0.1\alpha' + 0.05$.
            The ionization constant remains the same: $K = 10^{-5}$.
            Using the exact formula: $K = \frac{[A^+]_{total}[B^-]}{[AB]}$.
            $10^{-5} = $\frac{(0.1\alpha' + 0.05)(0.1\alpha')}{0.1(1-\alpha')}$.
            $10^{-5} = $\frac{(0.1\alpha' + 0.05)(0.1\alpha')}{0.1 - 0.1\alpha'}$.
            $10^{-5}(0.1 - 0.1\alpha') = 0.01(\alpha')^2 + 0.005\alpha'$.
            $10^{-6} - 10^{-6}\alpha' = 0.01(\alpha')^2 + 0.005\alpha'$.
            $0.01(\alpha')^2 + (0.005 + 10^{-6})\alpha' - 10^{-6} = 0$.
            $0.01(\alpha')^2 + 0.005001\alpha' - 10^{-6} = 0$.
            This is a quadratic equation for $\alpha'$.
            Using the quadratic formula: $\alpha' = $\frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$
            $a = 0.01$, $b = 0.005001$, $c = -10^{-6}$.
            $b^2 = (0.005001)^2 \approx 2.501 \times 10^{-5}$.
            $4ac = 4(0.01)(-10^{-6}) = -4 \times 10^{-8}$.
            $\sqrt{b^2 - 4ac} = \sqrt{2.501 \times 10^{-5} - (-4 \times 10^{-8})} = \sqrt{2.501 \times 10^{-5} + 0.004 \times 10^{-5}} = \sqrt{2.505 \times 10^{-5}} \approx 0.005005$.
            $\alpha' = $\frac{-0.005001 + 0.005005}{2(0.01)} = $\frac{0.000004}{0.02} = 0.0002$.
            The new degree of ionization $\alpha' = 0.0002$ or $0.02\%$.
            This confirms a significant decrease in the degree of ionization due to the common ion.

5.  **Detailed Answer:**
    (a) **Relationship between $K_a$, $\alpha$, and $C$:**
        For a weak monoprotic acid $HA$, the dissociation is represented by the equilibrium: $HA \rightleftharpoons H^+ + A^-$.
        The ionization constant $K_a$ is given by: $K_a = \frac{[H^+][A^-]}{[HA]}$.
        If the initial molar concentration of the acid is $C$, and its degree of ionization is $\alpha$, then at equilibrium:
        $[HA] = C(1-\alpha)$
        $[H^+] = C\alpha$
        $[A^-] = C\alpha$
        Substituting these into the $K_a$ expression:
        $K_a = \frac{(C\alpha)(C\alpha)}{C(1-\alpha)} = $\frac{C\alpha^2}{1-\alpha}$.
        This equation shows the relationship between $K_a$, $C$, and $\alpha$.
        For weak electrolytes where $\alpha \ll 1$, the equation simplifies to $K_a \approx C\alpha^2$, or $\alpha \approx \sqrt{K_a/C}$. This shows that $\alpha$ is inversely proportional to the square root of concentration (or directly proportional to the square root of dilution).

    (b) **Effect of Dielectric Constant:**
        The dielectric constant of a solvent is a measure of its ability to reduce the electrostatic force of attraction between ions. For an electrolyte $AB \rightleftharpoons A^+ + B^-$, the ions $A^+$ and $B^-$ are held together by electrostatic forces. A solvent with a high dielectric constant (like water, $\epsilon_r \approx 80$) can effectively screen the positive charge of the cation and the negative charge of the anion, significantly weakening the Coulombic attraction between them. This reduced attraction makes it easier for the ions to separate and exist independently in the solution, thus increasing the degree of ionization. Conversely, a solvent with a low dielectric constant (like hexane, $\epsilon_r \approx 2$) offers little shielding, resulting in strong interionic attractions and a low degree of ionization.

    (c) **Percentage of Acid Remaining Unionized:**
        Given: $K_a = 2.5 \times 10^{-5}$, Initial concentration $[HX]_0 = C = 0.02$ M.
        We need to find the percentage of $HX$ that remains unionized. This corresponds to $(1-\alpha) \times 100\%$.
        First, we calculate $\alpha$. We use the exact equation: $K_a = \frac{C\alpha^2}{1-\alpha}$.
        $2.5 \times 10^{-5} = $\frac{0.02 \alpha^2}{1-\alpha}$
        $2.5 \times 10^{-5} (1-\alpha) = 0.02 \alpha^2$
        $2.5 \times 10^{-5} - 2.5 \times 10^{-5} \alpha = 0.02 \alpha^2$
        Rearranging into a quadratic equation: $0.02 \alpha^2 + 2.5 \times 10^{-5} \alpha - 2.5 \times 10^{-5} = 0$.
        Using the quadratic formula $\alpha = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$, where $a=0.02$, $b=2.5 \times 10^{-5}$, $c=-2.5 \times 10^{-5}$.
        $b^2 = (2.5 \times 10^{-5})^2 = 6.25 \times 10^{-10}$.
        $4ac = 4(0.02)(-2.5 \times 10^{-5}) = -0.08 \times 2.5 \times 10^{-5} = -0.2 \times 10^{-5} = -2 \times 10^{-6}$.
        $\sqrt{b^2 - 4ac} = \sqrt{6.25 \times 10^{-10} - (-2 \times 10^{-6})} = \sqrt{6.25 \times 10^{-10} + 20000 \times 10^{-10}} = \sqrt{20000.625 \times 10^{-10}} \approx \sqrt{2.0000625 \times 10^{-6}} \approx 1.414 \times 10^{-3}$.
        *Correction in calculation:* $\sqrt{b^2-4ac} = \sqrt{6.25 \times 10^{-10} + 2 \times 10^{-6}} = \sqrt{0.000000000625 + 0.000002} = \sqrt{0.000002000625} \approx 0.001414$.
        $\alpha = \frac{-(2.5 \times 10^{-5}) \pm 0.001414}{2(0.02)} = $\frac{-0.000025 \pm 0.001414}{0.04}$.
        Since $\alpha$ must be positive:
        $\alpha = \frac{0.001389}{0.04} \approx 0.0347$.
        The degree of ionization $\alpha \approx 0.0347$.
        The percentage of acid that ionized is $0.0347 \times 100\% = 3.47\%$.
        The percentage of acid remaining unionized is $100\% - 3.47\% = \mathbf{96.53\%}$.

    *   **Reasoning:**
        *   **Conductivity and Ion Concentration:** The electrical conductivity of a solution is directly proportional to the concentration of ions present. More ions mean better conductivity.
        *   **Weak Electrolyte Behavior:** For a weak electrolyte like acetic acid ($CH_3COOH \rightleftharpoons H^+ + CH_3COO^-$), at higher concentrations, a smaller fraction of molecules is ionized. As the solution is diluted (concentration decreases), Ostwald's dilution law states that the degree of ionization ($\alpha$) increases.
        *   **Effect on Conductivity:** Although the overall concentration of the electrolyte decreases upon dilution, the *increase* in the degree of ionization means that the *concentration of ions* might not decrease proportionally, or in some cases, could even increase initially. However, for weak electrolytes, the decrease in molar concentration often dominates the overall conductivity change.
        *   *Re-evaluation for demonstration:* The *conductivity* itself will generally decrease with dilution because the number of charge carriers per unit volume decreases. However, the *degree of ionization* increases. To demonstrate the *increase in degree of ionization*, we need to relate conductivity to ion concentration.
        *   **Revised Reasoning for Demonstration:** The conductivity reading ($K_{cond}$) is roughly proportional to the total ion concentration ($[ions]$). For a weak electrolyte, $[ions] \approx 2 \times C \times \alpha$ (for a binary electrolyte). From Ostwald's law, $\alpha \approx \sqrt{K/C}$. So, $[ions] \approx 2 \times C \times \sqrt{K/C} = 2\sqrt{KC}$.
        *   This implies that the ion concentration might increase with dilution if the $\sqrt{C}$ term decreases faster than $C$ increases. This is where the interpretation can be tricky.
        *   **Simpler Demonstration:** The simplest way to demonstrate is to compare conductivities. If the conductivity *decreases* with dilution, it's because the molar concentration ($C$) decreases more significantly than the degree of ionization ($\alpha$) increases. However, the *ratio* of conductivity to concentration (or conductivity divided by C) would be expected to increase with dilution, indicating a higher proportion of ions.
        *   **Best Qualitative Demonstration:** Measure the resistance (reciprocal of conductivity) of solutions of increasing dilution. Resistance will increase. Then, relate this to the fact that even though resistance increases, the degree of ionization *increases* because the *per-molecule* ion production increases.

The 0.1 M solution will have the highest conductivity. Then, divide the conductivity by the molar concentration. This ratio should increase with dilution, showing a higher degree of ionization.

            1. Prepare solutions of a weak acid (e.g., acetic acid) at different concentrations that represent different dilutions (e.g., 0.1 M, 0.05 M, 0.025 M).

        *   **Reasoning:** This observed trend ($S_{dilute} > S_{concentrated}$) directly demonstrates that the degree of ionization increases with dilution, as the amount of conductivity contributed per mole of acid is higher in more dilute solutions.

2.  **Real-World Application & Calculation (Buffer System):**
    *   **Buffer System:** Weak acid $HA$ and its conjugate base $A^-$.
    *   **Given:** $K_a = 10^{-6}$, $[HA]_0 = 0.05$ M, $[A^-]_0 = 0.1$ M.
    *   **Calculations:**
        *   We can use the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation for buffers: $pH = pK_a + \log \frac{[A^-]}{[HA]}$.
        *   First, calculate $pK_a$: $pK_a = -\log(K_a) = -\log(10^{-6}) = 6$.
        *   At equilibrium, for a buffer, the concentrations of $HA$ and $A^-$ are assumed to be close to their initial concentrations if the buffer is effective and the added acid/base is not too strong or in large quantity.
        *   So, $[HA] \approx [HA]_0 = 0.05$ M and $[A^-] \approx [A^-]_0 = 0.1$ M.
        *   $pH = 6 + \log \frac{0.1}{0.05} = 6 + \log 2 = 6 + 0.301 \approx 6.301$.
        *   Now, calculate $[H^+]$ and $[OH^-]$:
            $[H^+] = 10^{-pH} = 10^{-6.301} = 10^{0.699} \times 10^{-7} \approx 5.0 \times 10^{-7}$ M.
            $[OH^-] = K_w / [H^+] = 10^{-14} / (5.0 \times 10^{-7}) = 2.0 \times 10^{-8}$ M.
    *   **Response to Addition of Strong Acid:**
        *   If a strong acid (e.g., $HCl$) is added, the $H^+$ ions from $HCl$ will react with the conjugate base $A^-$ present in the buffer: $A^- + H^+ \rightarrow HA$.
        *   This reaction consumes $H^+$ ions and converts $A^-$ into $HA$.
        *   The ratio $[A^-]/[HA]$ decreases, which according to the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation, causes a slight decrease in pH. However, because the buffer components are present in significant concentrations, the change in pH is much smaller than if the acid were added to pure water.
    *   **Response to Addition of Strong Base:**
        *   If a strong base (e.g., $NaOH$) is added, the $OH^-$ ions from $NaOH$ will react with the weak acid $HA$ present in the buffer: $HA + OH^- \rightarrow A^- + H_2O$.
        *   This reaction consumes $OH^-$ ions and converts $HA$ into $A^-$.
        *   The ratio $[A^-]/[HA]$ increases, which causes a slight increase in pH. Again, the change in pH is minimized due to the buffer action.

3.  **Thermodynamic Interpretation:**
    *   **Ionization of Water and Temperature:** The ionization of water is $H_2O \rightleftharpoons H^+ + OH^-$. The equilibrium constant for this reaction is $K_w$. The value of $K_w$ is known to increase with temperature. For example, at $25^\circ C$, $K_w = 1.0 \times 10^{-14}$, while at $50^\circ C$, $K_w \approx 5.47 \times 10^{-14}$.
    *   **Thermodynamic Explanation:** According to Le Chatelier's principle, if an increase in temperature shifts an equilibrium to the right (favoring products), the forward reaction must be endothermic ($\Delta H > 0$). Conversely, if it shifts to the left (favoring reactants), the reaction is exothermic ($\Delta H < 0$). Since $K_w$ increases with temperature, the equilibrium shifts towards the formation of $H^+$ and $OH^-$ ions. Therefore, the ionization of water is an **endothermic process** ($\Delta H > 0$). Increasing temperature provides the energy required to break the hydrogen bonds in water and form ions.
    *   **Implication for pH of Pure Water:** The pH of pure water is defined as $pH = -\log[H^+]$. For pure water, $[H^+] = [OH^-] = \sqrt{K_w}$.
        *   At $25^\circ C$: $[H^+] = \sqrt{1.0 \times 10^{-14}} = 1.0 \times 10^{-7}$ M. $pH = -\log(1.0 \times 10^{-7}) = 7$.
        *   At $50^\circ C$: $[H^+] = \sqrt{5.47 \times 10^{-14}} \approx 2.34 \times 10^{-7}$ M. $pH = -\log(2.34 \times 10^{-7}) \approx 6.63$.
    *   **Change in pH:** If the temperature of pure water is increased from $25^\circ C$ to $50^\circ C$, the concentration of $H^+$ ions increases, and therefore, the **pH of pure water decreases** from 7 to approximately 6.63. Although the pH decreases, the solution remains neutral because $[H^+] = [OH^-]$ at all temperatures.
