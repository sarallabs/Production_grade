**Scene 3**
**Duration:** 25 seconds
**Visual Prompt:** *A vibrant, steady shot of a typical titration setup. A burette is clamped vertically, filled with a clear liquid. Below it, a conical flask containing another clear liquid and a few drops of indicator. A scientist's gloved hand is carefully controlling the burette stopcock. Animated labels pop up and highlight: "Standard Solution" (in burette), "Titrant", "Titrate" (in flask). Close-up on the precise drop formation from the burette into the flask.*
**Narration:** Volumetric analysis involves a process called titration. Here, a solution of accurately known concentration, called the **standard solution** or **titrant**, is gradually added from a burette to a solution of unknown concentration, known as the **titrate**, in a conical flask. The goal is to react them completely.

**Scene 4**
**Duration:** 22 seconds
**Visual Prompt:** *Extreme close-up on the conical flask during titration. Initially, the solution is one color (e.g., colorless). As a single drop from the burette falls, there's a quick, almost magical flash of color change that persists. Animated text appears: "End Point" directly over the flask. Then, a subtle graphic illustration showing two overlapping points: "Equivalence Point" (theoretical) and "End Point" (observed), with a tiny gap labeled "Titration Error".*
**Narration:** The completion of the reaction is signaled by a sharp, visible color change, usually by an added **indicator**. This point is called the **end point**. Ideally, the end point should match the **equivalence point**, where chemically equivalent amounts of reactants have fully reacted. The slight difference between them is called the titration error.

**Scene 5**
**Duration:** 18 seconds
**Visual Prompt:** *Dynamic infographic style. Three distinct panels animate onto the screen. Panel 1: two chemicals vigorously mixing and reacting quickly, with a stopwatch icon. Panel 2: a simple, clear chemical equation appearing. Panel 3: a flask rapidly changing color. Soft, uplifting background music.*
**Narration:** For a reaction to be suitable for volumetric analysis, it must meet certain conditions. It should be rapid and proceed to completion. The reaction must be simple and well-defined. Crucially, there must be a prominent, detectable change in color at the end point, either by the reactants themselves or an added indicator.

**Narration:** Let's look at types of volumetric analysis. First, **Acidimetry and Alkalimetry**, also known as neutralization titrations. Here, an acid is titrated against a base, or vice-versa. For example, titrating hydrochloric acid with sodium hydroxide using phenolphthalein indicator, which turns pink at the end point.

**Narration:** Next are **Oxidation-Reduction, or Redox Titrations**. These involve reactions where oxidation states change or electrons are transferred. A classic example is titrating oxalic acid with potassium permanganate. Here, KMnO₄ acts as its own indicator, turning the solution a persistent light pink at the end point.
