Plastids are present in the form of proplastids, which are immature plastids. Essentially, meristematic cells are the parent cells from which all new cells are added to the plant body.

They are classified in three main ways: based on origin and development, based on position, and based on function.

This gives us three types: Promeristem, Primary Meristem, and Secondary Meristem.

What's a promeristem?

The promeristem is the earliest embryonic meristematic cell group found in a growing organ. In a plant, you'll find it at the very tip of the stem and root. It’s the origin point for primary meristems.

So, it's the very beginning of the meristematic tissue. And primary meristems?

Primary meristems are derived from the promeristem early in development. They are found at the tips of roots, stems, and appendages. Crucially, these meristems retain their ability to divide throughout the plant's life, forming the primary structure of the plant body.

And secondary meristems?

Secondary meristems arise when some primary permanent tissues regain their meristematic capacity. These are always found in a lateral position and are responsible for secondary growth, like healing wounds or increasing girth. Examples include the vascular cambium in dicot stems and the cork cambium.

That's a clear distinction. Now, let's move to classification based on position.

And lateral meristems?

Lateral meristems are found along the sides of roots and stems. As their name suggests, they are responsible for increasing the thickness or girth of the plant part. Vascular cambium and cork cambium are classic examples.

And the procambium?

And we learned about the unique features of the root apex, like the root cap and the quiescent center. Now, let's move on to the next major category of tissues: Permanent Tissues.

Permanent tissues are those that have lost their ability to divide and have attained a definite shape, size, and function. They can be living or dead, and thin-walled or thick-walled.

And how are permanent tissues classified?

They are divided into three main types: Simple Permanent Tissues, Complex Permanent Tissues, and Special or Secretory Tissues.

Let's start with Simple Permanent Tissues. What defines them?

Simple permanent tissues are made up of only one type of cell and are homogenous. They are further classified into three types: Parenchyma, Collenchyma, and Sclerenchyma.

Let's begin with Parenchyma. The term was given by Green.

Parenchyma cells are living, and they are generally isodiametric, though they can vary in shape – spherical, oval, rectangular, polygonal, elongated, or irregular. They have thin primary cell walls made of cellulose, hemicellulose, and calcium pectate.

And they usually have intercellular spaces?

Yes, they are typically loosely arranged with intercellular spaces. Each cell contains a vacuolated cytoplasm with a distinct nucleus. Parenchyma is abundant in soft parts of the plant like the pith, mesophyll of leaves, pulp of fruits, endosperm of seeds, and cortex of stems and roots.

What are the functions of parenchyma?

Their primary function is food storage. However, they can be modified for specialized roles. For instance, parenchyma containing chloroplasts are called chlorenchyma and perform photosynthesis.

Ah, so that's where photosynthesis happens in those tissues!

Correct. In aquatic plants, parenchyma cells develop large air spaces, forming aerenchyma, which provides buoyancy. In succulent xerophytic plants, they store water.

What about specialized parenchyma?

Some special parenchyma tissues, called idioblasts, produce and store substances like tannins, oils, and calcium oxalate crystals. Parenchyma in xylem and phloem, known as xylem parenchyma and phloem parenchyma respectively, are involved in the conduction of water and food. Elongated, thick-walled parenchyma cells are called prosenchyma, providing mechanical support.

That covers parenchyma well. Next up is Collenchyma.

Collenchyma tissues are also living and composed of somewhat elongated cells. Their defining feature is additional cellulose and pectin thickening in their cell walls, particularly at the corners.

So, they're like parenchyma but with thicker, more reinforced walls?

Exactly. In cross-section, they appear circular, oval, or polygonal. The cell walls are thick, primary, and non-lignified. They contain simple pits. Based on their thickening and arrangement, collenchyma is classified into three types.

Let's hear them.

(a) Angular Collenchyma, where thickenings are at the corners. (b) Lacunar Collenchyma, with thickenings bordering intercellular spaces. And (c) Plate or Lamellar Collenchyma, where thickenings occur on tangential walls.

Where are collenchyma typically found?

They are mainly found in the hypodermis of stems, petioles, pedicels, and leaves of dicot plants. They are usually absent in dicot roots and monocot plants, unless the root is exposed to light.

What are their functions?

They are a strengthening tissue, providing support, flexibility, and elasticity to growing organs. They prevent tearing of leaves and can perform photosynthesis if they contain chloroplasts. They also stretch with the growth and movement of the stem.

Moving on to Sclerenchyma. The name itself, "scleros" meaning hard, gives a clue.

Indeed. Sclerenchyma tissues have little or no protoplasm at maturity. Their cell walls are highly thickened and often lignified, with pits. They are dead at maturity and serve primarily mechanical functions, providing strength and rigidity.

So, they're like the structural beams of the plant.

Precisely. Sclerenchyma is classified into two groups: Fibres and Sclereids.

Let's start with Fibres.

Fibres are much elongated, narrow, thick-walled cells, usually with pointed ends. They possess great strength and flexibility. At maturity, they lose their protoplast and become dead and empty. Their secondary walls are highly thickened, often reducing the lumen, and they have simple pits.

Where do we find these fibres?

They are abundant in both monocot and dicot plants as chief mechanical tissues. They can occur in groups or as continuous layers. There are two types: bast fibres (found in pericycle and phloem) and wood fibres or xylem fibres (present in xylem).

And what about Sclereids?

Sclereids, often called stone cells, are highly thickened, dead sclerenchyma cells. They are typically shorter, circular, or more irregularly shaped and broader than fibres. They are common in the cortex and pith of gymnosperms and dicotyledons, and are often found in fruits and seeds, providing hardness.

So, they're the ones that make fruits like coconuts or almond shells so hard.

Exactly. Sclereids are further classified into five categories based on their shape: Brachysclereids (stone cells), Macrosclereids (elongated), Osteosclereids (bone-shaped), Astrosclereids (star-shaped), and Trichosclereids (hair-like).

So, to recap simple permanent tissues: Parenchyma for storage and various functions, Collenchyma for flexible support in growing regions, and Sclerenchyma for rigid mechanical strength.

That's a great summary. Now, let's move to Complex Permanent Tissues.

These are composed of different types of cells working together as a unit. They are heterogeneous and perform diverse functions. The two main complex permanent tissues are Xylem and Phloem.

Xylem, also known as wood, is primarily responsible for conducting water and minerals from the roots to the rest of the plant.

And Phloem, or bast, transports food materials from the leaves to other parts.

Xylem is composed of four types of cells: tracheids, vessels, xylem fibres, and xylem parenchyma. All except xylem parenchyma are dead.

Let's break down these components. Tracheids are elongated, tube-like dead cells with thick, lignified walls and a tapering or oblique end.

Vessels, or tracheae, are also long, cylindrical, dead structures, but they are formed by a series of cells placed end to end with perforated or dissolved end walls, forming continuous tubes. They are considered a more advanced type of conducting element than tracheids, found mainly in angiosperms.

What about thickenings on their walls?

Both tracheids and vessels show various types of thickenings like annular, spiral, reticulate, scalariform, and pitted, due to the deposition of lignin.

And xylem fibres?

Xylem fibres are sclerenchymatous cells, also dead, providing mechanical strength. Xylem parenchyma, on the other hand, are living cells involved in storing food and lateral conduction.

So, xylem is a team of water transporters and structural supports.

Absolutely. Now, for phloem. It's composed of sieve-tubes, companion cells, phloem parenchyma, and phloem fibres. All except phloem fibres are living.

Sieve-tubes are formed by elongated cells arranged end to end, with sieve plates on their end walls, which are perforated like a sieve.

Companion cells are thin-walled, living cells closely associated with sieve-tubes, aiding in their function and providing energy. They are found only in angiosperms.

What about phloem parenchyma and phloem fibres?

Phloem parenchyma are living cells that store food. Phloem fibres are elongated, dead sclerenchymatous cells that provide strength.

So, phloem is the plant's food distribution network.

Precisely. We've covered simple and complex permanent tissues. Let's briefly touch upon Special or Secretory Tissues.

These tissues are involved in secreting substances like gums, resins, oils, and latex.

They can be classified into laticiferous tissue (producing latex) and glandular tissue (forming glands). Laticiferous tissues can be latex vessels or latex cells. Glandular tissues can be internal, like oil glands, or external, like digestive glands and nectaries.

That gives us a good overview of the different tissue types. Now, let's move on to the internal structure of roots, stems, and leaves.

We'll start with the Dicot Root. A transverse section reveals distinct layers.

First, the Epidermis, also called epiblema or piliferous layer, which bears unicellular root hairs for water and mineral absorption.

Next is the Cortex, a well-developed region of parenchymatous cells with intercellular spaces, involved in storage.

Then comes the Endodermis, the innermost cortical layer, characterized by Casparian strips on radial and tangential walls, which regulate water movement.

Following the endodermis is the Pericycle, from which lateral roots originate.

And then the Vascular Bundles, which are radial in roots, meaning xylem and phloem alternate on different radii. They show an exarch condition, where protoxylem is towards the periphery.

Finally, there's the Conjunctive Tissue between the xylem and phloem, and a usually reduced or absent Pith in the center.

Let's contrast this with the Monocot Root.

Monocot roots also have epidermis, cortex, endodermis, and pericycle, similar to dicots. However, they typically have more vascular bundles, usually 8 to 20, arranged radially. The pith is well-developed.

And importantly, monocot roots generally lack secondary growth.

Now, let's move to the Dicot Stem. A transverse section shows Epidermis with cuticle and stomata, often with multicellular hairs.

Below that is the Hypodermis, a few layers of collenchymatous cells providing mechanical strength. Then the Cortex, composed of parenchymatous cells.

The Endodermis is the innermost cortical layer, sometimes called the starch sheath. The Pericycle is several-celled thick and consists of alternating parenchymatous and sclerenchymatous cells.

The Vascular Bundles in a dicot stem are arranged in a ring, and they are conjoint, collateral, and open, meaning they have cambium between xylem and phloem.

This cambium is crucial for secondary growth, increasing the stem's thickness. The Pith is well-developed and centrally located, separated by Medullary Rays or Pith Rays.

Let's compare this to the Monocot Stem.

Monocot stems have epidermis, but the hypodermis is sclerenchymatous, providing strength. The ground tissue is not differentiated into cortex, endodermis, and pericycle; it's a single mass of parenchymatous cells.

And the vascular bundles are scattered throughout the ground tissue, not arranged in a ring. They are conjoint, collateral, and closed, meaning cambium is absent, hence no secondary growth.

Each vascular bundle is surrounded by a sclerenchymatous bundle sheath. A unique feature in monocot stems like maize is the presence of a lysigenous cavity, a water cavity formed by the disintegration of protoxylem elements.

So, key differences are the arrangement of vascular bundles, presence of cambium, and differentiation of ground tissue.

Exactly. Now, for leaves. Dicot leaves are dorsiventral, meaning they have distinct upper and lower surfaces.

The upper and lower epidermis are covered by cuticle. Stomata are mostly found on the lower epidermis, making the leaf hypostomatic.

The Mesophyll, the tissue between the epidermis, is differentiated into palisade parenchyma (elongated, tightly packed, rich in chloroplasts) and spongy parenchyma (loosely arranged with air spaces).

Vascular bundles, or veins, are conjoint, collateral, and closed, embedded in the mesophyll and surrounded by a bundle sheath.

Monocot leaves, on the other hand, are isobilateral, meaning both surfaces are similar. Stomata are equally distributed on both surfaces (amphistomatic).

The mesophyll is not differentiated into palisade and spongy layers; it's a uniform tissue. A unique feature in some monocots like maize are bulliform cells, or motor cells, in the upper epidermis that help in leaf rolling.

Vascular bundles in monocot leaves are also conjoint, collateral, and closed, arranged in parallel series and surrounded by a bundle sheath.

Let's quickly summarize the differences: Dicot roots have radial vascular bundles and exarch xylem, while stems have conjoint, collateral, open vascular bundles and endarch xylem. Monocot roots also have radial bundles but more numerous ones and a developed pith. Monocot stems have scattered, conjoint, collateral, closed vascular bundles. Dicot leaves are dorsiventral with differentiated mesophyll, while monocot leaves are isobilateral with undifferentiated mesophyll and bulliform cells.

That brings us to Secondary Growth. This is the increase in thickness or girth of the plant axis due to the formation of secondary tissues by the activity of cambium and cork-cambium. It primarily occurs in dicot stems and roots.

In a dicot stem, the process begins with the formation of a Cambium Ring, where intrafascicular cambium (within vascular bundles) and interfascicular cambium (between vascular bundles) connect.

This cambium ring then produces Secondary Vascular Tissues. More secondary xylem is formed inwards, commonly called wood, and secondary phloem outwards. The primary phloem gets crushed.

Secondary growth also involves the formation of Vascular Rays, which are extensions of the pith that facilitate radial transport.

A fascinating aspect of secondary growth in temperate regions is the formation of Annual Rings. The activity of the vascular cambium varies with seasons, leading to the formation of spring wood (wider vessels, less dense) and autumn wood (narrower vessels, denser). Counting these rings helps determine a tree's age.

This study of tree rings is called Dendrochronology.

In older stems, the wood differentiates into Sapwood (outer, lighter, living, functional for conduction) and Heartwood (central, darker, dead, provides strength and resistance to decay).

And as the stem grows thicker, the epidermis ruptures, and a new protective layer, the Periderm, is formed by the Cork Cambium.

The periderm consists of phellem (cork), cork cambium (phellogen), and phelloderm (secondary cortex). Cork cells are dead, lignified, and suberized, making them impermeable and protective.

Bark is essentially all the tissues outside the vascular cambium, including secondary phloem and periderm.

Finally, Lenticels are raised pores in the bark that allow gaseous exchange between the atmosphere and the internal tissues, especially important during secondary growth.
