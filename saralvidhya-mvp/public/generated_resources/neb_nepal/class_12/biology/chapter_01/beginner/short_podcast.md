That's right. So, what exactly is plant anatomy?

Plant anatomy is the study of the internal structure of plants. Think of it like looking inside a plant to see how it's built and how its different parts work together. And we have a pioneer in this field, N. Grew, who is often called the Father of Plant Anatomy. He even coined the term "tissue"!

Tissue! So, what is a tissue?

A tissue is basically a group of cells that have a common origin and work together to perform specific jobs. In higher plants, these tissues are super specialized, meaning different cells have different roles, like a team with various players.

So, how do we classify these plant tissues?

Based on their ability to divide, plant tissues are broadly classified into two main types: meristematic tissue and permanent tissue.

Let's start with meristematic tissue. What's special about it?

The term "meristem" comes from a Greek word meaning "divisible." So, meristematic tissues are essentially groups of immature cells that have the capacity to divide. They are the plant's growth engines!

Ah, so they're like the construction crew of the plant, always building new parts! What are their characteristics?

Meristematic cells are typically small, thin-walled, and living. Their cell walls are elastic, and they're packed tightly together with no gaps or intercellular spaces. They're usually round or oval, with a dense cytoplasm, a large nucleus, and very small or absent vacuoles. And importantly, they contain proplastids, which are precursors to other plant structures.

Meristems can be classified in a few ways. One way is based on their origin and development.

What are those types?

First, we have promeristem, which are the earliest embryonic meristematic cells found at the tips of growing organs like the root and shoot. They give rise to primary meristems.

So, promeristem is like the very first blueprint.

Exactly! Then we have primary meristem, which develops from the promeristem. These are found at the tips of roots, stems, and appendages. They keep dividing throughout the plant's life, forming the primary structure of the plant body.

And what about secondary meristem?

Secondary meristems arise when some primary permanent tissues regain their ability to divide. These are usually found on the sides, or laterally, and are important for secondary growth, like increasing the thickness of a stem. Think of the vascular cambium in a dicot stem – that's a type of secondary meristem.

That's a great distinction. Is there another way to classify meristems?

And intercalary?

Lateral meristems are located along the sides of roots and stems. They are responsible for increasing the thickness or girth of the plant. Vascular cambium and cork cambium are good examples of lateral meristems.

That's clear. So, we have meristems based on origin and development, and then based on position. Are there any other classifications?

Yes, there's also classification based on function. This gives us protoderm, which forms the outer epidermal layer; procambium, which develops into primary vascular tissues; and ground meristem, which forms the ground tissue system.

It's amazing how many ways these growth tissues are organized! Now, what about the shoot apex and root apex?

The shoot apex is a dome-shaped structure covered by young leaves. It differentiates into organs like leaves and buds, and it's crucial for stem growth.

And the root apex?

The root apex is protected by a root cap, which shields the meristem as the root grows through the soil. Unlike the shoot apex, the root apex isn't involved in forming lateral appendages; those form further back. Also, root branches grow from the inside out (endogenous), while shoot branches grow from the outside in (exogenous). A unique feature of the root apex is the "quiescent center," an area of slower-dividing cells that acts as a reserve of meristematic cells.

That's a really important distinction between roots and shoots. Now, let's move on to permanent tissues. What happens to meristematic cells when they stop dividing?

When meristematic cells lose their ability to divide, they mature and specialize into permanent tissues. They take on a definite shape, size, and function. These can be living or dead, and have thin or thick walls.

And how are permanent tissues classified?

Permanent tissues are divided into three main types: simple permanent tissues, complex permanent tissues, and special or secretory tissues.

Let's start with simple permanent tissues. What are they?

Simple permanent tissues are made up of only one type of cell and are uniform in structure. They are further divided into three types: parenchyma, collenchyma, and sclerenchyma.

Tell us about parenchyma.

Parenchyma cells are living, typically isodiametric, and have thin primary cell walls made of cellulose. They are usually loosely arranged, meaning there are intercellular spaces between them. Each cell has cytoplasm, a nucleus, and vacuoles. Parenchyma is found in many soft parts of the plant, like the pith, mesophyll of leaves, and the pulp of fruits.

What are the functions of parenchyma?

Their main job is storing food. But they can be modified! Parenchyma with chloroplasts is called chlorenchyma and performs photosynthesis. In aquatic plants, parenchyma with large air spaces is called aerenchyma, which helps with buoyancy. In succulents, they store water. Some specialized parenchyma called idioblasts produce and store things like tannins or oils. And in xylem and phloem, parenchyma helps with conduction. Elongated, thick-walled parenchyma cells are called prosenchyma and provide mechanical support.

That's a lot of different roles for one type of cell! What about collenchyma?

Collenchyma cells are also living, but they are somewhat elongated and have extra cellulose and pectin thickening in their walls, especially at the corners. This gives them strength and flexibility. They are mainly found in the hypodermis of stems, petioles, and leaves of dicots.

So, collenchyma provides support, especially to growing parts?

Exactly! It's a strengthening tissue that provides support, flexibility, and elasticity. It can also perform photosynthesis if it has chloroplasts and store food.

And the third type of simple permanent tissue is sclerenchyma.

Sclerenchyma cells are characterized by thick, often lignified, and dead cell walls at maturity. Lignin makes them hard and impermeable. They primarily provide mechanical strength and rigidity to the plant.

So, they are the strong, rigid structural components. What are the types of sclerenchyma?

Sclerenchyma is divided into two main types: fibres and sclereids. Fibres are elongated, narrow, thick-walled cells that provide great strength and flexibility, used in textiles. Sclereids, often called "stone cells," are shorter, more irregularly shaped cells found in hard parts like fruit pits and seed coats, giving them their hardness.

That covers simple permanent tissues. What are complex permanent tissues?

Complex permanent tissues are made up of different types of cells that work together as a unit. The two main examples are xylem and phloem, which together form the vascular tissue system.

Let's talk about xylem, also known as wood.

Xylem is a conducting tissue responsible for transporting water and minerals from the roots to the rest of the plant. It consists of four types of cells: tracheids, vessels, xylem fibres, and xylem parenchyma.

Are all these cells living?

No, all except xylem parenchyma are dead. Tracheids are elongated, tube-like dead cells with thick, lignified walls and tapering ends. Vessels, found in most angiosperms, are long, cylindrical structures made of many cells joined end-to-end, with perforated end walls, forming continuous pipelines for water. Xylem fibres are sclerenchymatous cells that provide mechanical strength. And xylem parenchyma are living cells that store food and help in lateral conduction.

So, tracheids and vessels are the main water highways, while fibres provide structural support and parenchyma helps with storage and sideways transport. What about phloem?

Phloem, also known as bast, is another conducting tissue, but it transports food materials from the leaves to other parts of the plant. It's also a complex tissue and consists of sieve-tube elements, companion cells, phloem parenchyma, and phloem fibres.

Are these living too?

All except phloem fibres are living. Sieve-tube elements are elongated cells arranged end-to-end with sieve plates on their walls, allowing for food passage. Companion cells are closely associated with sieve tubes, providing them with energy and controlling material passage. Phloem parenchyma stores food, and phloem fibres provide mechanical support.

It's fascinating how xylem and phloem work together to keep the plant nourished and hydrated. Now, what are these special or secretory tissues?

Secretory tissues are specialized for secreting substances like gums, resins, oils, and latex. They can be classified into laticiferous tissue and glandular tissue.

Laticiferous tissue sounds interesting.

Laticiferous tissues contain specialized cells called laticifers, which secrete latex. This latex can be milky, watery, or colored and is found in plants like rubber trees and opium poppies.

And glandular tissue?

Glandular tissues form glands that secrete various substances. These can be internal, like oil glands in citrus fruits, or external, like digestive glands in insectivorous plants or nectaries in flowers that attract pollinators.

Wow, plants have a whole chemical factory inside them! Now, let's move on to the internal structure of roots, stems, and leaves. We'll start with a dicot root.

In a transverse section of a dicot root, we see the epidermis, also called epiblema, which has root hairs for water absorption. Inside that is the cortex, made of parenchyma cells. Then comes the endodermis, the innermost layer of the cortex, characterized by Casparian strips.

And inside the endodermis?

That's the pericycle, from which lateral roots originate. The vascular bundles in a dicot root are arranged radially, with xylem and phloem alternating. The xylem shows an exarch condition, meaning the protoxylem is towards the outside. And the pith is usually small or absent.

Now, what about a monocot root? How does it differ?

Monocot roots are quite similar, but they usually have more vascular bundles, typically 8 to 20, and a well-developed pith in the center. They also lack secondary growth.

That's a key difference! Let's move to stems. What's the internal structure of a dicot stem, like a sunflower stem?

A dicot stem has an epidermis covered by a cuticle, followed by a hypodermis, which is often collenchymatous for support. The cortex is made of parenchyma. The endodermis is the innermost layer of the cortex, and inside that is the pericycle. The vascular bundles are arranged in a ring, are conjoint, collateral, and open, meaning they have cambium between the xylem and phloem.

And what about the center of the dicot stem?

The center is occupied by the pith, which is made of parenchyma and stores food. The presence of vascular bundles in a ring and the open nature of these bundles allow for secondary growth, increasing the stem's thickness.

And a monocot stem, like maize?

Monocot stems have vascular bundles scattered throughout the ground tissue, not arranged in a ring. These bundles are conjoint, collateral, and closed, meaning they lack cambium. They also have a sclerenchymatous hypodermis for support, and the ground tissue is not differentiated into cortex, endodermis, and pith.

So, scattered vascular bundles and lack of cambium are the big giveaways for a monocot stem. What about leaves? We have dorsiventral leaves, like in dicots.

Dorsiventral leaves have distinct upper and lower surfaces. The epidermis on both sides is covered by a cuticle, and stomata are mostly found on the lower epidermis. The mesophyll, the tissue between the epidermis, is differentiated into palisade parenchyma (elongated cells with many chloroplasts) and spongy parenchyma (loosely arranged cells with air spaces).

And the vascular bundles in leaves?

They are conjoint, collateral, and closed, embedded in the mesophyll and surrounded by a bundle sheath.

And isobilateral leaves, like in monocots?

Isobilateral leaves have similar structures on both surfaces. Stomata are equally distributed on both sides, making them amphistomatic. The mesophyll isn't differentiated into palisade and spongy layers; it's just a layer of chlorenchymatous cells. Bulliform cells, which help in leaf rolling, are also a feature.
