A tissue, in simple terms, is a group of cells that share a common origin and work together to perform specific functions. Think of them like specialized teams within the larger plant body, each with its own job. In higher plants like angiosperms and gymnosperms, these teams are quite advanced, showing a clear division of labor.

Division of labor, that's a great way to put it. And based on their ability to divide, plant tissues are broadly categorized into two main types. Can you guess what they are?

Meristematic and Permanent tissues!

Exactly! Let's start with meristematic tissues. The term "meristem" itself comes from the Greek word "meristos," meaning divisible. So, meristematic tissues are essentially groups of immature cells that have the capacity to divide.

And these cells have some distinct features. They're small, living, and have thin, elastic cell walls, usually without a secondary wall. They're packed tightly together, with no intercellular spaces. They can be spherical, oval, or polygonal, and each contains a dense cytoplasm and a prominent nucleus. Vacuoles are either absent or very small, and they contain proplastids, which are the precursors to other plastids. Essentially, these are the parent cells that add new cells to the plant body.

That makes sense. So, if they're the "divisible" cells, how are they organized? Meristems can be classified in a few ways. One way is based on their origin and development.

Right. First, we have the Promeristem. This is the earliest embryonic meristematic tissue, found at the tips of growing organs like the root and shoot. It gives rise to the primary meristem.

Then comes the Primary Meristem. This is derived from the promeristem and is found at the tips of roots, stems, and appendages. These tissues retain their ability to divide throughout the plant's life, contributing to the primary structure of the plant body. Interestingly, the vascular cambium in dicot stems can also be considered a type of primary meristem, though it divides in only one plane.

And finally, the Secondary Meristem. These are primary permanent tissues that regain their meristematic capacity. They are always located laterally and are crucial for healing wounds or for secondary growth, like the vascular cambium in roots or the cork cambium.

That's origin and development. What about their position in the plant?

And the third type by position?

Lateral Meristems. These are found along the sides of roots and stems and are responsible for increasing the thickness or girth of the plant part. Vascular cambium and cork cambium are prime examples.

Moving on from meristems, let's briefly touch upon the Shoot Apex and Root Apex. The shoot apex is a dome-shaped structure that differentiates into leaves, buds, and floral buds, driving stem growth. The root apex, on the other hand, is protected by a root cap and is subterminal in position. It's not involved in forming lateral appendages like the shoot apex.

And the root apex has a unique feature called the "quiescent center," an area of slow-dividing cells that acts as a reservoir for active initials if the main ones are damaged.

Now, let's shift our focus to Permanent Tissues. These are tissues that have lost their dividing capacity and have attained a definite shape, size, and function. They can be living or dead, thin-walled or thick-walled.

Permanent tissues are further divided into three main types: Simple, Complex, and Special or Secretory tissues. We'll start with Simple Permanent Tissues.

Simple permanent tissues are made up of only one type of cell and are homogenous. The three types are Parenchyma, Collenchyma, and Sclerenchyma.

Let's break down Parenchyma. Coined by Green, parenchyma cells are living, usually isodiametric, and have thin primary cell walls made of cellulose, hemicellulose, and calcium pectate. They are often loosely arranged with intercellular spaces.

Parenchyma is found throughout the plant's soft parts – in the pith, mesophyll of leaves, fruit pulp, and more. Its primary role is food storage, but it has specialized forms. Chlorenchyma contains chloroplasts for photosynthesis. Aerenchyma, found in aquatic plants, has large air spaces for buoyancy. In succulents, it stores water. Special parenchyma cells called idioblasts produce and store tannins or oils. Elongated parenchyma cells are called prosenchyma and provide mechanical support.

Next up, Collenchyma. These are living, elongated cells with extra cellulose and pectin thickening in their walls, particularly at the corners, on tangential walls, or around intercellular spaces.

Collenchyma is a strengthening tissue, providing flexibility and elasticity to growing organs. It's often found in the hypodermis of stems and petioles. It can even perform photosynthesis if it contains chloroplasts.

And the last of the simple permanent tissues is Sclerenchyma. These tissues have little or no protoplasm at maturity, with thick, lignified cell walls, making them dead. They are the primary mechanical tissues, providing strength and rigidity.

Sclerenchyma is divided into two types: Fibres and Sclereids. Fibres are elongated, narrow, thick-walled cells with pointed ends, providing great strength and flexibility. Think of jute and hemp – those are bast fibres, found in the pericycle and phloem. Wood fibres are found in xylem.

And Sclereids, often called "stone cells," are shorter, more irregularly shaped, and provide hardness. They're common in fruits like almonds and coconuts, and seed coats, protecting the inner parts. We have different types like brachysclereids, macrosclereids, osteosclereids, astrosclereids, and trichosclereids, each with unique shapes and locations.

Now, let's move to Complex Permanent Tissues. These are made of different types of cells working together as a unit. The primary examples are Xylem and Phloem, collectively known as vascular tissues.

Xylem, or wood, is crucial for conducting water and minerals from the roots upwards. It consists of four types of cells: tracheids, vessels, xylem fibres, and xylem parenchyma. Tracheids and vessels are the main conducting elements and are dead at maturity.

Tracheids are elongated, tube-like dead cells with lignified walls and pits. Vessels are also long, cylindrical, dead structures, formed by the fusion of cells with perforated end walls, acting like continuous water pipes. Xylem fibres are sclerenchymatous cells providing mechanical strength, and xylem parenchyma are living cells that store food and aid in lateral conduction.

First-formed xylem is protoxylem, with smaller vessels and annular or spiral thickenings. Later-formed xylem is metaxylem, with larger vessels and reticulate or pitted thickenings.

Phloem, or bast, transports food materials from the leaves to other parts of the plant. It's also a complex tissue, composed of sieve-tube elements, companion cells, phloem parenchyma, and phloem fibres.

Sieve-tubes are elongated cells with sieve plates on their end walls. Companion cells are closely associated with sieve-tubes and provide them with energy and control material passage. Phloem parenchyma stores food, and phloem fibres, which are dead sclerenchymatous cells, provide mechanical support.

The Vascular Tissue System is organized into vascular bundles, containing xylem and phloem. In dicot stems, these bundles are arranged in rings, while in monocot stems, they are scattered.

Vascular bundles can be radial, where xylem and phloem alternate on different radii, characteristic of roots, or conjoint, where they lie on the same radius. Conjoint bundles can be collateral (xylem inside, phloem outside), bicollateral (phloem on both sides of xylem, seen in Cucurbita), or concentric (one tissue encircles the other).

Conjoint collateral bundles can be open if cambium is present (dicots) or closed if absent (monocots). Amphivasal bundles have phloem in the center surrounded by xylem, and amphicribal have xylem in the center surrounded by phloem, common in ferns.

The arrangement of xylem within the bundle also differs: exarch in roots (protoxylem towards the periphery) and endarch in stems (protoxylem towards the center). Mesarch is found in some ferns.

Finally, we have Secretory Tissues, responsible for secreting substances like gums, resins, oils, and latex. These can be laticiferous tissues, like latex vessels or cells, or glandular tissues, forming structures called glands.

These glands can be internal, like oil glands, or external, like digestive glands in insectivorous plants or nectaries that attract pollinators. Hydathodes, which secrete water, are another important type of secretory structure.
