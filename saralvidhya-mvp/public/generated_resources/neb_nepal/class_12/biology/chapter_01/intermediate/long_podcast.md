They're typically small, thin-walled, and living. Their cell walls are elastic and cellulosic, usually lacking a secondary wall. They're packed tightly together with no intercellular spaces. Plastids are present as proplastids. Essentially, these are the parent cells from which all new plant cells originate.

Promeristem refers to the earliest embryonic meristematic cells found in a growing organ, usually at the tips of the stem and root. It's the initial stage that gives rise to the primary meristem.

And Primary Meristem?

Primary meristem is derived from the promeristem during early development. It's found at the tips of roots, stems, and appendages. These tissues retain their division capacity throughout the plant's life, dividing in all planes to form the primary structure of the plant body. Interestingly, the vascular cambium of dicot stems is a type of primary meristem that divides in only one plane to form secondary permanent tissues.

Secondary meristems arise when some primary permanent tissues regain their meristematic capacity. They are always lateral in position and are crucial for healing wounds or enabling secondary growth. Examples include the vascular cambium of roots, interfascicular cambium of stems, and cork cambium.

Lateral meristems, as the name suggests, lie along the lateral sides of roots and stems. They are responsible for increasing the plant's thickness or girth. Vascular cambium and cork cambium are prime examples.

What's the difference between Tunica and Corpus?

The Tunica is the outer zone, consisting of one or more peripheral layers of cells that divide anticlinally (perpendicular to the surface), contributing to the surface area and forming the epidermis and leaf primordia. The Corpus is the central, multilayered mass of cells that divides in various planes, forming the bulk of the plant body, including the cortex, vascular tissue, and pith.

So, Tunica adds layers to the surface, and Corpus adds bulk. What about the root apex?

The root apex is similar but has a crucial difference: it's protected by a root cap, or calyptra. This means the active meristematic region is subterminal, not terminal. Also, lateral organs aren't formed at the root apex; they develop further back. Root branches are endogenous, originating from within.

Yes, the "quiescent center." This is a cup-like region of cells between the root cap and the active meristematic zone. That's a clever survival mechanism! So, after meristematic tissues, we move to permanent tissues. What defines them?

Permanent tissues are those that have lost their capacity to divide and have attained a definite shape, size, and function. They can be living or dead, thin-walled or thick-walled.

And how are permanent tissues classified?

They're divided into three main types: Simple Permanent Tissues, Complex Permanent Tissues, and Special or Secretory Tissues. Let's start with Simple Permanent Tissues.

Simple tissues are made of just one type of cell, right?

Precisely. They are homogeneous and include Parenchyma, Collenchyma, and Sclerenchyma.

Tell us about Parenchyma.

Parenchyma, a term given by Green, consists of living cells. They are typically isodiametric, though their shape can vary greatly – spherical, oval, rectangular, polygonal, or elongated. They have thin primary cell walls made of cellulose, hemicellulose, and calcium pectate. They are usually loosely arranged with intercellular spaces and contain a distinct nucleus and vacuolated cytoplasm.

Where do we find parenchyma?

It's abundant in soft plant parts: pith, mesophyll of leaves, fruit pulp, seed endosperm, and the cortex of stems and roots. It also occurs in xylem and phloem.

What are its functions?

Its primary role is food storage. But it's highly adaptable. Parenchyma with chloroplasts is called chlorenchyma and performs photosynthesis. In aquatic plants, specialized parenchyma called aerenchyma with large air spaces provides buoyancy. In succulents, it stores water. Special types called idioblasts can store tannins, oils, or crystals. Parenchyma in xylem and phloem aids in conduction. Elongated, thick-walled parenchyma cells are called prosenchyma, providing mechanical support.

That's a lot of versatility for one cell type! Next up, Collenchyma.

Collenchyma are living tissues made of elongated cells with additional cellulose and pectin thickenings in their walls. They are typically circular, oval, or polygonal in cross-section. Their cell walls are thick, primary, and non-lignified, containing simple pits.

How are they classified?

Based on thickening and arrangement, there are three types: Angular Collenchyma, with thickening at the corners; Lacunar Collenchyma, with thickening around intercellular spaces; and Plate or Lamellar Collenchyma, with thickening on tangential walls.

Where are they typically found?

Primarily in the hypodermis of stems, petioles, pedicels, and leaves of dicots. They're usually absent in dicot roots and monocots unless the root is exposed to light.

And their functions?

Collenchyma is a strengthening tissue providing support, flexibility, and elasticity to growing organs. It prevents tearing of leaves and can perform photosynthesis if it contains chloroplasts. It also aids in food storage and stretches with growth.

Now for Sclerenchyma. The name comes from "scleros," meaning hard.

These sound like the structural workhorses.

They are! Sclerenchyma cells have little or no protoplasm at maturity. Their cell walls are highly thickened and often lignified, making them impermeable to water and gases. They are dead at maturity and provide strength and rigidity, withstanding strains from stretching and bending.

How are sclerenchyma classified?

Into two main types: Fibres and Sclereids.

Let's start with Fibres.

Fibres are much elongated, narrow, thick-walled cells, usually with pointed ends. They possess great strength and flexibility. At maturity, they lose their protoplast and become dead and empty. Their secondary walls are highly thickened, reducing the lumen significantly. They lack intercellular spaces. Bast fibres and wood fibres are two types.

And Sclereids?

Sclereids, often called stone cells, are shorter, circular, or irregularly shaped cells with highly thickened, lignified, dead walls. They are broader and usually isodiametric. They're common in fruits, seeds, and barks, providing hardness and strength, like in the stony fruits of almonds or the seed coat of dates.

We have five categories of sclereids: Brachysclereids, Macrosclereids, Osteosclereids, Astrosclereids, and Trichosclereids.

Exactly! Each with unique shapes and locations, all contributing to mechanical strength.

That covers simple permanent tissues. Now, let's move to Complex Permanent Tissues.

Complex tissues are heterogeneous, composed of different types of cells that work together as a unit to perform diverse functions. The primary examples are Xylem and Phloem, collectively known as vascular tissues.

Xylem, or wood, is responsible for water and mineral conduction from roots to other parts.

Correct. Xylem consists of four types of cells: Tracheids, Vessels, Xylem fibres, and Xylem parenchyma. All except xylem parenchyma are dead.

Tell us about Tracheids and Vessels.

Tracheids are elongated, tube-like dead cells with thick, lignified walls and a wide lumen. They are the most primitive conducting elements. Vessels, or tracheae, are more advanced, forming long, cylindrical tubes made of multiple cells joined end-to-end with perforated or dissolved end walls. Both have various types of thickenings like annular, spiral, reticulate, scalariform, and pitted.

And Xylem fibres and parenchyma?

Xylem fibres are sclerenchymatous cells providing strength. Xylem parenchyma are living cells that store food and aid in lateral conduction.

What about the earliest formed xylem and later formed xylem?

The first formed xylem is protoxylem, with elements that can stretch easily, often showing annular and spiral thickenings. The later formed xylem is metaxylem, with larger vessels and reticulate or pitted thickenings, not subjected to much strain.

Now, Phloem, or bast. It transports food materials.

Phloem is also a complex tissue, composed of Sieve-tube elements, Companion cells, Phloem parenchyma, and Phloem fibres. All except phloem fibres are living.

Sieve-tubes are the main conducting channels, aren't they?

Yes, they are elongated tube-like cells arranged end-to-end with sieve plates on their end walls, which are perforated. They lack nuclei at maturity but are connected to companion cells.

And Companion cells?

Companion cells are thin-walled, living cells closely associated with sieve tubes. They have a dense cytoplasm and a prominent nucleus and play a vital role in supporting sieve tubes and regulating material passage. They are unique to angiosperms.

What about Phloem parenchyma and fibres?

Phloem parenchyma are living cells that store food. Phloem fibres are dead, sclerenchymatous cells that provide mechanical strength.

That's a solid foundation! Now, let's talk about Secretory Tissues.

Laticiferous tissue secretes latex. What is latex?

Latex is a watery, milky, colored, or viscous fluid found in specialized parenchymatous cells called laticifers. It can contain sugars, proteins, alkaloids, and even rubber, as in the case of Hevea.

And what are the types of laticiferous tissues?

There are two types: Latex Vessels, which are articulate latex ducts formed by fused cells, and Latex Cells, which are single, independent, elongated cells.

Correct. Glandular tissues can be internal or external. Internal glands include oil glands, which secrete essential oils, and glands secreting resins or gums. That wraps up our discussion on tissues. Now, let's transition to the internal structure of different plant parts. We'll start with roots.

A transverse section of a primary dicot root reveals the Epidermis, also called Epiblema or piliferous layer. It's the outermost layer, with thin-walled, living parenchymatous cells, often bearing unicellular root hairs for water and mineral absorption.

Following the epidermis is the Cortex.

The cortex is well-developed, composed of thin-walled parenchymatous cells with intercellular spaces. These cells store starch and lack chloroplasts.

And inside the cortex?

The innermost layer is the Endodermis, characterized by barrel-shaped cells without intercellular spaces. Most cells have Casparian strips – thickenings of suberin and lignin on radial and tangential walls. Some cells opposite the protoxylem remain unthickened and are called passage cells, facilitating water diffusion.

Just inside the endodermis is the Pericycle.

The pericycle is usually a single layer of parenchymatous cells. It's important because lateral roots originate from it, and it contributes to the vascular cambium and cork cambium during secondary growth.

What about the vascular bundles in a dicot root?

They are radial, meaning xylem and phloem are on different radii, alternating with each other. There are typically two to six xylem and phloem bundles. The xylem shows an exarch condition, with protoxylem towards the periphery and metaxylem towards the center.

And the pith?

In dicot roots, the pith is usually absent or highly reduced. The xylem and phloem strands are separated by conjunctive tissue, which can form cambium during secondary growth.

Let's contrast that with a Monocot Root.

Monocot roots also have an epidermis (epiblema) with root hairs, a cortex, and an endodermis with Casparian strips and passage cells. The pericycle is usually single-layered and gives rise to lateral roots.

What's different about the vascular bundles?

In monocot roots, there are typically many vascular bundles, usually 8 to 20, and the pith is well-developed. The xylem is exarch, similar to dicot roots.

Are there any other notable differences?

Yes, monocot roots generally lack secondary growth, which is a key distinguishing feature.

We've covered roots. Now, let's shift our focus to stems. First, the Dicot Stem, using the sunflower as an example.

The outermost layer is the Epidermis, a single layer of thin-walled parenchymatous cells, often covered by a cuticle and sometimes bearing multicellular hairs.

Following the epidermis is the Hypodermis, a few layers of collenchymatous cells providing mechanical strength.

Then comes the Cortex, composed of loosely arranged parenchymatous cells with intercellular spaces, which may contain chloroplasts.

The innermost layer of the cortex is the Endodermis, often called the starch sheath due to starch accumulation. However, in many dicots, it's not very distinct.

Inside the endodermis, we find the Pericycle, a multi-layered region of alternating parenchymatous and sclerenchymatous cells. The sclerenchymatous part, outside the vascular bundles, is called bundle cap or hard bast.

The Vascular Bundles are arranged in a ring around the central pith. They are conjoint, collateral, and open, meaning they have cambium between the xylem and phloem. Xylem is towards the center (endarch), and phloem is towards the periphery.

And separating these vascular bundles are the Medullary Rays, or Pith Rays, which are parenchymatous cells facilitating radial transport.

Finally, the central region is the Pith, consisting of parenchymatous cells for food storage.

The epidermis is similar, but the hypodermis is more developed, especially below the ridges. The cortex is composed of parenchymatous cells.

The endodermis is present as a starch sheath, and the pericycle is sclerenchymatous, forming a continuous wavy band.

The most striking feature of the Cucurbita stem is its vascular bundles. They are conjoint, bicollateral, and open, meaning there's phloem on both sides of the xylem, and cambium is present. They are arranged in two rings.

And the pith? In a mature stem, it often disintegrates to form a hollow pith cavity.

Now, for the Monocot Stem, using maize as an example.

The epidermis is similar, but the hypodermis is sclerenchymatous, providing mechanical strength.

The Ground Tissue is not differentiated into cortex, endodermis, and pericycle. Instead, it's a continuous mass of parenchymatous cells.

The Vascular Bundles are scattered throughout the ground tissue. They are conjoint, collateral, and closed, meaning no cambium is present. Each bundle is surrounded by a sclerenchymatous bundle sheath.

The xylem is typically Y-shaped, with metaxylem vessels and a lysigenous cavity below the protoxylem, which stores water.

Absolutely. Now, let's move to leaves. We'll first examine a Dorsiventral Leaf, typical of dicots.

The leaf is covered by upper and lower epidermis, usually with a cuticle. Stomata are mostly found on the lower epidermis, making the leaf hypostomatic.

The Mesophyll, the green tissue between the epidermis, is differentiated into palisade parenchyma (elongated, tightly packed cells with many chloroplasts) and spongy parenchyma (loosely arranged cells with large intercellular spaces).

The Vascular Bundles, or veins, are conjoint, collateral, and closed, embedded in the mesophyll. Xylem is towards the upper epidermis, and phloem towards the lower. They are often surrounded by bundle sheath extensions.

For contrast, let's look at an Isobilateral Leaf, common in monocots like maize.

In these leaves, stomata are equally distributed on both surfaces, making them amphistomatic. The mesophyll is not differentiated into palisade and spongy layers; instead, it's a uniform mass of chlorenchymatous cells.

And some cells of the upper epidermis are modified into bulliform cells, or motor cells, which help in leaf rolling during dry conditions.

The vascular bundles are also conjoint, collateral, and closed, with xylem towards the upper side and phloem towards the lower. They are surrounded by bundle sheaths, and sclerenchymatous patches provide mechanical support.

We've now covered the internal structures of roots, stems, and leaves for both dicots and monocots. A key concept we've touched upon is secondary growth.

Secondary growth is the increase in thickness or girth of plant axes due to the activity of secondary meristems like cambium and cork cambium. It's typically found in dicot stems and roots but absent in monocots.

In dicot stems, the first step is the formation of a Cambium Ring, where interfascicular cambium (from medullary rays) joins with intrafascicular cambium (within vascular bundles).

This cambium ring then produces Secondary Vascular Tissues. Secondary phloem is formed on the outer side, and secondary xylem, commonly known as wood, is formed on the inner side. The primary phloem gets crushed.

The cambium also forms Vascular Rays, which are parenchymatous cells extending radially, involved in radial translocation.

A fascinating aspect of secondary growth is the formation of Annual Rings. The activity of the vascular cambium varies with seasons, leading to the formation of spring wood (wider vessels, early summer) and autumn wood (narrower vessels, late summer/autumn). Counting these rings helps determine the age of a tree.

In older stems, we see Sapwood (outer, lighter, living, functional) and Heartwood (inner, darker, dead, structural support). Heartwood contains antiseptic compounds and tyloses, making it more durable.

As the stem increases in girth, the epidermis ruptures, and a new protective layer, the Periderm, is formed by the Cork Cambium (phellogen).

Phellogen produces cork (phellem) on the outside and secondary cortex (phelloderm) on the inside. Cork cells are dead, lignified, and suberized, making them impermeable and protective.

All tissues outside the vascular cambium constitute the Bark, which can be ring bark or scale bark.

And Lenticels are raised pores in the bark that facilitate gaseous exchange. They are formed by complementary cells.
