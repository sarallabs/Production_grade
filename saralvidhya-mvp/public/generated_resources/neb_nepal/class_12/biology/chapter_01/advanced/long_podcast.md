Absolutely, we owe a lot to N. Grew, often called the Father of Plant Anatomy. He not only coined the term 'tissue' but also laid the groundwork for understanding the internal structure of plants.

And that's precisely what plant anatomy is all about, isn't it? The study of the internal structure of plants. A tissue, as N. Grew defined it, is a group of cells with a common origin, performing specific functions.

In higher plants like angiosperms and gymnosperms, these tissues are incredibly specialized, showing a clear division of labor. And the key to classifying these tissues lies in their ability to divide.

Right. Based on their dividing capacity, tissues are broadly categorized into two main types: Meristematic tissue and Permanent tissue. Let's dive into meristematic tissue first.

The term 'meristem' itself comes from the Greek word 'meristos,' meaning divisible. And that perfectly describes meristematic tissue: a group of immature cells with the capacity to divide.

C. Nageli coined the term 'meristem' in 1858. These cells are characterized by being small, thin-walled, and living. Their cell walls are elastic and cellulosic, usually lacking a secondary wall.

They're also compactly arranged, meaning no intercellular spaces. Their shapes are typically isodiametric, spherical, oval, or polygonal. Inside, you'll find dense cytoplasm, a large, distinct nucleus, and either no vacuoles or very small ones.

And plastids are present as proplastids. Essentially, meristematic cells are the parent cells, the origin point for all new cells added to the plant body.

Now, meristems can be classified in a few ways. Let's start with classification based on origin and development. We have three types here: Promeristem, Primary Meristem, and Secondary Meristem.

Promeristem is the earliest embryonic meristematic cells in a growing organ, found at the tips of the stem and root. It gives rise to the primary meristem.

Primary meristem, derived from the promeristem, is found at the tips of roots, stems, and appendages. These retain their division power throughout the plant's life, dividing in all planes to form the primary structure of the plant body. Even the vascular cambium of dicot stems, which divides in one plane to form secondary permanent tissues, is a type of primary meristem.

Then we have secondary meristems. These arise when some primary permanent tissues regain their meristematic capacity. They are always lateral and develop to heal wounds or facilitate secondary growth. Examples include the vascular cambium of roots, interfascicular cambium of stems, and cork cambium.

And lateral meristems, found along the sides of roots and stems, are responsible for increasing the plant's thickness. Vascular cambium and cork cambium are prime examples.

The third classification is based on function, proposed by Haberlandt in 1914. This gives us Protoderm, Procambium, and Ground Meristem.

Let's zoom in on the shoot apex. It's a dome-shaped mass of cells covered by young leaves, responsible for differentiating into organs like leaves and buds, and driving stem growth.

Then there's the Histogen Theory by Hanstein, which proposes differentiation into three regions: Dermatogen (outermost, forming epidermis), Periblem (middle, forming cortex), and Plerome (central, forming the stele). However, further studies showed this isn't always strictly applicable.

Tunica is the outer zone, comprising one or more peripheral layers. Its outer layer forms the epidermis, while others contribute to leaf primordia and hypodermal cells. Corpus is the central, undifferentiated mass of cells, dividing irregularly and forming the cortex, vascular tissue, and pith.

Now, let's consider the root apex. It's unique because it's enclosed by a root cap, or calyptra, protecting the meristem as the root grows through the soil. This makes the root apex subterminal, not terminal.

And unlike the shoot apex, the root apex isn't involved in forming lateral appendages. Those form further behind. A remarkable feature of the root apex is the 'quiescent center' – an area of slow-dividing cells between the root cap and the active meristematic region.

This quiescent center acts as a reservoir of cells, becoming active if the primary initials are damaged. It’s a critical backup system. The root apex also shows the three meristematic regions: Protoderm, Procambium, and Ground Meristem, just like the shoot.

With that foundational understanding of meristematic tissues, we can now move on to Permanent Tissues. These are cells that have lost their dividing capacity and have attained a definite shape, size, and function.

Permanent tissues can be living or dead, thin-walled or thick-walled. They are broadly classified into three types: Simple Permanent Tissue, Complex Permanent Tissue, and Special or Secretory Tissue.

Let's start with Simple Permanent Tissues, which are composed of only one type of cell and are homogenous. These are Parenchyma, Collenchyma, and Sclerenchyma.

Parenchyma, a term given by Green, consists of living cells. They are typically isodiametric but can vary in shape – spherical, oval, rectangular, polygonal, or elongated. Their cell walls are thin, primary, and made of cellulose, hemicellulose, and calcium pectate.

Parenchyma cells are usually loosely arranged, with intercellular spaces. Each cell has a vacuolated cytoplasm and a distinct nucleus. They are abundant in soft plant parts like pith, mesophyll of leaves, fruit pulp, endosperm, and cortex.

Parenchyma's primary function is food storage, but it has specialized roles. Parenchyma with chloroplasts is called chlorenchyma, performing photosynthesis. In aquatic plants, large air spaces in parenchyma form aerenchyma, providing buoyancy.

Succulent xerophytes use parenchyma for water storage. Special types, called idioblasts, produce and store tannins, oils, or crystals. Parenchyma in xylem and phloem, called xylem parenchyma and phloem parenchyma respectively, aids in conduction.

Elongated, thick-walled parenchyma cells are called prosenchyma, providing mechanical support.

Next is Collenchyma. These are living tissues with somewhat elongated cells. Their defining feature is additional cellulose and pectin thickening in their walls.

In cross-section, they appear circular, oval, or polygonal. The cell wall is thick, primary, and non-lignified, composed of cellulose, hemicellulose, and pectic materials, with a high water content. They have simple pits.

Collenchyma is similar to parenchyma but with thicker walls and longer cells. Based on thickening and arrangement, they are classified into Angular, Lacunar, and Plate or Lamellar collenchyma.

Angular collenchyma has thickenings at the corners. Lacunar collenchyma has thickenings bordering intercellular spaces. Plate or lamellar collenchyma has thickenings on tangential walls.

Collenchyma is mainly found in the hypodermis of stems, petioles, and leaves of dicots, providing support, flexibility, and elasticity, especially to growing organs. It can also perform photosynthesis if it contains chloroplasts.

Finally, for simple permanent tissues, we have Sclerenchyma, derived from 'scleros' meaning hard. These tissues have little to no protoplasm at maturity. Their cell walls are highly thick and often lignified, with pits.

At maturity, they are dead, as lignin makes the walls impermeable. Sclerenchyma's primary role is mechanical – providing strength and rigidity to withstand strains. They are classified into Fibres and Sclereids.

Fibres are much elongated, narrow, thick-walled cells with pointed ends, offering great strength and flexibility. They are typically dead at maturity, with a reduced lumen. Bast fibres (from pericycle and phloem) and wood fibres (from xylem) are the two types.

Sclereids, also called stone cells, are shorter, more irregularly shaped, and broader cells. They are common in fruits, seeds, and barks, providing hardness and strength, like in the stony fruits of almonds or the seed coat of dates.

Sclereids are further categorized into Brachysclereids (stone cells), Macrosclereids (columnar), Osteosclereids (bone-shaped), Astrosclereids (star-shaped), and Trichosclereids (hair-like).

So, to recap simple permanent tissues: Parenchyma for storage and photosynthesis, Collenchyma for flexible support in growing regions, and Sclerenchyma for rigid mechanical strength.

Now, let's move to Complex Permanent Tissues. Unlike simple tissues, these are composed of different types of cells working together as a unit. The primary complex tissues are Xylem and Phloem, often called vascular tissues.

Xylem, or wood, is the conducting tissue for water and minerals from the roots. It consists of four types of cells: Tracheids, Vessels, Xylem fibres, and Xylem parenchyma. All except xylem parenchyma are dead.

Tracheids are elongated, tube-like dead cells with thick, lignified walls and tapering ends. They are the most primitive conducting elements. Vessels, or tracheae, are long, cylindrical, tube-like structures made of multiple cells joined end to end, with perforated or dissolved end walls, forming continuous water pipes.

Both tracheids and vessels show various types of thickenings: annular, spiral, reticulate, scalariform, and pitted. Vessels are more advanced and found in most angiosperms, while ferns and gymnosperms rely solely on tracheids.

Xylem fibres are sclerenchymatous cells providing mechanical strength. Xylem parenchyma, the only living component, stores food and aids in lateral conduction.

The first formed xylem is protoxylem, characterized by annular, spiral, and rarely scalariform thickenings. Later formed xylem is metaxylem, with reticulate, scalariform, or pitted thickenings and wider vessels.

Phloem, or bast, is the tissue responsible for transporting food materials from leaves. It's also a complex tissue, composed of Sieve-tubes, Companion cells, Phloem parenchyma, and Phloem fibres. All except phloem fibres are living.

Sieve-tubes are formed by rows of cells with sieve plates on their end walls, allowing food passage. They are living but lack a nucleus at maturity. Companion cells are closely associated with sieve tubes, controlling material passage and providing energy.

Phloem parenchyma cells store food, and phloem fibres, which are sclerenchymatous, provide strength. Companion cells are unique to angiosperms; pteridophytes and gymnosperms have albuminous cells instead.

The Vascular Tissue System is comprised of vascular bundles, containing xylem and phloem, often with cambium. In dicot stems, these bundles are arranged in rings, while in monocot stems, they are scattered.

Vascular bundles can be radial (xylem and phloem on different radii, alternating – typical of roots) or conjoint (xylem and phloem on the same radius). Conjoint bundles can be collateral (phloem on one side of xylem), bicollateral (phloem on both sides of xylem – found in Cucurbita), or concentric (one tissue encircles the other – amphivasal or amphicribal).

A key distinction in xylem arrangement is exarch (protoxylem towards the periphery, metaxylem towards the center – in roots) and endarch (protoxylem towards the center, metaxylem towards the periphery – in stems). Mesarch is found in the rachis and rhizome of some ferns.

Lastly, we have Special or Secretory Tissues, responsible for secreting gums, resins, oils, latex, and nectar. These are divided into Laticiferous tissue and Glandular tissue.

Laticiferous tissues contain specialized parenchymatous cells that secrete latex. These can be latex vessels (articulate, formed by fusion) or latex cells (non-articulate, independent).

Glandular tissues are organized into glands. Internal glandular tissues include oil glands and glands secreting resins and gums. External ones include digestive glands in insectivorous plants, nectaries for pollination, glandular hairs, and hydathodes for water secretion.

Hydathodes are particularly interesting, as they lead to guttation – the loss of water in liquid form, often containing dissolved substances.

With that broad overview of tissues, let's now delve into the internal structure of specific plant parts: Dicot and Monocot Roots, Stems, and Leaves.

Starting with the Dicot Root. A transverse section reveals the Epidermis (or Epiblema), which lacks cuticle and stomata but bears unicellular root hairs for absorption.

Following that is the well-developed Cortex, composed of thin-walled parenchymatous cells with intercellular spaces, storing starch. Then comes the Endodermis, the innermost cortical layer, characterized by Casparian strips – thickenings of suberin and lignin on radial and tangential walls.

Some endodermal cells, called passage cells, remain unthickened opposite the protoxylem, facilitating water diffusion. Internal to the endodermis is the Pericycle, where lateral roots originate.

The vascular bundles in a dicot root are radial, with xylem and phloem alternating, typically two to six bundles. The xylem is exarch. Conjunctive tissue separates the xylem and phloem, and the pith is usually absent or reduced.

Monocot roots share similarities but have key differences. They have a well-developed pith and a larger number of vascular bundles, usually 8 to 20. Secondary growth is absent.

The Epidermis, Cortex, Endodermis with Casparian strips, and Pericycle function similarly. However, the vascular bundles are numerous (8-20 or more) and exarch.

Moving to stems, let's examine the Dicot Stem, using the sunflower stem as an example. The outermost layer is the Epidermis, covered by cuticle and often bearing multicellular hairs.

Beneath the epidermis is the Hypodermis, providing mechanical strength, followed by the Cortex, composed of loosely arranged parenchymatous cells. The Endodermis, often indistinct, is the innermost cortical layer, sometimes called the starch sheath.

The Pericycle, a few-celled thick layer, consists of alternating parenchyma and sclerenchyma patches. The Vascular Bundles are arranged in a ring, conjoint, collateral, and open, with cambium between xylem and phloem. Xylem is endarch.

Medullary rays, parenchymatous cells between vascular bundles, facilitate radial transport. The central Pith is well-developed and involved in food storage.

In contrast, the Monocot Stem (like maize) has a different arrangement. The Epidermis is covered by cuticle, and the Hypodermis is sclerenchymatous, providing mechanical support.

The Ground Tissue is undifferentiated, without distinct cortex, endodermis, or pith. Vascular bundles are scattered, conjoint, collateral, and closed, each surrounded by a sclerenchymatous bundle sheath.

The xylem in monocot stems is typically Y-shaped, with a lysigenous cavity below the protoxylem, formed by the disintegration of elements, which stores water.

Now, let's look at leaves. Dicot leaves, or dorsiventral leaves, show distinct upper and lower surfaces. The Epidermis, covered by cuticle, contains stomata, mostly on the lower surface (hypostomatic).

The Mesophyll is differentiated into Palisade parenchyma (elongated, tightly packed cells with many chloroplasts) and Spongy parenchyma (loosely arranged cells with large intercellular spaces).

Vascular bundles in leaves are conjoint, collateral, and closed, embedded in the mesophyll and surrounded by a bundle sheath.

Monocot leaves, or isobilateral leaves, have stomata equally distributed on both surfaces (amphistomatic). The mesophyll is not differentiated into palisade and spongy layers. Bulliform cells, large thin-walled cells, are also present, helping in leaf rolling.

Vascular bundles in monocot leaves are conjoint, collateral, and closed, arranged in parallel series, with xylem towards the upper side and phloem towards the lower.

Finally, let's discuss Secondary Growth in Dicot Stems. This is the increase in girth due to the activity of cambium and cork-cambium.

It begins with the formation of a Cambium Ring, where intrafascicular cambium (within vascular bundles) and interfascicular cambium (between vascular bundles, from medullary rays) join.

This ring produces secondary vascular tissues: Secondary Phloem on the outside and Secondary Xylem (wood) on the inside. The secondary xylem forms the bulk of the woody trunk.

Vascular rays, formed by ray initials, facilitate radial transport. The varying activity of the vascular cambium due to seasonal changes leads to the formation of Annual Rings, composed of spring wood (wider vessels) and autumn wood (narrower vessels).

In older stems, sapwood (living, conducting) is distinguished from heartwood (dead, non-conducting but provides strength and resistance to decay).

As the stem grows thicker, the epidermis ruptures, and a new protective layer, the Periderm, is formed by the Cork-Cambium (phellogen). Phellogen produces cork (phellem) on the outside and secondary cortex (phelloderm) on the inside.

The Bark comprises all tissues outside the vascular cambium, including secondary phloem and periderm. Lenticels, raised pores in the periderm, allow gaseous exchange.

The process of becoming heartwood is quite interesting, too. The tree deposits various substances like tannins, resins, and oils into these central cells. This not only gives heartwood its characteristic darker color but also acts as a defense mechanism, making the wood much more resistant to fungal attacks and decay.

It’s also how we get those beautiful annual rings. Because the vascular cambium's activity fluctuates with the seasons—producing larger, lighter cells in the spring and smaller, denser cells in the fall—we see those distinct layers that help us determine the tree's age.
