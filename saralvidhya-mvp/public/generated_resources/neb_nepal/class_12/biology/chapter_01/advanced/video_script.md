**Scene 1**

*   **Duration:** 0:15
*   **Visual Prompt:** Cinematic macro-to-micro transition across leaf and stem tissues. Overlay: "Plant Anatomy — tissues, meristems, and theories." Subtle on-screen text: "N. Grew — Father of Plant Anatomy; coined 'tissue'."
*   **Narration:** Plant anatomy explains how cells are organized into **tissues** and how tissues enable growth and specialization. **N. Grew**, often called the Father of Plant Anatomy, also **coined the term tissue**—a classic exam anchor linking history to structure.

**Scene 2**

*   **Duration:** 0:30
*   **Visual Prompt:** Split animation: left, meristematic cells (small, dense cytoplasm, minimal vacuole, thin walls, no intercellular spaces); right, permanent cells (larger vacuole, specialized walls). Overlay: "Meristematic vs Permanent — key discriminator: ability to divide."
*   **Narration:** Plants organize growth using **meristematic tissue**—immature, actively dividing cells with thin primary walls, dense cytoplasm, prominent nuclei, and small or absent vacuoles. **Permanent tissues** arise by **differentiation**: they lose division capacity and acquire specialized roles—this contrast is the conceptual spine of the chapter.

**Scene 3**

*   **Duration:** 0:45
*   **Visual Prompt:** Three parallel classification wheels labeled **Origin**, **Position**, **Function**. Animate Promeristem → Primary → Secondary; Apical / Intercalary / Lateral; Protoderm / Procambium / Ground meristem with arrows to epidermis, vascular tissues, and ground tissues.
*   **Narration:** Meristems are classified by **origin** (promeristem to primary meristems; secondary meristems arise from dedifferentiation), by **position** (apical for length, intercalary for localized elongation—think grasses, **intercalary is considered a detached portion of apical meristem**), and by **function**—**protoderm**, **procambium**, and **ground meristem** mapping to the fundamental tissue systems.

**Scene 4**

*   **Duration:** 1:00
*   **Visual Prompt:** Animated shoot apex dome covered by leaf primordia; label **Tunica** (outer layers) vs **Corpus** (central mass). Show arrows: tunica divisions mostly **anticlinal** (perpendicular to surface); corpus divisions **irregular** / multiple planes. Overlay: "Schmidt — Tunica–Corpus (widely accepted for angiosperm shoot apex)."
*   **Narration:** For the **shoot apex**, **Schmidt's Tunica–Corpus theory** is the standard advanced framing: the **tunica** contributes to surface growth and leaf primordia and divides mainly **anticlinally**, preserving layered organization, while the **corpus** drives bulk internal contribution—**cortex, vascular tissues, and pith**—with more irregular divisions.

---

**Scene 5**

*   **Duration:** 1:15
*   **Visual Prompt:** Root tip longitudinal section: **root cap** ahead, meristem behind; emphasize **subterminal** position of the meristem because the cap covers the tip. Highlight **quiescent centre** as a low-activity central zone. Overlay: "Root branches — **endogenous** origin."
*   **Narration:** The **root apex** is **subterminal**—protected by the **root cap (calyptra)**, formed from **calyptrogen** in the classical root-apex vocabulary. A high-yield differentiator is the **quiescent centre**: a region of **slowly dividing cells** acting as a **reservoir of initials** and participating in meristem regulation after damage. Root lateral roots arise **endogenously** from internal tissues—contrast with **exogenous** shoot branching.

**Scene 6**

*   **Duration:** 1:30
*   **Visual Prompt:** Side-by-side diagrams: **Hanstein Histogen** for root (Dermatogen → epidermis; Periblem → cortex; Plerome → stele) vs **Tunica–Corpus** shoot schematic. Brief text: "Avoid over-generalizing older theories across all plant groups."
*   **Narration:** At the expert level, you should be able to **compare theories without collapsing them**: **Histogen regions** help describe root apical zoning, while **Tunica–Corpus** targets **shoot** organization. Exam traps include applying **apical cell theory** (Nageli; lower-plant emphasis) or Histogen diagrams rigidly to every angiosperm context—use the textbook's **most accepted** model for shoots: **Tunica–Corpus**.

**Scene 7**

*   **Duration:** 1:45
*   **Visual Prompt:** Three tissue panels: parenchyma (thin walls, living; storage/photosynthesis cues), collenchyma (uneven **primary** wall thickenings at corners; flexible support), sclerenchyma (thick **lignified** secondary walls; fibers vs sclereids). Flash label: "Sclerenchyma often **dead at maturity** — function-linked."
*   **Narration:** **Simple permanent tissues** are single-type systems with different engineering trade-offs: **parenchyma** is metabolically versatile with thin walls; **collenchyma** provides **flexible support** via uneven primary-wall thickening in young regions; **sclerenchyma** provides **rigid** support using lignified secondary walls and is **often dead at maturity**—a favorite conceptual distinction.

---

**Scene 8**

*   **Duration:** 2:00
*   **Visual Prompt:** Stem cross-section animation: **vascular cambium** and **cork cambium** as lateral meristems increasing girth. Short on-screen caveat: "**Not every lateral meristem story is identical in origin** — vascular cambium can be primary or secondary depending on development."
*   **Narration:** **Secondary growth** increases girth via **lateral meristems**: **vascular cambium** adds secondary xylem and phloem, while **cork cambium** builds periderm-related protective layers. Advanced students should note the common misconception that "**all lateral meristems are secondary**": **vascular cambium** may continue from **procambium** (primary continuity) or arise later from **dedifferentiation**, whereas **cork cambium** is typically treated as a **secondary meristem**.
