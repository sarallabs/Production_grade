## Question 1

According to Schmidt's **Tunica–Corpus** theory of the angiosperm shoot apex, which division pattern BEST explains why the tunica maintains a discrete layered organization while contributing to surface growth?

A. The tunica divides mainly by **periclinal** divisions, increasing radial thickness of each layer
B. The tunica divides mainly by **anticlinal** divisions, expanding the surface area without adding new superficial layers from within
C. The corpus divides only by **anticlinal** divisions, forming the epidermis
D. The corpus is derived entirely from the calyptrogen at the root apex

**Correct Answer: B**

**Explanation:** In the Tunica–Corpus model, the **tunica** is described as dividing predominantly by **anticlinal** (perpendicular to the surface) divisions, which enlarge the shoot apex surface while preserving the layered arrangement. **Periclinal** divisions (parallel to the surface) would add layers from within and are characteristic of how the **corpus** contributes bulk growth through more irregular divisions. The corpus forms internal tissues (cortex, vascular tissues, pith), not the epidermis as a primary tunica alternative in this framing. **Calyptrogen** belongs to **root** apex organization (root cap formation), not shoot tunica.

## Question 2

In a typical dicot root apex, the **quiescent centre** is significant because it:

A. Contains the most rapidly dividing initials that exclusively produce the root cap
B. Is a region of **slowly dividing** cells that acts as a reservoir of initials and can respond to damage by altering division activity
C. Is identical to the **protoderm** in functional classification
D. Secretes suberin to prevent water loss from the root tip

**Correct Answer: B**

**Explanation:** The **quiescent centre** is a hallmark of many root apices: it is a central zone of **relatively low mitotic activity** compared with surrounding initials, often interpreted as a **buffer/reservoir** that helps maintain meristem stability and can modulate regeneration after injury. It is **not** simply "the fastest dividing" region. **Protoderm** is a **primary meristem** category based on **function** (epidermis precursor), not the quiescent centre. Suberin-related barriers are associated with mature/exodermal/peridermal tissues, not the quiescent centre concept.

## Question 3

Hanstein's **Histogen** theory partitions the root apical meristem into dermatogen, periblem, and plerome. Which derivative pairing is MOST accurate?

A. **Dermatogen** → stele (vascular cylinder); **Plerome** → epidermis
B. **Periblem** → cortex; **Plerome** → central stele (including vascular tissues and associated core tissues as described in the theory)
C. **Plerome** → root cap; **Dermatogen** → cortex
D. **Periblem** → leaf primordia; **Dermatogen** → pith only

**Correct Answer: B**

**Explanation:** In the Histogen scheme for roots, **dermatogen** forms the **epidermis**, **periblem** forms the **cortex**, and **plerome** forms the **central stele** region (vascular tissues, pericycle, pith as applicable in the textbook framing). Root cap formation is commonly linked to **calyptrogen** in many texts (including the terminology used alongside root apex descriptions in your chapter notes), not the plerome. Leaf primordia are shoot-side organogenesis contexts, not the standard Histogen root mapping.

## Question 4

A microscopy instructor shows three permanent tissue mounts: (i) thin walls, living, abundant intercellular spaces; (ii) living, elongated cells with **uneven** thickening of **primary** walls at corners; (iii) thick **lignified** secondary walls, often dead at functional maturity, providing rigid support. Which identification sequence is BEST?

A. (i) Collenchyma, (ii) Parenchyma, (iii) Meristematic tissue
B. (i) Sclerenchyma, (ii) Parenchyma, (iii) Collenchyma
C. (i) Parenchyma, (ii) Collenchyma, (iii) Sclerenchyma
D. (i) Collenchyma, (ii) Sclerenchyma, (iii) Parenchyma

**Correct Answer: C**

**Explanation:** **Parenchyma** is typically thin-walled, living, and commonly shows intercellular spaces (depending on organ/tissue arrangement), serving storage/photosynthesis/secretion roles. **Collenchyma** provides flexible support via **localized, uneven thickening of primary walls**, commonly at corners in elongated living cells. **Sclerenchyma** is characterized by **strong, often lignified secondary walls** and is frequently **dead at maturity**, providing rigid mechanical strength (fibers/sclereids). Meristematic tissue would not match mount (iii).

## Question 5

Which statement BEST resolves the common misconception: "**All lateral meristems are secondary meristems**"?

A. It is correct because lateral position always implies dedifferentiation from permanent tissues
B. It is incorrect because **vascular cambium** may be **primary** (procambial continuity) or **secondary** (arising from dedifferentiation), while **cork cambium** is typically described as a secondary meristem
C. It is incorrect because intercalary meristems are lateral meristems located at shoot tips
D. It is correct because apical meristems are also lateral meristems

**Correct Answer: B**

**Explanation:** **Lateral** refers to position (along the sides of stem/root) and functionally includes **vascular cambium** and **cork cambium**. However, not every lateral meristem is automatically "secondary" in the developmental sense: **vascular cambium** can be interpreted as **primary** when it continues from **procambium** or as **secondary** when it arises later from dedifferentiated permanent tissues (depending on developmental origin). **Cork cambium** is commonly treated as a **secondary meristem**. **Intercalary** meristems are not "lateral" in this classification; **apical** meristems are not lateral meristems.
