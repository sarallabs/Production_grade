It's amazing to think that the study of plant anatomy, the internal structure of plants, has such a long history. Did you know that N. Grew is considered the Father of Plant Anatomy? He even coined the term "tissue."

That's right. And a tissue, in simple terms, is a group of cells that share a common origin and work together to perform specific functions. In higher plants, these tissues are quite specialized, leading to a division of labor.

Exactly. And depending on their ability to divide, plant tissues are broadly categorized into two main types: meristematic and permanent.

Let's start with meristematic tissue. The term "meristem" itself comes from a Greek word meaning "divisible," which perfectly describes these cells.

Meristematic tissues are essentially immature cells with a high capacity for division. They are typically small, thin-walled, and living. Their cell walls are elastic and cellulosic, and they lack intercellular spaces, meaning they are packed very tightly together.

They usually have a spherical, oval, or polygonal shape, with dense cytoplasm, a large nucleus, and very small or absent vacuoles. Interestingly, they contain proplastids, which are precursors to other plastids. These meristematic cells are the parent cells from which all new plant cells originate.

Now, meristems can be classified in a few ways. One important classification is based on their origin and development.

We have Promeristem, which are the earliest embryonic meristematic cells found at the tips of growing organs like the root and stem. They give rise to primary meristems.

Then there's Primary Meristem, derived from promeristem. These are found at the tips of roots, stems, and appendages, and crucially, they retain their ability to divide throughout the plant's life, forming the primary structure of the plant body. Even the vascular cambium in dicot stems can be considered a type of primary meristem, though it divides in a specific plane to form secondary permanent tissues.

And finally, Secondary Meristem. These arise when some primary permanent tissues regain their meristematic capacity. They are always lateral and help in wound healing or secondary growth. Examples include vascular cambium in roots and interfascicular cambium in stems.

Another way to classify meristems is by their position in the plant.

Then Intercalary Meristems, found between regions of permanent tissues, like at the base of leaves in some plants or at the base of internodes in grasses. They also contribute to elongation.

And Lateral Meristems, situated along the sides of roots and stems, which are responsible for increasing the plant's thickness, like the vascular cambium and cork cambium.

A third classification is based on function. Here, we have Protoderm, the outermost layer that forms the epidermis.

Procambium, located inside the protoderm, which differentiates into primary vascular tissues.

It's fascinating how these different meristem types are organized. For instance, the shoot apex is a dome-shaped mass of cells that differentiates into organs like leaves and buds.

However, the Tunica-Corpus Theory by Schmidt is more widely accepted for shoot apex organization. It describes two distinct zones: the tunica, which is the outer layer(s) responsible for the epidermis and leaf primordia, and the corpus, the central mass of cells that forms the cortex, vascular tissue, and pith.

This quiescent center is crucial for root regeneration. And just like the shoot apex, the root apex also has protoderm, procambium, and ground meristem, which differentiate into specific tissues.

Moving on from meristematic tissues, we come to Permanent Tissues. These are cells that have lost their dividing capacity and have attained a definite shape, size, and function. They can be living or dead, thin-walled or thick-walled.

Permanent tissues are broadly divided into three types: Simple, Complex, and Special or Secretory Tissues.

Let's start with Simple Permanent Tissues, which are composed of only one type of cell. These are Parenchyma, Collenchyma, and Sclerenchyma.

Parenchyma, a term given by Green, consists of living cells that are typically isodiametric but can vary in shape. They have thin primary cell walls, are usually loosely arranged with intercellular spaces, and contain a distinct nucleus and vacuolated cytoplasm.

Parenchyma is abundant in soft plant parts like the pith, mesophyll of leaves, and fruit pulp. Its primary function is food storage, but it has specialized roles. For instance, parenchyma with chloroplasts is called chlorenchyma and performs photosynthesis.

Parenchyma with large air spaces in aquatic plants is called aerenchyma, providing buoyancy. In succulents, it stores water. Special parenchyma cells called idioblasts can store tannins or oils.

And in xylem and phloem, parenchyma cells are involved in conduction. Elongated, thick-walled parenchyma cells are known as prosenchyma, providing mechanical support.

Next, we have Collenchyma. These are living, elongated cells with additional cellulose and pectin thickenings in their walls, particularly at the corners, making them angular collenchyma. They can also have thickenings bordering intercellular spaces (lacunar collenchyma) or on tangential walls (plate or lamellar collenchyma).

Collenchyma is a strengthening tissue, providing flexibility and elasticity to growing organs. It prevents tearing of leaves and, if it contains chloroplasts, can photosynthesize.

Sclerenchyma, derived from "scleros" meaning hard, are tissues with little or no protoplasm at maturity. Their cell walls are thick and often lignified, making them dead. They provide strength and rigidity.

Sclerenchyma is classified into two main types: Fibres and Sclereids. Fibres are elongated, narrow, thick-walled cells with pointed ends, providing great strength and flexibility. They are used in textiles.

Bast fibres are found in the pericycle and phloem, while wood fibres are in the xylem. Sclereids, often called stone cells, are shorter, more irregularly shaped cells. They are common in fruits and seeds, providing hardness and protection.

Sclereids further subdivide into brachysclereids, macrosclereids, osteosclereids, astrosclereids, and trichosclereids, each with distinct shapes and locations.

Now, let's move to Complex Permanent Tissues, which are made up of different types of cells working as a unit. The main examples are Xylem and Phloem, collectively known as vascular tissues.

Xylem, or wood, is responsible for conducting water and minerals from the roots upwards. It consists of four types of cells: tracheids, vessels, xylem fibres, and xylem parenchyma.

Tracheids and vessels are the main conducting elements. Tracheids are elongated, tube-like dead cells with lignified walls and tapering ends. Vessels are longer, cylindrical structures formed from a series of cells with perforated end walls, essentially forming water pipes. Both have various types of wall thickenings like annular, spiral, and pitted.

Xylem fibres are sclerenchymatous cells that provide mechanical strength, while xylem parenchyma are living cells that store food and aid in lateral conduction.

We also distinguish between protoxylem (first formed xylem) and metaxylem (later formed xylem), differing in vessel size and thickening patterns.

Phloem, or bast, transports food materials from leaves to other parts of the plant. It's also a complex tissue composed of sieve-tubes, companion cells, phloem parenchyma, and phloem fibres.

Sieve-tubes are elongated, living cells arranged end-to-end, with sieve plates on their end walls for food transport. Companion cells are closely associated with sieve tubes, providing them with energy and controlling material passage.

Phloem parenchyma stores food, and phloem fibres, which are sclerenchymatous, provide mechanical support. Unlike xylem, most phloem cells are living.

The vascular tissue system forms vascular bundles, which can be arranged in different ways. In dicot stems, they form rings, while in monocot stems, they are scattered.

Vascular bundles can be radial, where xylem and phloem alternate on different radii, characteristic of roots. Or they can be conjoint, where xylem and phloem are on the same radius.

Conjoint bundles can be collateral, with xylem and phloem side-by-side, or bicollateral, with phloem on both sides of the xylem, found in plants like Cucurbita. Concentric bundles have one tissue surrounding the other, like amphivasal (phloem in center, xylem surrounds) or amphicribal (xylem in center, phloem surrounds).

The arrangement of protoxylem and metaxylem also defines the xylem's condition: exarch in roots (protoxylem towards periphery) and endarch in stems (protoxylem towards center). Mesarch is found in some ferns.

Finally, we have Special or Secretory Tissues, responsible for secreting substances like gums, resins, and latex.

Laticiferous tissue contains latex vessels or latex cells that secrete latex, which can be milky, watery, or colored. Glandular tissues form glands that secrete essential oils, resins, nectar, or digestive enzymes.

Hydathodes are specialized pores that exude water in liquid form, a process called guttation.
