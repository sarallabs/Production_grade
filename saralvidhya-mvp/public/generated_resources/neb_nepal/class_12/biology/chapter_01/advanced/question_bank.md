**Section 3: High-Difficulty Long Answer Questions**

1.  **Answer:**
    *   **Apical Cell Theory (Nageli):** Proposed a single apical cell responsible for all growth in algae, bryophytes, and pteridophytes.
        *   **Strengths:** Simple, explained growth in simpler plants.
        *   **Limitations:** Not applicable to all plants, especially angiosperms where meristems are multicellular.
    *   **Histogen Theory (Hanstein):** Divided the root and shoot apices into three distinct regions: Dermatogen (epidermis), Periblem (cortex), and Plerome (stele).
        *   **Strengths:** Introduced distinct zones of meristematic activity.
        *   **Limitations:** Strict delineation of these layers is not always clear in higher plants; difficult to distinguish in gymnosperms and angiosperms; assumed a fixed origin for tissues.
    *   **Tunica-Corpus Theory (Schmidt):** Divided the shoot apex into two zones: Tunica (peripheral, anticlinal divisions, surface growth, leaf primordia) and Corpus (central, anticlinal and periclinal divisions, bulk growth, vascular tissues, pith).
        *   **Strengths:** More accurately describes the multicellular nature of angiosperm shoot apices. Explains the origin of leaf primordia and stem tissues more precisely. Accounts for variations in tunica layers between monocots and dicots.
        *   **Limitations:** Primarily focused on shoot apex organization; root apex organization has different complexities.
    *   **Comprehensiveness:** The Tunica-Corpus theory is more comprehensive for angiosperms because it reflects the complex, multicellular organization and the differential division patterns that lead to the formation of specific tissues and organs, a more accurate depiction than a single apical cell or strictly defined histogen layers.

2.  **Answer:**
    *   **Promeristem:**
        *   **Origin:** Earliest embryonic meristematic cells.
        *   **Location:** Small area at the tip of stem and root.
        *   **Gives rise to:** Primary meristem.
    *   **Primary Meristem:**
        *   **Origin:** Derived from promeristem.
        *   **Location:** Tips of root, stem, and appendages.
        *   **Gives rise to:** Primary tissues forming the **primary structure** of the plant body (e.g., epidermis, cortex, pith, primary vascular tissues). Cells retain their division capacity. Example: Apical meristems of root and shoot, vascular cambium in *early stages* of dicot stem development (initially considered primary).
    *   **Secondary Meristem:**
        *   **Origin:** Dedifferentiation of **primary permanent tissues**.
        *   **Location:** Always lateral (e.g., vascular cambium, cork cambium).
        *   **Gives rise to:** **Secondary permanent tissues**, responsible for **secondary growth** (increase in girth). Examples: Vascular cambium (in dicot roots and stems after initial primary growth), cork cambium.

3.  **Answer:**
    *   **Increase in Length (Primary Growth):** Achieved by **apical meristems** located at the root and shoot tips. These meristems divide to add new cells, causing the plant to grow taller. **Intercalary meristems**, considered separated parts of apical meristems, also contribute to elongation by adding length to specific regions, like internodes in grasses.
    *   **Increase in Thickness (Secondary Growth):** Primarily achieved by **lateral meristems**.
        *   **Vascular Cambium:** Lies between xylem and phloem in stems and roots of dicots. It divides to produce secondary xylem towards the inside (wood) and secondary phloem towards the outside, thus increasing the girth.
        *   **Cork Cambium:** Forms a protective layer on the outside, producing cork cells towards the exterior, contributing to the bark and increasing the overall thickness and protection of older stems and roots.
    *   **Interaction:** Apical meristems establish the initial length. As the plant matures, lateral meristems become active to provide structural support and increased vascular capacity, enabling the plant to support a larger body and survive adverse conditions. Intercalary meristems allow for rapid elongation in specific plant types like grasses.

4.  **Answer:**
    *   **Dermatogen:** Outermost single layer of cells. Forms the **epidermis**. In roots, it merges with periblem and its derivative, the calyptrogen, forms the root cap.
    *   **Periblem:** Middle region of isodiametric cells. Forms the **cortex**.
    *   **Plerome:** Central or inner meristematic region. Forms the **central stele** (vascular tissues, pericycle, pith, medullary rays).
    *   **Limitations:** In many angiosperms and gymnosperms, these distinct layers are not clearly demarcated. The Tunica-Corpus theory provides a more accurate model for shoot apices, showing overlapping functions and dynamic interactions. While the root apex has a cap (calyptra) and meristematic regions, the precise histogen model is less universally applicable.

5.  **Answer:**
    *   **Secondary Growth in Dicot Stem:**
        *   **Vascular Cambium:** Originates from procambium between primary xylem and phloem, and from interfascicular cambium (derived from medullary rays). It divides to produce secondary xylem (wood) inwards and secondary phloem outwards. This increases stem girth and provides structural support and efficient transport.
        *   **Cork Cambium:** Forms from outer cortical cells. It produces cork cells (phellem) outwards (protective, impermeable layer) and phelloderm inwards (parenchymatous cells). These two layers (cork and phelloderm), along with the cork cambium, form the periderm, which replaces the epidermis and protects the stem.
    *   **Secondary Xylem vs. Secondary Phloem:** Secondary xylem (wood) is primarily for support and water transport, while secondary phloem is for sugar transport.
    *   **Absence in Monocots:** Monocots generally lack vascular cambium and cork cambium. Their vascular bundles are closed (no cambium), and they do not exhibit secondary growth. Their increased thickness, if any, is due to the enlargement of existing cells or the formation of a greater number of vascular bundles, not true secondary growth.
