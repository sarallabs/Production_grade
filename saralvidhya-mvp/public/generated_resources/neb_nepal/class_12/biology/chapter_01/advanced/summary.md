"Plant Anatomy," meticulously introduces the fundamental concept of **tissues** as the building blocks of plant structure and function. Coined by N. Grew, the term "tissue" signifies a group of cells originating from a common source, dedicated to specific functions, embodying the principle of **division of labor** crucial in complex organisms. The chapter primarily bifurcates plant tissues into two overarching categories based on their cellular capacity for division: **meristematic** and **permanent**.

**Meristematic tissues**, derived from the Greek "meristos" (divisible), are characterized by actively dividing, immature, thin-walled, living cells. Their key features—compact arrangement, absence of intercellular spaces, dense cytoplasm, prominent nuclei, and rudimentary plastids (proplastids)—underscore their specialized role in generating new cells. These tissues are further classified through multiple lenses:

*   **Origin and Development**: Differentiating between **promeristem** (embryonic source), **primary meristem** (originating from promeristem, responsible for primary growth), and **secondary meristem** (derived from permanent tissues, facilitating secondary growth like thickening).
*   **Position**: Categorizing them into **apical meristems** (at shoot and root tips, extending length), **intercalary meristems** (found between permanent tissues, contributing to elongation, e.g., grasses), and **lateral meristems** (vascular and cork cambium, increasing girth).
*   **Function**: Highlighting **protoderm** (forms epidermis), **procambium** (develops into vascular tissues), and **ground meristem** (differentiates into ground tissue systems).

The **shoot apex**, a vital meristematic region, is presented as the genesis of plant organs and stem elongation, a testament to its dynamic differentiation capabilities. This initial exploration of meristematic tissues lays the groundwork for understanding how plants achieve growth and structural complexity, connecting to broader biological principles of cell differentiation and developmental biology. Its significance lies in establishing the cellular basis for plant architecture and growth, essential for subsequent chapters on plant physiology and development.
