## Section B: Short Answer Questions (2–3 marks each)

**11.** Define Diffusion Pressure Deficit (DPD). What is its value in pure water and in a fully turgid cell?

**Answer:** DPD is the difference between the osmotic pressure and turgor pressure of a cell (DPD = OP − TP). It represents the net driving force for water movement into a cell (suction pressure). DPD of pure water = 0 (no solutes, no osmotic pressure). DPD of a fully turgid cell = 0 (because TP = OP, so OP − TP = 0). In both cases DPD is zero; water enters neither system.

**12.** Distinguish between plasmolysis and deplasmolysis.

**Answer:**
| Feature | Plasmolysis | Deplasmolysis |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | Shrinkage of protoplast away from cell wall | Recovery of protoplast to original position |
| Solution | Hypertonic (exosmosis) | Hypotonic / water (endosmosis) |
| Cell state | Flaccid / shrunken | Turgid / normal |
| TP | Drops to 0 | Increases |

Plasmolysis is used in food preservation; deplasmolysis demonstrates that it is reversible in living cells.

**13.** Why is Root Pressure Theory considered insufficient to explain ascent of sap?

**Answer:** Root pressure is the osmotic hydrostatic pressure built up in roots that can push water upward. Its main limitations:
1. Root pressure is usually only ~2 atm, which can lift water to only ~21 metres.
2. It cannot explain water movement in tall trees (e.g., Sequoia, 90+ m).
3. Root pressure is absent or negligible in gymnosperms.
4. It is a seasonal phenomenon, mainly observed in the rainy season.
Therefore, root pressure alone cannot be the primary mechanism for ascent of sap.

---

**14.** What is transpiration? Name its types and give the percentage of water lost through each.

**Answer:** Transpiration is the loss of water in the form of water vapour from the aerial parts of a plant (leaves, stems, flowers). Types:
1. Stomatal transpiration — ~90% of total water loss; through stomata.
2. Cuticular transpiration — 3–10%; through the waxy cuticle of leaf epidermis.
3. Lenticular transpiration — ~1%; through lenticels in the bark of perennial plants.
Total: 97–99% of water absorbed by plants is lost through transpiration.

**15.** Explain the cobalt chloride paper experiment and what it proves.

**Answer:** Dry cobalt chloride paper is blue; when it absorbs moisture it turns pink. Two pieces of this paper are placed on the upper (adaxial) and lower (abaxial) surfaces of a dorsiventral leaf and sealed with glass slides. The paper on the lower surface turns pink faster than the upper surface. This proves: (a) Transpiration occurs from the leaf surface, and (b) the rate of stomatal transpiration is higher on the lower surface of a dorsiventral (dicot) leaf because most stomata are located there.

**16.** Write a short note on Independent Diffusion. How is it significant for plants?

**Answer:** Independent diffusion means that different substances diffuse simultaneously through the same medium, each according to its own concentration gradient (partial pressure), without interfering with each other. Significance for plants: Through the same stomatal pore at the same time, CO₂ can diffuse inward for photosynthesis, O₂ can diffuse outward (product of photosynthesis), and water vapour can diffuse outward during transpiration. This is possible because each gas responds only to its own partial pressure gradient.

**17.** How do guard cells regulate the opening and closing of stomata?

**Answer:** Guard cells are bean/kidney-shaped (dicots) or dumb-bell shaped (monocots) cells flanking the stomatal pore. Opening: When guard cells gain water (become turgid), they swell; their thicker inner walls (facing the pore) are less elastic, so the cells bow outward, pulling the pore open. Closing: When guard cells lose water (become flaccid), they straighten and the pore closes. The main trigger is K⁺ ion movement: K⁺ enters guard cells in light → lowers water potential → water enters → stomata open. In dark/drought, K⁺ exits → water leaves → stomata close.

**18.** Differentiate between transpiration and guttation.

| Timing | Mainly daytime | Night / early morning |
| Residue left | None | White mineral/salt deposits |
| Stomatal condition | Stomata open | Stomata closed |

## Section C: Long Answer Questions (5 marks each)

**19.** Explain Dixon's Cohesion-Tension Theory of ascent of sap. How does it explain water movement in tall trees?

**Answer:**
Dixon's Cohesion-Tension (C-T) Theory is the most widely accepted explanation for ascent of sap.

**Three key components:**

1. **Continuous water column:** Water fills the xylem vessels/tracheids continuously from roots to leaves, forming an unbroken column.

2. **Cohesion and Adhesion:**
   - *Cohesion* — Strong mutual attraction between water molecules through hydrogen bonds (can withstand up to ~350 atm of tension). This holds the water column together as a single unit.
   - *Adhesion* — Attraction between water molecules and the hydrophilic walls of xylem vessels. This prevents the water column from slipping downward due to gravity.

3. **Transpiration Pull:**
   - Water evaporates from mesophyll cells through stomata.
   - This reduces the water potential at the leaf end of the xylem.
   - A tension/negative pressure is created in the leaf xylem.
   - This tension is transmitted through the continuous water column all the way down to the root xylem.
   - Water is thereby "pulled" upward from roots to leaves.

**How it explains tall trees:** Since cohesion forces can be up to 350 atm, the water column can withstand very high tensions without breaking, allowing water to be pulled to heights of 90+ metres in trees like Sequoia.

**Evidence:** Transpiring twig connected to mercury tube — mercury rises as twig transpires; blocking stomata stops the rise. Stain test and Ringing test confirm xylem as the pathway.

**20.** What is osmosis? Explain endosmosis, exosmosis, plasmolysis, and deplasmolysis with suitable examples.

**Answer:**
**Osmosis:** Passive movement of water molecules through a differentially permeable (semi-permeable) membrane from a region of higher water potential (dilute solution) to a region of lower water potential (concentrated solution). No energy is required.

**Endosmosis:**
When a cell is placed in a hypotonic solution (lower concentration than cell sap), water enters the cell by osmosis. The cell swells and becomes **turgid**. Turgor pressure builds up against the cell wall, and the wall pushes back (wall pressure). Example: A wilted plant cell regains turgidity when watered.

**Exosmosis:**
When a cell is placed in a hypertonic solution (higher concentration than cell sap), water moves out of the cell. The cell shrinks and becomes **flaccid**. Example: Slicing a cucumber and putting it in salt water — it releases water and becomes soft.

**Plasmolysis:**
If exosmosis continues in a hypertonic solution, the protoplasm shrinks so much that it actually pulls away from the rigid cell wall. This separation is called **plasmolysis**. The cell is said to be plasmolysed. Significance: used in food preservation (pickling), testing for living cells.

**Deplasmolysis:**
If a plasmolysed cell is placed in a hypotonic solution or distilled water, water re-enters by endosmosis. The protoplasm swells back and makes contact with the cell wall again. This process is called **deplasmolysis**. It works only in living cells (proves membrane is still functional).

## Section D: HOTS Question

**21.** A plant cell has an osmotic pressure of 8 atm and turgor pressure of 5 atm. (a) Calculate its DPD. (b) If this cell is now placed in pure water, what will happen to its DPD? Explain.

**Answer:**
(a) DPD = OP − TP = 8 − 5 = **3 atm**
(b) When placed in pure water (higher water potential → DPD = 0), water will enter the cell by endosmosis. As water enters, turgor pressure increases. DPD = OP − TP will decrease progressively. When the cell reaches full turgidity (TP = OP = 8 atm), DPD = 8 − 8 = **0 atm**. At this point, no further net movement of water occurs.
