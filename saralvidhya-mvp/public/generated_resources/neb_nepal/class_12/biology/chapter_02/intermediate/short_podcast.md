Speaking of physics and chemistry, let's start with the "elixir of life"—water.

Water! It’s everywhere in a plant. It makes up about 75% to 90% of the protoplasm in active cells. Without it, the protoplasm becomes inactive or even dead. It’s used as raw material for photosynthesis, it acts as a solvent for metabolic reactants, and it maintains cell turgidity.

It’s basically the medium that keeps everything moving and shaped. Now, to understand how water moves, we have to understand diffusion.

Diffusion is pretty straightforward. It’s the movement of molecules from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration until they reach equilibrium. It happens on its own without any energy required.

Think of it like opening a small bottle of gas in a room. Very quickly, those gas molecules spread out evenly. In plants, it’s a bit more complex, but the principle is the same. And the rate of diffusion depends on things like temperature—higher temperature means faster diffusion because of increased kinetic energy—and the density of the medium. Gases diffuse fastest, while solids are the slowest.

There was a cool experiment mentioned about copper sulphate crystals in water. When you drop them in, you see an intense blue color around the crystals that slowly spreads until the whole solution is a homogenous blue. That’s diffusion in action!

And we can’t talk about diffusion without mentioning Diffusion Pressure, or DP. This is the pressure exerted by the movement of these particles. It’s proportional to the concentration. If you add solutes to pure water, the diffusion pressure drops. That reduction is called the Diffusion Pressure Deficit, or DPD. Some people also call it suction pressure or suction force.

That leads us perfectly into osmosis. Osmosis is a special kind of diffusion. The key difference is that osmosis involves the movement of water through a semi-permeable membrane. It moves from a region of higher water concentration—like a dilute solution—to a region of lower water concentration—a stronger solution.

And that membrane is crucial. It’s a "differentially permeable" membrane, meaning it allows water and certain solutes to pass through but holds back the large solute particles. Examples include the plasma membrane or even an egg membrane.

There are two main types of osmosis to remember: Exosmosis and Endosmosis. Exosmosis is when water moves out of a cell because it's in a hypertonic solution—that’s a solution with a higher concentration than the cell sap. Endosmosis is the opposite; water moves into the cell when it's in a hypotonic solution.

This explains why cells change shape! If a cell takes in too much water, it becomes "turgid"—swollen and hard. If it loses water, it becomes "flaccid"—weak and shrunken. If it loses so much water that the cell membrane actually shrinks away from the cell wall, that’s called plasmolysis.

And if you put that plasmolysed cell back into a hypotonic solution, the water enters again, and the membrane returns to normal. That’s called deplasmolysis. This is actually why we use salt to preserve meat or make pickles—the high salt concentration causes plasmolysis in bacteria, killing them!

That’s a great real-world application. Okay, let's do a quick recap before we move to the next big topic. We've covered the importance of water, the mechanics of diffusion and DPD, and how osmosis leads to cell states like turgidity or plasmolysis. Ready for the "Ascent of Sap"?

I am. This is the part that puzzled botanists for a long time: how does water move from the roots all the way to the top of a tree that might be 90 meters tall, against the force of gravity?

It’s incredible. We know the path is through the xylem—specifically the tracheids and vessels. You can prove this with a stain test; if you put a plant in colored water, the xylem vessels turn red.

But the "how" is the big question. There were these "Vital Force Theories" where scientists like Godlewski and Sir J.C. Bose thought living cells were actively pumping the water. But those were rejected because water still rises even if the living cells are killed with picric acid.

Which brings us to the most widely accepted explanation: the Cohesion-Tension Theory, or Dixon’s Theory.

This one is brilliant. It relies on physics. It says there is a continuous column of water from the roots to the leaves. This is possible because of "cohesion"—the strong attraction between water molecules—and "adhesion"—the attraction between water and the xylem walls.

And the "engine" driving it all is the transpiration pull. As water evaporates from the leaves through the stomata, it creates a suction or tension that pulls the entire water column upward. It’s like drinking through a straw!

Exactly. And that leads us to our next major topic: Transpiration. This is the loss of water vapor from the aerial parts of the plant. Interestingly, plants absorb a huge amount of water, but they only use about 1 to 2 percent for growth. The other 97 to 99 percent is lost through transpiration.

There are three types of transpiration: Stomatal, which is the main one through the pores in leaves; Cuticular, through the cuticle; and Lenticular, through the lenticels in the bark.

Stomata are the stars here. In dicots, the guard cells are kidney-shaped, while in monocots, they are dumb-bell shaped. When the guard cells are turgid, the stomata open; when they lose turgidity, they close. This regulates the rate of transpiration.

Factors like light, temperature, wind, and humidity all affect it. For example, high light and high temperature usually increase transpiration, while high humidity decreases it.

We also see experiments like the "Bell Jar method" to show water loss, or using "Cobalt-Chloride paper" to show that more water evaporates from the lower surface of a leaf because that's where most stomata are located in dorsiventral leaves.

Before we wrap up, we should mention Guttation, because people often confuse it with transpiration.

Right. Guttation isn't water vapor; it's actual water droplets exuding from special pores called hydathodes, usually at the leaf margins. This happens at night or in the morning when soil moisture is high and transpiration is low, so the root pressure pushes the water out.

So, to summarize: Water moves via diffusion and osmosis, which maintains cell turgidity. The ascent of sap is driven primarily by the cohesion-tension mechanism and transpiration pull through the xylem. And finally, plants lose water through transpiration or, in specific conditions, through guttation.
