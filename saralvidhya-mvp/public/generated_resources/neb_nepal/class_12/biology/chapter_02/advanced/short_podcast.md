Right. And before we get into the heavy science, let's just define what we're actually studying. Physiology is the study of life processes—things like metabolism, growth, locomotion, and respiration. So, plant physiology is specifically the study of those life processes within plants.

Exactly. The term "physiology" has been around since 1552, used by Jean Fernel, though back then it just meant "natural knowledge." But according to Palladin, the real goal of studying plant physiology is to understand all the phenomena occurring in plants, analyze those complex processes, and eventually reduce them to the simpler terms of physics and chemistry.

Speaking of physics and chemistry, we can't talk about plants without talking about water. The text calls it the "liquid of life" or the "elixir of life." It’s a major component of protoplasm, making up about 75 to 90 percent of the living matter in active cells.

And it’s not just "there" for no reason. Water is essential for so many things: it’s a raw material for photosynthesis, a medium for dispersing organic components, it maintains cell turgidity, and it acts as a solvent for metabolic reactants. Without water, the protoplasm becomes inactive or even dead. It even influences plant movements, like how flowers open or how stomata move.

So, to understand how water moves, we have to understand two key concepts: Diffusion and Osmosis. Let's start with Diffusion. It’s basically the movement of molecules from an area of higher concentration to an area of lower concentration until they reach equilibrium. It happens all on its own—no energy required, no stirring or shaking needed.

A great way to visualize that is the gas bottle example. If you open a small bottle of gas in a room, the molecules quickly spread out until they are evenly distributed. And in terms of speed, gases diffuse the fastest, followed by liquids, and then solids, which are the slowest.

There’s actually a cool experiment with copper sulphate to prove this. If you drop copper sulphate crystals into a beaker of water, you’ll see an intense blue color around the crystals at first. As time passes, those molecules diffuse throughout the water until the whole solution has a homogenous color.

It’s also important to note what affects the rate of diffusion. Temperature increases it because it increases the kinetic energy of the molecules. Density of the medium also matters—the denser the medium, the more resistance there is, so diffusion slows down. And according to Graham's law, lighter gases will diffuse faster than heavier ones.

We also see the term "Diffusion Pressure" or DP. This is the pressure exerted by the movement of these particles. And there’s a specific concept called Diffusion Pressure Deficit, or DPD. When you add solute to a pure solvent, the diffusion pressure of that solution drops compared to the pure solvent. That reduction is the DPD. It’s also known as suction pressure or suction force because it’s what causes solvent molecules to move toward the solution.

And don't forget "Independent Diffusion." In plants, different substances like CO2 and Oxygen can diffuse through the stomata at the same time, independently of each other, based on their own concentration gradients or "partial pressures."

Okay, let's transition to the "special" type of diffusion: Osmosis. While diffusion is general, osmosis specifically involves the movement of water through a semi-permeable membrane. It moves from a region of higher water concentration—a dilute solution—to a region of lower water concentration—a stronger solution.

This is all about "chemical potential" or water potential. Pure water has the highest water potential. When you add solutes, you lower that potential. The membrane involved, like a plasma membrane, is "semi-permeable," meaning it lets water and certain solutes through but holds back the large ones.

Osmosis can go two ways. There is Exosmosis, where water moves out of the cell because it's in a hypertonic solution—that's a solution with a higher concentration than the cell sap. Then there is Endosmosis, where water moves into the cell because it's in a hypotonic solution—a lower concentration.

This leads to two different states for a cell. A "turgid" cell is swollen and hard because it's taken in water. A "flaccid" cell is limp or lacking firmness because it has lost water pressure. If a cell is in a very strong hypertonic solution, it undergoes plasmolysis—the cell membrane actually shrinks away from the cell wall. If you then put that cell in a hypotonic solution, it undergoes deplasmolysis, where water enters and the membrane moves back to the cell wall.

We can actually see this in experiments, like using a potato osmometer or an egg membrane. In the potato experiment, you carve a cavity in a potato, fill it with sugar solution, and place it in water. You'll see the liquid level in the cavity rise because water is moving through the potato cells via osmosis.

Let's do a quick recap before we move to the next big topic. We've covered how water is the elixir of life, how diffusion moves things from high to low concentration, and how osmosis is that specialized movement through a semi-permeable membrane that can lead to turgidity or plasmolysis.

Perfect. Now, let's tackle one of the great mysteries in plant physiology: The Ascent of Sap. How does water travel from the roots all the way to the top of a 90-meter tree, like a Sequoia, against the force of gravity?

It’s a huge question! We know the path is through the xylem—specifically the tracheids and vessels, which form hollow tubes. You can prove this with a "Stain Test" using eosin solution; the xylem will turn red. Or the "Ringing Test," where you remove the cortex and phloem but leave the xylem intact; the leaves stay fresh because the sap still moves through the xylem.

But the how is where it gets controversial. There are several theories. First, there were the "Vital Force Theories," like Godlewski’s relay pump theory or Sir J.C. Bose's pulsation theory. They thought living cells were actively pumping the water. But researchers like Strasburgher showed that water still rises even if you kill the living cells with picric acid. So, those theories are no longer accepted.

Then there's the "Root Pressure Theory" by J.H. Priestly. It suggests hydrostatic pressure in the roots pushes the water up. While root pressure exists, it can only push water up to about 21 meters, so it can't explain how giant trees work. It's also often absent in gymnosperms.

Which leads us to the most widely accepted explanation: The Cohesion-Tension Theory, or Dixon's Theory. It’s a combination of forces. First, there is a continuous column of water in the xylem. Second, water molecules stick to each other via "cohesion" and to the xylem walls via "adhesion." Finally, there is "transpiration pull." As water evaporates from the leaves, it creates a tension or suction that pulls the entire water column upward. It’s like drinking through a straw!

And that brings us perfectly to our next topic: Transpiration. This is the loss of water in the form of vapor from the aerial parts of the plant. Interestingly, plants take up a lot of water, but only about 1 to 2 percent is used for growth. The other 97 to 99 percent is lost through transpiration.

There are three main types of transpiration. Stomatal transpiration is the big one—about 90 percent of water loss happens through the stomata. Then there is cuticular transpiration through the cuticle, which is about 3 to 10 percent, and lenticular transpiration through the lenticels in the bark, which is only about 1 percent.

Let's talk about those stomata. They are minute pores bordered by two guard cells. In dicots, these guard cells are kidney-shaped, but in monocots, they are dumb-bell shaped. When the guard cells become turgid, they stretch and the pore opens. When they lose water and become flaccid, the pore closes.

Transpiration is actually super important. It cools the leaf surface, helps maintain turgidity, and allows minerals to move from the soil to the rest of the plant. We can measure the rate of water absorption—which is nearly equal to transpiration—using a device called Ganong's potometer.

One last thing before we wrap up: Guttation. People often confuse this with transpiration, but they are different. Transpiration is the loss of water vapor. Guttation is the secretion of actual water droplets from special structures called hydathodes, usually at the leaf margins.

Exactly. Guttation usually happens at night or in the morning when the soil is very wet and humidity is high. Because the stomata are closed, the plant can't transpire, so the root pressure builds up and forces liquid water out through the hydathodes.
