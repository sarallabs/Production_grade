## Section A: HOTS Multiple Choice Questions (1 mark each)

**1.** A plant cell has an osmotic pressure of 10 atm and a turgor pressure of 4 atm. Its DPD is:
- A. 14 atm
- B. 4 atm
- C. 6 atm ✓
- D. 10 atm

*Explanation: DPD = OP − TP = 10 − 4 = 6 atm*

**2.** When a fully turgid cell is placed in a solution, there is no net movement of water. The solution is:
- A. Hypotonic
- B. Hypertonic
- C. Isotonic ✓
- D. Distilled water

*Explanation: A fully turgid cell has DPD = 0. No net movement occurs when external solution has the same water potential — i.e., isotonic solution.*

## Section B: Case-Study / Passage-Based Questions (3–5 marks each)

**Question 11 (5 marks)**

*Read the following passage and answer the questions below:*

"A botanist conducts an experiment with two identical potato cylinders. Cylinder A is placed in distilled water and Cylinder B is placed in a concentrated sugar solution. After 30 minutes, Cylinder A is firm and slightly enlarged, while Cylinder B is limp and has decreased in size."

a) Name the process responsible for the changes in each cylinder. (1)
b) Explain, using water potential terminology, why Cylinder A became firm. (2)
c) If Cylinder B is now transferred to distilled water, what will happen? Name this process. (1)
d) What would happen if both cylinders were placed in a solution of the same concentration as the cell sap? (1)

**Answers:**
a) Cylinder A: Endosmosis (osmosis); Cylinder B: Exosmosis (plasmolysis)
b) Distilled water has higher water potential (Ψ = 0) than potato cell sap (Ψ < 0). Water moves from distilled water into cells down the water potential gradient. Turgor pressure increases, making the cell firm.
c) Deplasmolysis — water re-enters the cells, the protoplast moves back to the cell wall, and turgidity is restored.
d) No net movement of water (isotonic condition); cylinders remain unchanged.

**Question 12 (4 marks)**

*A farmer notices white crystalline deposits along the leaf margins of his paddy crop early in the morning. The leaves appear healthy otherwise.*

a) Identify the process responsible for these deposits. (1)
b) Under what conditions does this process occur? (1)
c) Through what structures does this process occur? (1)
d) How does this process differ mechanistically from transpiration? (1)

**Answers:**
a) Guttation
b) High soil moisture, high humidity, low transpiration — typically at night or early morning when stomata are closed
c) Hydathodes (located at leaf tips and margins)
d) Guttation is driven by positive root pressure (a push from below); transpiration is driven by negative pressure / transpiration pull (a pull from above). Guttation results in liquid water exudation; transpiration involves water vapour loss.

**Question 13 (5 marks)**

*A scientist poisons a tall tree's living cells using picric acid. After 24 hours, a dye injected at the base is found in the leaves. However, guttation has stopped.*

a) Which theory of ascent of sap is supported by the continued dye movement? (1)
b) Which scientist first performed a similar experiment? (1)
c) Why has guttation stopped even though the dye is still moving? (2)
d) What does this tell us about the mechanisms driving guttation vs. ascent of sap? (1)

**Answers:**
a) Cohesion-Tension Theory (Dixon's Theory)
b) Strasburgher
c) Root pressure is generated by osmotic activity of living root cells. Picric acid kills root cells → root pressure cannot be generated → guttation stops. Ascent of sap, however, continues because it is driven by physical forces (cohesion, adhesion, transpiration pull) that do not require living cells.
d) Guttation requires living (metabolically active) root cells for root pressure; ascent of sap via cohesion-tension is a purely physical mechanism independent of living cells.

**Question 14 (3 marks)**

*In an experiment, cobalt chloride paper (blue when dry, pink when wet) is placed on both surfaces of a dicot leaf and sealed. After 5 minutes, the lower surface paper turns pink faster.*

a) What does this experiment demonstrate? (1)
b) Explain why the lower surface turns pink faster. (1)
c) Name one xerophytic adaptation that would slow this colour change on both surfaces. (1)

**Answers:**
a) It demonstrates that the rate of transpiration (stomatal) is higher on the lower (abaxial) surface of a dorsiventral leaf.
b) Dicot dorsiventral leaves have more stomata on the lower (abaxial) surface than the upper (adaxial) surface → more water vapour is lost from the lower surface → faster colour change.
c) Sunken stomata (crypts) — reduces the effective vapour pressure gradient, slowing water vapour loss.

**Question 15 (4 marks)**

*Consider a plant cell with OP = 12 atm and TP = 12 atm.*

a) Calculate the DPD. (1)
b) What is the physiological state of this cell? (1)
c) If this cell is placed in a solution with OP = 8 atm, what will happen? (1)
d) What is the DPD of pure water? (1)

**Answers:**
a) DPD = OP − TP = 12 − 12 = 0 atm
b) Fully turgid cell (no net water movement occurs)
c) The cell's water potential is equal to or higher than the solution. Since the solution has lower solute concentration (OP = 8 atm) than the cell sap, it is hypotonic relative to the cell. However, since the cell's DPD = 0, it will lose water slightly by exosmosis until a new equilibrium is reached. (The cell will undergo slight plasmolysis.)
d) DPD of pure water = 0

## Section C: High-Difficulty Long Answer Questions (5 marks each)

**Question 16:** Critically evaluate all theories of ascent of sap. Which theory is most widely accepted, and what experimental evidence supports it?

**Answer outline:**
- **Vital Force Theories:** Godlewski (relay pump) and Bose (pulsation) — both rejected because Strasburgher showed water rises in stems with dead/poisoned cells.
- **Root Pressure Theory (Priestly):** Root osmotic pressure creates positive hydrostatic pressure. Can lift water ~21 m only (2 atm). Absent in gymnosperms. Cannot explain ascent in tall trees.
- **Atmospheric Pressure Theory:** Works to ~10 m only. Requires a free exposed surface at root base — does not exist.
- **Imbibition Theory:** Slow movement; insufficient force.
- **Cohesion-Tension Theory (Dixon & Joly):** ACCEPTED. Three components: (1) Continuous water column in xylem — maintained by cohesion (H-bonds, ~350 atm) and adhesion to xylem walls. (2) Transpiration pull — evaporation from mesophyll cells creates negative pressure/tension, transmitted through continuous column to roots. (3) Root → stem → leaf: a continuous tension gradient. Experimental evidence: twig + mercury tube experiment shows mercury rises as twig transpires; stain/ringing tests confirm xylem is the pathway.

**Question 17:** With a labelled diagram and ion-transport mechanism, explain how guard cells regulate stomatal opening and closing. How does this relate to transpiration and carbon assimilation?

**Answer outline:**
- **Structure:** Two guard cells flank stomatal pore. In dicots: kidney-shaped (thicker inner walls). In monocots: dumb-bell shaped.
- **Opening (light/day):**
  - Light activates H⁺-ATPase pump in guard cell membrane
  - H⁺ exported → membrane hyperpolarises → K⁺ channels open → K⁺ floods into guard cells (along with Cl⁻ and malate²⁻)
  - Solute concentration ↑ → water potential ↓ → water enters by osmosis
  - Turgor pressure ↑ → thin outer walls stretch more → cells bow → pore opens
- **Closing (dark/water stress):**
  - ABA (abscisic acid) released under drought → Ca²⁺ signalling → K⁺ exits guard cells
  - Water exits → cells become flaccid → pore closes
- **Physiological significance:**
  - Open stomata: CO₂ enters → photosynthesis; water vapour exits → transpiration pull drives water/mineral uptake
  - Closed stomata: prevents water loss but also limits CO₂ → reduced photosynthesis
  - Guard cells thus regulate the trade-off between gas exchange and water conservation

---

**Question 18:** Define osmosis. Distinguish between endosmosis, exosmosis, and plasmolysis. Explain the relationship between DPD, osmotic pressure, and turgor pressure in a plant cell across different states.

**Answer outline:**
- **Osmosis:** Passive movement of water molecules through differentially permeable membrane from high water potential to low water potential.
- **Endosmosis:** Cell in hypotonic solution → water enters → turgor ↑ → cell swells (turgid)
- **Exosmosis:** Cell in hypertonic solution → water exits → turgor ↓ → plasmolysis
- **Plasmolysis:** Protoplast shrinks away from wall. Incipient plasmolysis: just as protoplast loses contact with wall; TP = 0, DPD = OP
- **DPD relationships:**

| Cell State | OP | TP | DPD | WP |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pure water | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| Flaccid cell (in hypertonic) | X | 0 | = OP | 0 |
| Partially turgid | X | < OP | OP − TP | = TP |
| Fully turgid | X | = OP | 0 | = OP |

- Water always moves from low DPD cell → high DPD cell

---

**Question 19:** Explain transpiration as a necessary evil. Describe all types, factors affecting it, and methods of measurement.

**Answer outline:**
- **"Necessary evil":** 97–99% water lost, yet transpiration drives mineral transport, cools leaves, and generates the transpiration pull for ascent of sap. Without it, these functions fail.
- **Types:** Stomatal (~90%), Cuticular (3–10%), Lenticular (~1%)
- **Factors:**
  - External: Light (stomatal opening), temperature (up to 35°C increases rate), humidity (inverse), wind (increases rate by removing saturated air), CO₂ concentration
  - Internal: Stomata number/position, cuticle thickness, sunken stomata, leaf area
- **Measurement:**
  - Ganong's potometer — measures water absorption ≈ transpiration rate via air bubble displacement
  - Bell jar experiment — visual/qualitative proof
  - Cobalt chloride paper — locates and compares stomatal density on surfaces

## Section D: Challenge Questions

**Question 21:** A 100-metre Eucalyptus tree transpires vigorously on a hot afternoon. Using the Cohesion-Tension theory, trace the exact path and forces acting on a water molecule from soil → root → xylem → leaf → atmosphere. Quantify the approximate tensions required.

*Expected points:*
- Soil water → root hair (by osmosis; DPD of root > soil water)
- Root hair → cortex → endodermis → xylem (symplast/apoplast paths)
- Xylem column maintained by cohesion (~350 atm possible) + adhesion
- At leaf: evaporation from mesophyll cell walls into intercellular spaces → water potential gradient → water pulled from xylem into mesophyll
- Tension in xylem of a 100-m tree: ~10 atm just to support the water column + additional tension from evaporation (~20–30 atm total estimated)
- Water vapour exits through stomata into drier atmosphere (following humidity gradient)

**Question 22:** Design an experiment to prove that stomatal opening is triggered by light and mediated by K⁺ ions. Include hypothesis, materials, procedure, expected results, and conclusion.

*Expected points:*
- **Hypothesis:** Light causes K⁺ influx into guard cells, lowering water potential, causing osmotic water entry and stomatal opening
- **Materials:** Epidermal peels, K⁺ indicator dye (e.g., sodium cobalt nitrite), light source, microscope, potassium-free buffer
- **Procedure:** (1) Prepare epidermal peels; (2) Incubate half in light, half in dark; (3) Stain for K⁺; (4) Observe stomatal aperture under microscope
- **Expected results:** Light-treated peels: guard cells show K⁺ accumulation + open stomata. Dark-treated: K⁺ in subsidiary cells, closed stomata
- **Conclusion:** K⁺ ion redistribution from subsidiary cells to guard cells is the mechanism for light-induced stomatal opening

**Question 23:** A student argues: "Since gymnosperms lack root pressure, they should wilt easily and not survive." Is this argument valid? Justify using the principles of water transport.

*Expected points:*
- Argument is INVALID
- Ascent of sap in gymnosperms depends entirely on the Cohesion-Tension mechanism (transpiration pull + cohesion + adhesion) — this does not require root pressure
- Root pressure is a supplementary/minor mechanism seen mainly in herbaceous angiosperms in rainy season
- Gymnosperms (e.g., conifers) have highly efficient tracheids with bordered pits that maintain continuous water column integrity
- Stomatal regulation reduces excessive transpiration (needle-like leaves, thick cuticle, sunken stomata in many gymnosperms)
- Therefore, gymnosperms are well-adapted for efficient water transport without root pressure
