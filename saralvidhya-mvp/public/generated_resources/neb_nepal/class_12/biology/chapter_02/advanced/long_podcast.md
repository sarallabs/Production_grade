Let's start with diffusion. It sounds simple, but it's fundamental. Diffusion is just the movement of molecules from an area of higher concentration to an area of lower concentration until they reach equilibrium.

And the best part is that it happens on its own. It doesn't require any energy or stirring. It just... happens. Think about opening a small bottle of gas in a room; very soon, those molecules are evenly distributed.

Right. And the speed matters. Gases diffuse much faster than liquids, and liquids are faster than solids. In fact, the rate of diffusion depends on the concentration of the molecules and the density of the medium. The denser the medium, the more resistance the particles face, so the slower they move. And then we have the "Diffusion Pressure Deficit," or DPD. This one is a bit tricky. Pure water has the maximum diffusion pressure. But when you add solutes to that water, the diffusion pressure drops. That reduction is the DPD. It’s also called suction pressure or suction force. It’s basically the pressure that causes solvent molecules to move toward a solution.

Okay, so diffusion is about molecules moving down a concentration gradient. But what happens when we add a membrane into the mix? That’s where we get to osmosis.

Osmosis is a special type of diffusion. It specifically involves the movement of water through a semi-permeable membrane—or a differentially permeable membrane—from a region of higher water concentration, like a dilute solution, to a region of lower water concentration, like a stronger solution.

Or, if you want to get fancy with the terminology, it’s the movement of water from a higher chemical potential to a lower chemical potential. Pure water has the highest water potential. And since osmosis is passive, it doesn't require energy.

And we have to remember what a semi-permeable membrane actually does. It allows water molecules and certain solutes to pass through, but it holds back the large solute particles. Examples include the plasma membrane, an egg membrane, or even a fish bladder.

There are two main types of osmosis: Exosmosis and Endosmosis. Exosmosis is when water moves out of a cell because it's placed in a hypertonic solution—a solution with a higher concentration than the cell sap. Endosmosis is the opposite; water moves into the cell when it's placed in a hypotonic solution.

And that brings us to the three types of solutions: Isotonic, where concentrations are the same; Hypertonic, where the surrounding solution is more concentrated; and Hypotonic, where the surrounding solution is less concentrated.

This directly affects whether a cell is turgid or flaccid. A turgid cell is swollen and hard because it’s taken up water. A flaccid cell is limp or shrunken because it has lost pressure.

We also need to distinguish between Turgor Pressure and Wall Pressure. When endosmosis happens, the incoming water stretches the protoplasm against the cell wall. That pressure is the Turgor Pressure. But since the cell wall is rigid, it pushes back with an equal and opposite force. That's the Wall Pressure.

This leads to something called Plasmolysis. If you put a plant cell in a hypertonic solution, water rushes out via exosmosis. The cell shrinks, and eventually, the cell membrane actually pulls away from the cell wall. That’s plasmolysis. If you then put that cell in a hypotonic solution, water enters, the membrane moves back to the wall, and that’s deplasmolysis.

Plasmolysis is actually quite significant. It helps scientists understand if a cell is living, it’s used in food preservation like pickling to kill bacteria, and it proves that the cell wall is permeable while the plasma membrane is selectively permeable.

And it’s been a huge puzzle for botanists. The main questions are: what is the path, and what are the forces? So the path is clear: the xylem. But the force? That’s where the theories come in. First, there were the "Vital Force Theories." But those were rejected, right?

Yeah, because researchers like Strasburgher and Mac Dougal showed that water continues to rise even after the living cells are killed by boiling or picric acid. So, the movement isn't driven by living cells.

Then there's the Root Pressure Theory by J.H. Priestly. He suggested that hydrostatic pressure builds up in the roots and pushes the water up. But there are huge objections to that. Root pressure is usually very low—only about 2 atmospheres—which can only push water up about 21 meters. It can't explain how water reaches 90 meters. Plus, it's often absent in gymnosperms and only really seen in the rainy season.

So, we move into the Physical Force Theories. The first one is Atmospheric Pressure. The idea was that evaporation at the surface creates a vacuum, and atmospheric pressure pushes the water up. But that only works up to 10 meters, and you’d need a free exposed surface at the bottom of the plant, which doesn't exist.

And Imbibition Theory? That’s where water is absorbed by substances that swell, like wood. While imbibition force is strong, the movement is way too slow to be the main driver for the ascent of sap.

Which brings us to the theory that most scientists actually believe in today: the Cohesion-Tension Theory, or Dixon's Theory.

This is the big one. It relies on a few key features. First, there is a continuous column of water from the roots to the leaves. Second, there are two forces at work: cohesion and adhesion. Cohesion is the strong mutual attraction between water molecules—it can be as high as 350 atmospheres! Adhesion is the attraction between water and the xylem walls. Together, they keep the water column intact.

And the "pull" comes from transpiration, right?

Exactly. When water evaporates from the leaves through the stomata, it creates a tension or a "transpiration pull." This pull is transmitted all the way down to the roots. As water is lost, the osmotic pressure in the leaf cells increases, drawing more water from the xylem. This combined pressure is strong enough to pull water up even the tallest trees. It’s a purely physical process that requires almost no metabolic energy.

There was an objection about air bubbles breaking the column, but Dixon explained that the interconnected network of vessels helps maintain continuity. You can even demonstrate this with an experiment where a transpiring twig is attached to a tube of water and dipped in mercury; as the twig transpires, the mercury level rises, showing the suction force.

So, we've moved from the roots to the leaves. And that leads us directly into our next topic: Transpiration.

Transpiration is the loss of water in the form of vapor from the exposed aerial parts of the plant—leaves, stems, and flowers. Interestingly, plants take up a huge amount of water, but they only use about 1-2% for growth and metabolism. The other 97-99% is lost through transpiration.

It’s a massive loss! There are three types: Stomatal, which is the main one—about 90% of water loss—through the stomata. Then Cuticular transpiration, through the cuticle, which is about 3-10%. And finally, Lenticular transpiration, through the lenticels in the bark of perennial plants, which is only about 1%.

Let’s talk about the structure of the stomata, because that’s how the plant regulates this. In dicots, the guard cells are kidney-shaped, while in monocots, they are dumb-bell shaped. They are surrounded by subsidiary cells. When the turgor pressure in the guard cells increases, the outer walls stretch, pulling the inner walls apart and opening the pore. When they lose water, they lose turgidity and the pore closes.

This regulation is vital. Transpiration helps cool the leaf surface, maintains cell turgidity, and allows minerals to move from the soil to the rest of the plant. It also helps in growth and development.

There are so many factors that affect how much a plant transpires. Light is huge because it controls stomatal opening. Temperature also matters—higher temperatures generally increase transpiration up to 35°C. Wind can increase it by removing saturated air, but if the wind is too fast, it might actually cause the stomata to close. Humidity is the opposite: high humidity decreases transpiration, while low humidity increases it.

And don't forget plant-specific factors, like the thickness of the cuticle, the number of stomata, or having "sunken stomata," which helps reduce water loss.

We've seen some cool experiments for this too. Like the Bell Jar method, where you see water droplets form on the inside of a jar covering a potted plant. Or the Cobalt-Chloride paper experiment, which proves that stomatal transpiration is higher on the lower surface of a dorsiventral leaf because that’s where most stomata are located.

And for measuring the rate, there's Ganong's potometer. It measures the absorption of water, which is nearly equal to the rate of transpiration. You just track the movement of an air bubble in a graduated tube.

Okay, we are almost at the end. We have one more specific process to cover: Guttation.

Guttation is different from transpiration. Instead of losing water as vapor, the plant secretes actual water droplets. This happens through special structures called hydathodes, usually found at the leaf tips or margins.

It usually happens at night or in the early morning when the soil is very wet and humidity is high. In these conditions, the roots absorb a lot of water, but transpiration is low because the stomata are closed. This builds up root pressure, which forces the water out through the hydathodes.

And unlike transpiration, guttation leaves behind a white crust of inorganic and organic compounds, like potassium and sugars, when the water evaporates.

While guttation helps balance water and nutrients, it can actually be a bit risky for the plant. The salt deposits can sometimes cause injury to the leaf margins, or lead to dehydration if too much water and nutrients are lost.
