## 1. What is Plant Physiology?

Plant physiology is the study of **how plants work** — how they grow, breathe, make food, and move water. Think of it as biology from the inside out.

- **Father of Plant Physiology:** Stephan Hales
- **Father of Indian Plant Physiology:** Sir J.C. Bose
- The word "physiology" was first used by Jean Fernel in 1552 and simply meant "natural knowledge"

---

## 2. Why is Water So Important?

Water is called the **"elixir of life"** or **"liquid of life"** because:

- It makes up **75–90%** of the living part (protoplasm) of plant cells
- Without water, protoplasm becomes inactive — the cell can die
- Water is needed for:
  - Making food through photosynthesis
  - Carrying nutrients and minerals around the plant
  - Keeping cells firm (turgid)
  - Helping flowers open and stomata move

---

## 3. Diffusion — Molecules on the Move

**What is diffusion?**
Molecules move from where there are **a lot of them** (high concentration) to where there are **fewer of them** (low concentration) — all by themselves, without needing any energy.

**Easy example:** Open a bottle of perfume in a room. Soon, everyone in the room can smell it — that's diffusion!

**Another example (from the book):** Drop a copper sulphate crystal (blue) into water. At first, the blue colour is only near the crystal. Slowly, the blue spreads until the whole water is evenly blue. Diffusion!

**Key facts:**
- Gases diffuse fastest → liquids → solids (slowest)
- Higher temperature = faster diffusion
- Denser medium = slower diffusion

**Diffusion Pressure Deficit (DPD):**
- Pure water has the most diffusion pressure
- When you add salt or sugar to water, the pressure drops
- That drop is called DPD — also known as **suction pressure**
- DPD is the "thirst" of a solution — how much it wants to pull water in

## 4. Osmosis — Special Water Movement

**What is osmosis?**
Osmosis is a **special type of diffusion** where only **water** moves through a thin, selective membrane (semi-permeable membrane) from a watery area to a less watery area.

**Semi-permeable membrane:** Acts like a filter — lets water through but blocks big molecules. Examples: plasma membrane, egg membrane.

**Types:**
| Type | What happens | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Endosmosis | Water enters the cell (hypotonic solution outside) | Cell swells → **Turgid** |
| Exosmosis | Water leaves the cell (hypertonic solution outside) | Cell shrinks → **Flaccid** |

**Solution types:**
- **Hypotonic:** Less concentrated than cell sap → water enters cell
- **Hypertonic:** More concentrated than cell sap → water leaves cell
- **Isotonic:** Same concentration as cell sap → no net movement

**Plasmolysis:**
When a cell is in a very concentrated (hypertonic) solution, it loses so much water that the inner membrane actually pulls away from the cell wall. This is called **plasmolysis**.

**Deplasmolysis:**
Put the plasmolysed cell back in water → water enters → membrane returns to wall → cell recovers!

**Turgor Pressure:** The pressure of water pushing against the cell wall from inside — makes the cell firm.
**Wall Pressure:** The cell wall pushes back equally — stops the cell from bursting.

## 5. Ascent of Sap — How Water Climbs a Tree

**The big question:** How does water travel from the roots all the way to the top of a tree that can be 90 metres tall? That's fighting gravity!

**Path:** Water travels through **xylem** — special tube-like structures (tracheids and vessels) inside the plant.

**Proof:**
- **Stain test:** Put a plant in red eosin dye → xylem turns red
- **Ringing test:** Remove bark (cortex + phloem) → plant still survives → xylem is doing the work

**Old theories (rejected):**
- Scientists first thought living cells were pumping the water — but water still rises even if you kill those cells. So these theories were thrown out.
- **Root pressure** can only push water about 21 metres — not enough for giant trees.

**The ACCEPTED theory — Cohesion-Tension Theory (Dixon's Theory):**
1. Water molecules **stick together** — this is **cohesion** (like links in a chain)
2. Water sticks to xylem walls — this is **adhesion** (stops the chain from slipping)
3. When leaves lose water vapour through stomata, it creates a **pull** (like drinking through a straw) — this is the **transpiration pull**
4. This pull drags the entire water chain upward from roots to leaves

## 6. Transpiration — Plants Breathing Out Water

**What is transpiration?**
Transpiration is the loss of **water vapour** from the leaves, stems, and flowers of a plant.

**Did you know?** Plants absorb a LOT of water, but they only use **1–2%** for growth. The other **97–99%** is lost as vapour through transpiration!

**Types of transpiration:**
| Type | Where | How much |
|---|---|---|
| Stomatal | Through stomata (tiny pores) | ~90% (most!) |
| Cuticular | Through the waxy skin (cuticle) | 3–10% |
| Lenticular | Through tiny holes in bark (lenticels) | ~1% |

**Stomata and Guard Cells:**
- Stomata are tiny pores on leaves
- Two **guard cells** surround each pore
- **Dicots:** Guard cells are kidney-shaped
- **Monocots:** Guard cells are dumb-bell shaped
- When guard cells fill with water (turgid) → pore **opens**
- When guard cells lose water (flaccid) → pore **closes**

**Why is transpiration useful?**
- Cools the leaf surface (like sweating)
- Creates the pull that brings water and minerals up from roots
- Keeps cells turgid

**Factors that affect transpiration:**
- **Light:** More light → stomata open → more transpiration
- **Temperature:** Warmer → faster transpiration
- **Wind:** More wind → removes moist air → more transpiration
- **Humidity:** More humidity → less transpiration (air already moist)

**Cool experiments:**
- **Bell Jar:** Cover a plant with a glass jar → water droplets form inside
- **Cobalt Chloride paper:** Blue paper turns pink when wet — turns pink faster on the lower leaf surface (where more stomata are)

## 7. Guttation — Liquid Water Drops

**What is guttation?**
Guttation is when a plant releases **liquid water droplets** (not vapour!) from the tips or edges of leaves. You might see these tiny drops early in the morning and mistake them for dew.

**How it happens:**
- Happens at night or early morning
- Soil is very wet → roots absorb lots of water
- Stomata are closed → no transpiration happening
- Root pressure builds up → pushes water out through **hydathodes** (special pores at leaf margins)

**Guttation vs. Transpiration:**
| | Transpiration | Guttation |
|---|---|---|
| Type of water | Vapour | Liquid droplets |
| Time | Daytime | Night / early morning |
| Exit point | Stomata, cuticle | Hydathodes |
| Driving force | Transpiration pull | Root pressure |

---

## 8. Common Mistakes to Avoid

- Don't confuse **guttation** (liquid drops) with **transpiration** (vapour)
- Don't think osmosis needs energy — it doesn't, it's passive!
- Don't confuse **turgid** (swollen, firm) with **flaccid** (limp, soft)
- Don't think all water loss is through stomata — there's also cuticular and lenticular

## 10. Quick Revision

1. Water = 75–90% of protoplasm; essential for photosynthesis and life
2. Diffusion = molecules move from high to low concentration; no energy needed
3. DPD = suction pressure; created when solutes are added to water
4. Osmosis = water through semi-permeable membrane from high to low water concentration
5. Endosmosis → turgid; Exosmosis → flaccid → Plasmolysis
6. Ascent of sap = through xylem; best theory = Cohesion-Tension (transpiration pull + cohesion + adhesion)
7. Transpiration = water vapour loss; 90% through stomata; regulated by guard cells
8. Guttation = liquid water from hydathodes; driven by root pressure; at night
