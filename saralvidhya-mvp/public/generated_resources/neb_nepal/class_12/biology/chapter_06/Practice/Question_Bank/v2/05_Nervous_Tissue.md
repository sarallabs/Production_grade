## Q1. Describe the structure of a multipolar neuron with a labelled diagram description.
**Type:** Describe  |  **Bloom's:** Understand  |  **Difficulty:** Medium
**Mindmap Path:** `Animal Tissue > Nervous Tissue > Neurone`
**Recommended Diagram:** Labelled diagram of a multipolar motor neuron

**Answer:**
1. A multipolar neuron is the most common type of neuron in the body. It has one axon and multiple dendrites arising from the soma.
2. Soma (Cyton): The cell body — roughly spherical or polygonal. Contains: a large, centrally placed nucleus with a prominent nucleolus; Nissl bodies (rER + ribosomes) for protein synthesis; neurofibrils for structural support; mitochondria; Golgi apparatus; and lysosomes.
3. Dendrites: Multiple, short, branched processes arising from the soma. They increase the receptive surface area. They carry nerve impulses toward the soma (afferent to cell body). They contain Nissl bodies but no myelin.
4. Axon (Nerve fibre): A single, long, uniform process arising from the axon hillock of the soma. Carries impulses away from soma (efferent). Covered by myelin sheath (formed by Schwann cells in PNS; oligodendrocytes in CNS) interrupted at regular intervals by Nodes of Ranvier. Ends in terminal branches called telodendria, each tipped by a synaptic knob (bouton) that releases neurotransmitters.
5. Myelin Sheath: Lipid-rich layer; insulates axon; enables saltatory conduction. Outer to myelin: Neurilemma (sheath of Schwann) — the outer nucleated layer of Schwann cells important for axon regeneration in PNS.
6. Examples of multipolar neurons: motor neurons of spinal cord anterior horn, Purkinje cells of cerebellum, interneurons of cerebral cortex.
7. Labelling checklist: Dendrites → Soma/Cyton → Nucleus → Nissl bodies → Axon hillock → Axon → Myelin sheath → Node of Ranvier → Schwann cell → Telodendria → Synaptic knob.

## Q2. Classify neurons based on (a) number of processes and (b) direction of impulse conduction, with examples.
**Type:** Classify  |  **Bloom's:** Understand  |  **Difficulty:** Medium
**Mindmap Path:** `Animal Tissue > Nervous Tissue > Neurone > Structure and Function`
**Recommended Diagram:** Diagrams of apolar, unipolar, bipolar, and multipolar neurons

**Answer:**
1. Neurons are classified on two main bases: structural (number of processes) and functional (direction of impulse).
2. Structural Classification by Number of Processes: (a) Apolar neurons — no processes; found only in embryonic nervous system during early development; (b) Pseudopolar (Pseudounipolar) neurons — one process from soma that divides into central and peripheral branches; common in dorsal root ganglia (sensory neurons); (c) Unipolar neurons — one process; rare in vertebrates; found in invertebrate nervous systems; (d) Bipolar neurons — two processes (one dendrite, one axon); found in retina (bipolar cells), olfactory epithelium, auditory/vestibular ganglia; (e) Multipolar neurons — multiple dendrites, one axon; most common; all motor neurons and most interneurons.
3. Functional Classification by Impulse Direction: (a) Sensory (Afferent) neurons — carry impulses from sense organs to CNS; most are pseudounipolar (dorsal root ganglia); (b) Motor (Efferent) neurons — carry impulses from CNS to muscles/glands; most are multipolar (anterior horn of spinal cord); (c) Intermediate (Interneurons/Adjustor neurons) — located entirely within CNS; connect sensory and motor neurons; responsible for integration, learning, and reflexes; are multipolar.
4. Comparison Table: | Classification | Type | Processes | Location | | Structural | Apolar | 0 | Embryo | | | Pseudopolar | 1 (splits) | Dorsal root ganglia | | | Bipolar | 2 | Retina, cochlea | | | Multipolar | Many dendrites | Motor cortex, spinal cord | | Functional | Sensory | — | Skin to CNS | | | Motor | — | CNS to muscles | | | Interneuron | — | Within CNS |
5. The diversity of neuron types reflects the complexity of nervous system functions: simple reflex arcs use all three functional types; complex behaviours involve vast networks of interneurons.
6. Clinically, loss of specific neuron types produces characteristic disorders: loss of motor neurons (ALS), loss of specific sensory neurons (peripheral neuropathy), loss of interneurons (epilepsy, cognitive disorders).

## Q3. Classify and describe the types of neuroglia in both the CNS and PNS with their functions.
**Type:** Classify  |  **Bloom's:** Understand  |  **Difficulty:** Hard
**Mindmap Path:** `Animal Tissue > Nervous Tissue > Neuroglia`
**Recommended Diagram:** Diagram of CNS tissue showing astrocyte, oligodendrocyte, microglia, and ependymal cell

**Answer:**
1. Neuroglia (glial cells) are non-neuronal supporting cells of the nervous system. They outnumber neurons approximately 10:1 and do not conduct impulses.
2. CNS Neuroglia — 4 types: (a) Astrocytes (star-shaped): largest glial cells; provide structural scaffolding; regulate extracellular K+ and neurotransmitters; form the blood-brain barrier (BBB) with end-feet on blood vessels; supply nutrients to neurons; (b) Oligodendrocytes: produce myelin sheaths in the CNS; one cell can myelinate several axons simultaneously; (c) Microglia: smallest glial cells; phagocytic (CNS macrophages); remove cellular debris and pathogens; activated in neuroinflammation; (d) Ependymal cells: ciliated epithelial-like cells lining brain ventricles and central canal of spinal cord; produce and circulate cerebrospinal fluid (CSF).
3. PNS Neuroglia — 2 types: (a) Schwann cells: produce myelin sheaths in the PNS; one Schwann cell covers one internodal segment of one axon; the outer neurilemma (sheath of Schwann) is critical for axon regeneration after PNS injury; (b) Satellite (Stellate) cells: surround cell bodies of neurons in peripheral ganglia (dorsal root ganglia, autonomic ganglia); regulate the microenvironment of the ganglionic neurons.
4. Key differences — CNS vs PNS myelination: Oligodendrocytes (CNS) myelinate multiple axon segments; Schwann cells (PNS) myelinate one segment per cell. PNS axons can regenerate (Schwann cell guidance + neurilemma); CNS axons generally cannot.
5. Comparison Table: | Glial Type | Location | Key Function | | Astrocyte | CNS | Support, BBB, ionic homeostasis | | Oligodendrocyte | CNS | Myelination (multiple axons) | | Microglia | CNS | Immune surveillance, phagocytosis | | Ependymal cell | CNS | CSF production and circulation | | Schwann cell | PNS | Myelination (one axon) + regeneration | | Satellite cell | PNS | Support of ganglionic neurons |
6. Glial dysfunction is implicated in major neurological diseases: astrocyte dysfunction in epilepsy; oligodendrocyte loss in multiple sclerosis (MS); microglial overactivation in Alzheimer's disease and Parkinson's disease.

## Q4. Explain saltatory conduction in myelinated nerve fibres and compare it with continuous conduction in non-myelinated fibres.
**Type:** Mechanism  |  **Bloom's:** Analyze  |  **Difficulty:** Hard
**Mindmap Path:** `Animal Tissue > Nervous Tissue > Neurone > Myelin Sheath > Myelinated`
**Recommended Diagram:** Diagram showing saltatory vs continuous conduction with Nodes of Ranvier

**Answer:**
1. Nerve impulse conduction depends on whether the axon is myelinated or non-myelinated.
2. Continuous Conduction (Non-myelinated fibres): The action potential is generated at one point and spreads continuously along the entire axon membrane, activating every adjacent patch of membrane. This is slow and energy-intensive because Na+/K+ pumps must restore ion gradients at every point. Example: autonomic nerve fibres, pain (C fibres).
3. Saltatory Conduction (Myelinated fibres): The myelin sheath insulates the axon except at Nodes of Ranvier (small unmyelinated gaps ~1 μm wide spaced 1–2 mm apart). The action potential 'jumps' from node to node — ions can only enter/exit the axon at nodes. This is possible because the myelin prevents current dissipation between nodes.
4. Mechanism of Saltatory Conduction: Depolarisation at Node 1 → current flows inside axon to Node 2 (myelin prevents lateral loss) → Node 2 is depolarised above threshold → action potential fires at Node 2 → repeats to next node.
5. Advantages of Saltatory Conduction: (a) Speed — up to 120 m/s in thick myelinated fibres (vs. 0.5–2 m/s in non-myelinated); (b) Energy efficiency — Na+/K+ pumps only restore gradients at nodes, not along the entire axon; (c) Reduced fibre diameter needed for high-speed conduction.
6. Disease relevance: In Multiple Sclerosis (MS), autoimmune destruction of the myelin sheath by oligodendrocyte damage disrupts saltatory conduction → slowed or blocked nerve signals → symptoms of weakness, sensory loss, and vision problems.
7. Diagram description: Myelinated axon with thick sheath segments and narrow nodes; arrows showing 'jumping' of depolarisation from node to node.

## Q5. Compare gray matter and white matter in the CNS under five key parameters.
**Type:** Compare  |  **Bloom's:** Analyze  |  **Difficulty:** Medium
**Mindmap Path:** `Animal Tissue > Nervous Tissue > Neurone > Color`
**Recommended Diagram:** Cross-section of spinal cord showing central H-shaped gray matter and peripheral white matter

**Answer:**
1. The CNS (brain and spinal cord) is composed of both gray matter and white matter, each with distinct composition, location, and function.
2. Comparison Table: | Parameter | Gray Matter | White Matter | | Composition | Neuronal cell bodies, dendrites, unmyelinated axons, glial cells | Myelinated axons, myelin sheaths, glial cells | | Colour | Gray/pink (no myelin) | White (lipid-rich myelin) | | Location in spinal cord | Central (H-shaped: anterior, posterior, lateral horns) | Peripheral (dorsal, ventral, lateral columns) | | Location in brain | Peripheral cortex + deep nuclei | Internal (beneath cortex); forms tracts | | Primary function | Information processing, integration, reflex centres | Rapid conduction of impulses between brain regions and to/from spinal cord |
3. Gray matter contains: motor neuron cell bodies in anterior horns (spinal cord), sensory relay cells in posterior horns, and interneurons throughout. In the brain, the cortical gray matter is responsible for all higher cognitive functions.
4. White matter contains myelinated axon tracts that connect different brain regions (association fibres), the two hemispheres (commissural fibres — corpus callosum), and the cortex with the spinal cord (projection fibres).
5. Gray matter processes and interprets; white matter transmits and connects. Together, they form the integrated neural network of the CNS.
6. Clinical relevance: Strokes affecting gray matter cause loss of cognitive function, motor, or sensory deficits. White matter diseases (e.g., multiple sclerosis, leukoencephalopathy) impair signal conduction between brain regions, causing coordination, balance, and cognitive problems.
