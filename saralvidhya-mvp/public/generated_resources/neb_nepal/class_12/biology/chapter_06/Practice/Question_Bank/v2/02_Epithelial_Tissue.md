## Q1. Classify epithelial tissue based on the number of cell layers and cell shape, giving examples of each type.
**Type:** Classify  |  **Bloom's:** Understand  |  **Difficulty:** Medium
**Mindmap Path:** `Animal Tissue > Epithelial Tissue > Layers`
**Recommended Diagram:** Classification chart/table of epithelial tissue types

**Answer:**
1. Epithelial tissue is primarily classified by the number of cell layers: Simple (single layer) and Compound/Stratified (multiple layers).
2. Simple Epithelium — Single layer of cells all touching the basement membrane: (a) Simple Squamous — flat tile-like cells; lines blood vessels (endothelium) and body cavities (mesothelium); (b) Simple Cuboidal — cube-shaped cells; found in kidney tubules and thyroid follicles; (c) Simple Columnar — tall column-shaped cells; lines the stomach and intestines; (d) Pseudostratified — all cells on basement membrane but nuclei at different levels; appears stratified; lines the trachea.
3. Compound Epithelium — Multiple cell layers; protective function: (a) Stratified Squamous — most common; keratinised (skin) or non-keratinised (oral mucosa); (b) Stratified Cuboidal — rare; found in sweat gland ducts; (c) Stratified Columnar — uncommon; found in parts of male urethra; (d) Transitional (Urothelium) — can change shape; lines the urinary bladder.
4. Structural comparison: | Type | Layers | Shape | Example | | Simple Squamous | 1 | Flat | Capillaries | | Simple Cuboidal | 1 | Cube | PCT of kidney | | Simple Columnar | 1 | Tall | Intestinal mucosa | | Pseudostratified | 1 (appears multi) | Mixed | Trachea | | Stratified Squamous | Multiple | Flat (surface) | Skin, Oesophagus | | Transitional | Multiple | Variable | Urinary bladder |
5. Modified epithelium includes ciliated, brush-bordered, glandular, neuro-sensory, pigmented, and germinal types, each with structural modifications reflecting their specialised function.
6. The classification is important in histopathology — changes in epithelial type (metaplasia) or abnormal proliferation (dysplasia) are early markers of disease and cancer.

## Q2. Compare and contrast simple epithelium and compound epithelium with five key differences.
**Type:** Compare  |  **Bloom's:** Analyze  |  **Difficulty:** Medium
**Mindmap Path:** `Animal Tissue > Epithelial Tissue > Layers`
**Recommended Diagram:** Comparison diagram of simple vs compound epithelium cross-sections

**Answer:**
1. Number of Layers: Simple epithelium consists of a single cell layer; all cells rest on the basement membrane. Compound epithelium consists of multiple layers; only the basal layer contacts the basement membrane.
2. Primary Function: Simple epithelium primarily functions in secretion, absorption, and diffusion (e.g., lung alveoli, kidney tubules). Compound epithelium primarily functions in protection against mechanical wear, chemicals, and pathogens.
3. Thickness and Durability: Simple epithelium is thin and fragile, suited for rapid exchange. Compound epithelium is thick and durable, suited for high-wear surfaces.
4. Examples: Simple — simple squamous (capillaries), simple columnar (intestine), pseudostratified (trachea). Compound — stratified squamous (skin, oral mucosa), transitional (urinary bladder).
5. Regeneration Rate: Simple epithelium cells are replaced rapidly (e.g., intestinal epithelium renews in 3–5 days). Compound epithelium renews more slowly as it has multiple layers, with basal layer stem cells supplying new cells.
6. The classification of epithelium into simple vs. compound is a critical diagnostic tool in histopathology, where metaplastic changes (e.g., columnar to squamous) signal pre-cancerous conditions.

## Q3. Describe the different types of cell junctions found in epithelial tissue and their functions.
**Type:** Describe  |  **Bloom's:** Understand  |  **Difficulty:** Hard
**Mindmap Path:** `Animal Tissue > Epithelial Tissue > Characteristics > Cell junctions`
**Recommended Diagram:** Diagram of junctional complex in epithelial cells

**Answer:**
1. Cell junctions are specialised protein complexes at the interface between adjacent epithelial cells that serve to maintain tissue integrity and regulate communication.
2. Tight Junctions (Zonula Occludens): Located near the apical surface; form a complete seal between adjacent cells, preventing paracellular leakage of substances. Critical in intestinal epithelium to prevent toxins from entering the bloodstream.
3. Adherens Junctions (Zonula Adherens): Form a belt-like structure around cells just below tight junctions; link the actin cytoskeletons of adjacent cells via cadherins; provide mechanical cohesion and coordinate cell movement.
4. Desmosomes (Macula Adherens): Spot-like junctions that anchor intermediate filaments (keratin) between cells; provide great tensile strength against shear forces; abundant in skin and cardiac muscle.
5. Gap Junctions (Communicating Junctions): Composed of connexin protein channels (connexons) that directly connect the cytoplasm of adjacent cells; allow passage of ions, small molecules, and electrical signals; important for coordinated secretion in glands and electrical coupling.
6. The four junctions create the 'junctional complex' in epithelium: Tight → Adherens → Desmosome → Gap (in apical to basal order).
7. Disruption of cell junctions is associated with various pathologies — loss of tight junctions leads to 'leaky gut syndrome'; loss of desmosomes (e.g., Pemphigus vulgaris) causes the epithelium to blister and separate.

## Q4. Classify glandular epithelium under all five classification schemes with examples.
**Type:** Classify  |  **Bloom's:** Understand  |  **Difficulty:** Hard
**Mindmap Path:** `Animal Tissue > Epithelial Tissue > Modified Epithelium > Glandular`
**Recommended Diagram:** Classification table of glandular epithelium with diagrams

**Answer:**
1. Glandular epithelium is specialised epithelium that is modified for secretion. Glands are classified as follows:
2. By Ducts: (a) Exocrine glands — have ducts; secrete onto surfaces (e.g., salivary glands, sweat glands); (b) Endocrine glands — no ducts (ductless); secrete hormones into bloodstream (e.g., thyroid, adrenal glands); (c) Heterocrine/Mixed glands — both exocrine and endocrine portions (e.g., pancreas — islets of Langerhans = endocrine; acini = exocrine).
3. By Consistency of Secretion: (a) Serous glands — watery, enzyme-rich secretion (e.g., parotid gland); (b) Mucous glands — thick mucus secretion (e.g., goblet cells); (c) Mixed glands — both serous and mucous (e.g., submandibular gland).
4. By Mode/Quantity of Secretion: (a) Merocrine — secretion by exocytosis; no cell damage (e.g., pancreas, salivary glands); (b) Apocrine — apical cytoplasm is lost with secretion (e.g., mammary glands); (c) Holocrine — entire cell disintegrates; maximum secretion (e.g., sebaceous glands).
5. By Cell Count: (a) Unicellular glands — single secretory cell (e.g., goblet cells in intestinal epithelium); (b) Multicellular glands — groups of secretory cells organised into gland structures.
6. By Complexity: (a) Simple glands — unbranched ducts: simple tubular, simple branched tubular, simple coiled tubular, simple alveolar, simple branched alveolar; (b) Compound glands — branched ducts: compound tubular, compound alveolar, compound tubulo-alveolar.
7. This five-way classification is used in histology to identify and categorise glands in tissue sections, and disruption of glandular structure/secretion is significant in diseases such as cystic fibrosis, Sjögren's syndrome, and various cancers.

## Q5. Describe the types of modified epithelium with their structural features and functions.
**Type:** Describe  |  **Bloom's:** Understand  |  **Difficulty:** Medium
**Mindmap Path:** `Animal Tissue > Epithelial Tissue > Modified Epithelium`
**Recommended Diagram:** Diagram showing kino-cilia (9+2 arrangement) and stereo-cilia (0+0 arrangement)

**Answer:**
1. Modified epithelium refers to simple epithelial cells that have undergone structural specialisation to perform specific functions beyond simple lining.
2. Ciliated Epithelium: Cells bear motile cilia on the apical surface. (a) Kino-ciliated — 9+2 microtubule arrangement (standard axoneme); cilia actively beat to move mucus and particles (e.g., respiratory tract, fallopian tubes); (b) Stereo-ciliated — 0+0 arrangement; non-motile; function in absorption (e.g., epididymis).
3. Brush-bordered Epithelium: Cells have numerous microvilli on the apical surface, forming a 'brush border' that greatly increases the absorptive surface area. Found in PCT of kidney (cuboidal) and intestinal mucosa (columnar).
4. Neuro-sensory Epithelium: Specialised epithelial cells that function as sensory receptors, detecting stimuli and transmitting nerve impulses. Found in the olfactory epithelium (smell), taste buds (taste), and the organ of Corti (hearing).
5. Pigmented Epithelium: Contains melanin or other pigment granules. The retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) absorbs stray light to prevent glare and supports photoreceptor metabolism.
6. Germinal Epithelium: Lines the gonads and gives rise to germ cells (gametes). The germinal epithelium of the testis produces spermatogonia; that of the ovary produces primary oocytes.
7. Glandular Epithelium: Specialised for secretion. Includes both exocrine glands (with ducts) and endocrine glands (ductless), classified further by consistency, quantity, cell count, and complexity of secretion.
