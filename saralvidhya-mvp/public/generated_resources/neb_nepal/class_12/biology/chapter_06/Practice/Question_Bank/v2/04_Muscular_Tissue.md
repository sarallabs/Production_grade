## Q1. Compare and contrast the three types of muscular tissue (skeletal, smooth, and cardiac) under six parameters.
**Type:** Compare  |  **Bloom's:** Analyze  |  **Difficulty:** Medium
**Mindmap Path:** `Animal Tissue > Muscular Tissue`
**Recommended Diagram:** Comparative diagrams of skeletal, smooth, and cardiac muscle cells

**Answer:**
1. Muscular tissue is of three types: Skeletal (striated, voluntary), Smooth (non-striated, involuntary), and Cardiac (striated, involuntary).
2. Comparison Table: | Parameter | Skeletal | Smooth | Cardiac | | Striation | Present | Absent | Present | | Control | Voluntary | Involuntary | Involuntary | | Nuclei | Multinucleate | Uninucleate | Uninucleate | | Cell shape | Cylindrical, unbranched | Fusiform (spindle) | Cylindrical, branched | | Intercalated discs | Absent | Absent | Present | | Location | Attached to bones | Visceral organs, blood vessels | Heart only |
3. Skeletal Muscle: Long, multinucleated fibres arranged in parallel. Contracts rapidly and powerfully but fatigues quickly. Red fibres are myoglobin-rich (aerobic); white fibres are myoglobin-poor (anaerobic).
4. Smooth Muscle: Fusiform (spindle-shaped) uninucleate cells. Contracts slowly and sustainably. Two types: single-unit (cells coupled by gap junctions; contract as one sheet, e.g., gut) and multi-unit (cells contract independently, e.g., iris).
5. Cardiac Muscle: Branched, uninucleate, striated cells joined by intercalated discs (containing gap junctions for electrical coupling and desmosomes for mechanical coupling). Intrinsically rhythmic; does not fatigue.
6. Summary: Skeletal = power and speed; Smooth = sustained tone and gradual control; Cardiac = continuous, non-fatiguing, coordinated pumping.

## Q2. Describe the structure of cardiac muscle with its unique features and explain why it is involuntary and non-fatiguing.
**Type:** Describe  |  **Bloom's:** Understand  |  **Difficulty:** Medium
**Mindmap Path:** `Animal Tissue > Muscular Tissue > Cardiac / Heart muscle`
**Recommended Diagram:** Labelled diagram of cardiac muscle showing intercalated discs

**Answer:**
1. Cardiac muscle is found exclusively in the heart (myocardium). Its cells (cardiomyocytes) are cylindrical, branched, and uninucleate.
2. Like skeletal muscle, cardiac muscle is striated, showing A-bands (myosin) and I-bands (actin) due to the regular sarcomere arrangement.
3. Intercalated Discs: The most distinctive feature of cardiac muscle. These are complex junctions located at the ends of cardiomyocytes. They contain: (a) Gap junctions — allow rapid passage of electrical signals (action potentials) between cells, enabling synchronised contraction; (b) Desmosomes — mechanically anchor cells together, preventing separation during forceful contractions.
4. The intercalated discs make the heart a functional syncytium — electrically and mechanically coupled — so that excitation spreads rapidly across the entire myocardium for coordinated pumping.
5. Why Involuntary: Cardiac muscle contracts rhythmically without conscious control. The sinoatrial (SA) node acts as the pacemaker, generating spontaneous action potentials that spread through the AV node, bundle of His, and Purkinje fibres to trigger coordinated contraction.
6. Why Non-fatiguing: Cardiac muscle cells have: (a) abundant mitochondria (25–35% of cell volume) for continuous aerobic ATP production; (b) a rich blood supply from coronary arteries; (c) myoglobin for local oxygen storage; (d) preferential use of fatty acids as fuel for sustained energy.
7. Cardiac muscle can also exhibit the Starling effect (Frank-Starling law): the greater the stretch (filling), the greater the force of contraction — allowing the heart to automatically adjust output to match venous return.

## Q3. Explain the functional significance of red and white muscle fibres in skeletal muscle. Why do different muscles have different proportions of these fibres?
**Type:** Application  |  **Bloom's:** Apply  |  **Difficulty:** Hard
**Mindmap Path:** `Animal Tissue > Muscular Tissue > Striped > Red muscle / White muscle`
**Recommended Diagram:** Diagram showing red vs white muscle fibre cross-sections under microscope

**Answer:**
1. Skeletal muscle fibres are classified as red (Type I, slow-twitch) and white (Type II, fast-twitch) based on myoglobin content, metabolism, and contraction speed.
2. Red Muscle Fibres (Type I): Rich in myoglobin (hence the red colour); abundant mitochondria; rely on aerobic (oxidative) metabolism; contract slowly; highly fatigue-resistant; smaller in diameter. Function: sustained, endurance activities (e.g., maintaining posture, long-distance running). Example: soleus muscle (leg).
3. White Muscle Fibres (Type II): Low myoglobin; fewer mitochondria; rely on anaerobic glycolysis; contract rapidly and powerfully; fatigue quickly; larger diameter. Function: rapid, powerful, short-duration activities (e.g., sprinting, jumping). Example: fast-twitch fibres of quadriceps.
4. Most muscles contain a mix of both fibre types. The proportion varies according to functional demand: muscles involved in sustained postural work (e.g., erector spinae) are predominantly red; muscles involved in rapid movements (e.g., extraocular muscles) are predominantly white.
5. Training can alter fibre composition: endurance training increases mitochondrial density and oxidative capacity (red-like); resistance training increases fibre diameter (hypertrophy).
6. This fibre specialisation allows the body to be energy-efficient: red fibres handle baseline, sustained needs while white fibres are recruited only for peak demands.
7. In fish, the red (lateral line) and white (trunk) muscle separation is even more pronounced, clearly illustrating the evolutionary and functional basis of this division.
