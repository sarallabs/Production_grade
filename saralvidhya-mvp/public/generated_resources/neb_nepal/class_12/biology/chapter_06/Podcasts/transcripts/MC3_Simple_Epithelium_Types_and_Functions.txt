This is the brief on simple epithelium. It's basically a single layer of cells resting on a collagen-based basement membrane that lines our body's surfaces and cavities, acting as the crucial gatekeeper for what we absorb, secrete, and filter, all without its own blood vessels.

First, simple squamous are thin, flat, polygonal cells. So if they're just flat tiles, where do they go?

Think pavement for easy diffusion, like in your long alveoli. Formed by the endoderm, it's endothelium inside your heart and capillaries.

From the mesoderm, it's mesothelium lining outside cavities like the gut and body wall. But for heavier tasks, we need thicker cells.

Second, let's group the 3D shapes, simple cuboidal and columnar cells. Cuboidal cells are equal in height and width with a central nucleus, handling secretion and selective reabsorption in the kidneys and thyroid.

Columnar cells are taller than they are wide with a basal nucleus. Found in the stomach, intestines, and gallbladder, they're like thicker sponges designed to hold and move materials, sometimes using cilia or microvilli to sweep things along or absorb nutrients.

So, what happens when these tall columns get crowded and squished together? Finally, we get pseudostratified epithelium, a total fake-out tissue.

Pseudo means false, so it just looks multilayered because the columnar cells are different heights with staggered nuclei, but every single cell actually touches the basement membrane. Guarding your upper respiratory tract and trachea, some modify into goblet cells to secrete mucus, while others use cilia to filter and sweep dust out of the air we breathe.

Whether they're frat tiles for diffusion or tall columns sweeping out dust, these single-layered cells are the ultimate, hardworking gatekeepers of our internal world.
