This is the brief on compound and transitional epithelium. Drawn from a dense animal biology textbook, this brief cuts straight to the facts about our multilayered tissues, revealing exactly how our bodies build protective outer armor and highly stretchable internal organs.

First, let's explore compound epithelium, specifically stratified squamous tissue. It uses multiple cell layers to bear tension and comes in two types.

Non-carotenized tissue doesn't secrete keratin, keeping internal areas like the buccal cavity, esophagus and vagina smooth and moist, kind of like a soft internal sponge. Carotenized tissue, however, secretes a tough scleroprotein called keratin.

This creates a rough surface, acting as a tough, waterproof, scratch-proof raincoat for your outer skin, hair and nails. Second, let's talk about transitional epithelium, or plastic epithelium.

Built from five to six unique cell layers, its superpower is extreme stretchability to bear stress. So, how does a tissue stretch out without just tearing apart?

Well, the cells physically change shape. They transition from rounded and cuboidal when relaxed, to flat and squamous under tension.

Finally, where is it? You'll find this in the urinary system, specifically, the renal pelvis, ureters, urinary bladder and urethra, allowing for high expansion.

It acts just like a balloon, thinning out as it fills. Plus, there's this wild structural quilt.

The tissue's basement membrane is clearly visible when relaxed, but it completely disappears from view when fully stretched. Whether they are laying down a waterproof shield for your skin or shape-shifting to let your bladder expand, compound and transitional epithelia are the ultimate structural shape-shifters of the animal body.
