To picture it, just think of a communication tower. The soma, or cell body, is the headquarters.

The dendrites are short receiving antennas picking up incoming signals, and the axon is the single long fiber optic cable sending outgoing messages. Second, that tower can't work alone, so we have the essential support staff, the neuroglia.

These cells protect and maintain neurons by producing a fatty insulation called the myelin sheath, but they specialize based on location. In the central nervous system, oligodendrocytes make this myelin, while Schwann cells do the exact same job out in the peripheral nervous system.

So, why do we need entirely different cells just to make the exact same insulation depending on where we are in the body? Finally, let's see how the axon and myelin physically combine to supercharge our reaction times.

The myelin insulation isn't continuous. There are tiny, unmyelinated gaps called the nodes of Ranvier.

These nodes let the electrical impulse jump rapidly from gap to gap, which vastly increases the signal speed. It's kind of like a flat stone skipping quickly across the surface of a pond, rather than dragging slowly through the water.

Ultimately, our body's lightning fast reflexes depend entirely on this perfect, microscopic partnership between the electrical wiring and its cellular insulation.
