This is the brief on muscular tissue. Basically, it's your body's contractile tissue, responsible for all movement, from sprinting to digesting to just keeping your heart beating.

Now, you might be wondering why some muscles tire out instantly, while others literally never stop working. Well, we're going to move from the muscles we consciously control that fatigue immediately, to the muscles we don't control that fatigue slowly, all the way to the ultimate involuntary muscle that never ever fatigues.

First, we have voluntary skeletal, or striped, muscles wrapping our bones, which tire immediately. But they do vary.

Red muscle contains myoglobin, making it an endurance worker for everyday humans and goats. On the flip side, white muscle lacks myoglobin, acting way more like a quick-burst sprinter for elite athletes and fish.

Second, we transition to involuntary smooth, or unstriped, muscle. Since we aren't actively controlling knees, they take much longer to fatigue, generally 10 to 12 hours.

Single-unit muscles act like a synchronized swimming team contracting together as one sheet, like in your bladder or alimentary canal. Meanwhile, multi-unit muscles are like independent contractors doing their own thing, found in blood vessels or the tiny erector pili, that give you goosebumps.

Finally, we reach the ultimate involuntary tissue, cardiac muscle, found only in the heart. It's completely involuntary, but it literally never fatigues.

So, how does the heart beat forever without getting tired? It's all thanks to intercalated discs, structures that separate the adjacent cardiacocytes, or heart cells, keeping the whole network firing perfectly without exhaustion.

Ultimately, whether it's a voluntary sprint or an involuntary heartbeat, the specific structure of your muscle fibers dictates exactly how and how long you can keep moving.
