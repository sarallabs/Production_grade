**[History & Overview]**
Before we dive in, meet the three scientists who built the science of tissues. **Marcello Malpighi** (1628–1694) peered through early microscopes and became the **founder of histology** — the study of tissue microstructure. Over a century later, **Xavier Bichat** (1771–1802) dissected organs *without* a microscope, identified twenty-one types of elementary tissues, coined the word *tissue*, and earned the title **father of histology** and **founder of animal histology**. And in **1801**, **Karl Mayer** gave this science its formal name — *histology*.
> [VISUAL: Three-panel portrait sequence: Malpighi, Bichat, Mayer. Animated text labels display each name, date, and contribution.]

Animal tissues first evolved in **Phylum Coelenterata** — the jellyfish and their relatives — making them the first phylum to achieve a true tissue-grade of organisation. This was the evolutionary leap from single cells into coordinated, functional tissue layers.

Tissues form the second level of the biological hierarchy: Cell → **Tissue** → Organ → Organ System → Organism. Animal tissues come in four types: **Epithelial**, **Connective**, **Muscular**, and **Nervous**.

**[Epithelial Tissue]**
Epithelial tissue covers all body surfaces — outer, inner, and glandular. Its defining features are that it is **avascular** (no blood supply — it gets nutrition by diffusion), has **minimal matrix**, and is held together by four types of cell junctions: tight, adherens, desmosome, and gap junctions.

The number of surfaces an epithelium covers depends on the animal's body plan: two in acoelomates, three in pseudocoelomates, and four in coelomates.
> [VISUAL: Side-by-side comparison diagram of Acoelomate, Pseudocoelomate, and Coelomate body plans, with surface numbers highlighted.]

**Pedagogical Mode: Descriptive & Spatial** — Classified by layers:

**Simple Epithelium** has a single cell layer. It has four sub-types:
- **Simple Squamous** — flat, tile-like cells. If of endodermal origin, it forms the **endothelium** (capillaries, endocardium). If mesodermal, it forms the **mesothelium** (body cavities, visceral surfaces).
- **Simple Cuboidal** — equal height and width, used for secretion and selective reabsorption; found in kidney tubules and thyroid vesicles.
- **Simple Columnar** — taller than wide; non-ciliated in the stomach and intestine, ciliated in the fallopian tube and oviduct, brush-bordered in the intestinal mucosa.
- **Pseudostratified** — appears multi-layered but all cells touch the basement membrane; lines the trachea and bronchi, filtering airborne particles.
> [VISUAL: Clean anatomical cross-section diagram showing all four simple epithelium types stacked vertically with cell shape comparisons and location labels.]

**Compound Epithelium** has multiple cell layers and is specialised for protection under mechanical stress.
- **Stratified Squamous** — non-keratinised in moist areas like the oral cavity and oesophagus; keratinised in the skin and its derivatives (hair, nails, horns, feathers) where it provides waterproofing and protection.
- **Stratified Cuboidal** — rare; found in sweat gland ducts.
- **Stratified Columnar** — uncommon; found in conjunctiva, pharynx, and male urethra.
- **Transitional (Urothelium)** — unique to urinary passageways (ureter, bladder, urethra); cells transition from rounded to flat when stretched.
> [VISUAL: Animation contrasting relaxed vs. stretched transitional epithelium cells, showing shape-change dynamics.]

**Modified Epithelium** shows six specialisations on the free border:
1. **Ciliated** — kino-ciliated (9+2 microtubule arrangement, moves mucus and ova) vs. stereo-ciliated (0+0 arrangement, microvillar modifications).
2. **Brush-bordered** — microvilli for absorption.
3. **Neuro-sensory** — contains sensory receptor cells.
4. **Pigmented** — contains melanin; in the retina.
5. **Germinal** — produces gametes.
6. **Glandular** — specialised for secretion, classified by: ducts (exocrine, endocrine, heterocrine), secretion consistency (serous, mucous, mixed), secretion quantity (apocrine, merocrine, holocrine), cell count (unicellular, multicellular), and structural complexity (simple vs. compound, tubular vs. alveolar).

**[Connective Tissue]**
While epithelial tissue covers surfaces, connective tissue binds, supports, and transports. It is the most diverse tissue type in the body and is classified into three broad groups: **Proper**, **Supportive**, and **Fluid** connective tissue.

**Proper Connective Tissue** is built from three components:
- **Cells:** Fibroblasts (produce fibres), mast cells (release histamine), histocytes (macrophages), lymphocytes, and plasma cells.
- **Matrix:** A jelly-like or gel-like ground substance.
- **Fibres:** Collagen (white, for tensile strength), elastic (yellow, for elasticity), and reticular (colourless, forms frameworks).

**Pedagogical Mode: Causal & Process-Oriented** — Loose proper connective tissue (areolar tissue, the "packing tissue" of the body, and adipose tissue with its white and brown fat) fills space and cushions organs. Dense proper connective tissue — including tendons (muscle-to-bone), ligaments (bone-to-bone), elastic CT, and reticular CT — handles mechanical load.
> [VISUAL: 3D cutaway showing how collagen fibres run in parallel bundles within a tendon, then elastic fibres in a wavy pattern in an elastic ligament. A scanning electron microscope (SEM) style render.]

**Supportive Connective Tissue** provides rigidity:
- **Cartilage** is avascular. Chondrocytes live in fluid-filled lacunae within a matrix. Types: Hyaline (smooth, commonest), Calcified, Fibro-cartilage (strongest, white fibrous), and Elastic (yellow fibrous) cartilage.
- **Bone** is vascular. Osteocytes live in lacunae within an ossein matrix. Long bone structure has an outer compact layer (diaphysis) with a **Haversian system (Osteon)** containing yellow marrow, and spongy ends (epiphysis) with red marrow for blood cell formation.
> [VISUAL: Labelled cross-section of a long bone from epiphysis to diaphysis, with zoom-in into the Haversian canal system showing concentric lamellae and central canal.]

**Fluid Connective Tissue** flows and transports:
- **Blood:** 55% plasma + 45% formed elements (RBCs, WBCs — granulocytes: neutrophils, basophils, eosinophils; agranulocytes: lymphocytes B&T, monocytes — and platelets).
- **Lymph:** A clear fluid derived from tissue fluid that returns to the circulatory system.

**[Muscular Tissue]**
Muscle tissue is specialised for contraction and relaxation — converting chemical energy into mechanical movement. It is mesodermal in origin.
> [VISUAL: Three side-by-side muscle fibre microscopy images — striated parallel fibres, smooth spindle cells, and branching cardiac fibres with intercalated discs.]

**Pedagogical Mode: Causal & Process-Oriented:**

**Striped (Skeletal) Muscle:** Multi-nucleated, unbranched, cylindrical fibres with clear transverse striations (A-bands, I-bands, H-bands, M-line). Organised as myofibrils → myofibres → fascicles → muscle block. Covers bones, voluntary, fatigues quickly. Includes red muscle (myoglobin-rich, found in humans and goats) and white muscle (no myoglobin, in athletes and fish). Muscle contraction explained by the **sliding filament theory** (Huxley and Hanson).

**Unstriped (Smooth) Muscle:** Uninucleated, spindle-shaped myocytes. Involuntary, fatigues after 10–12 hours. Found in visceral organs performing peristalsis. Two types: single-unit (contracts as a whole, e.g., bladder, alimentary canal) and multi-unit (contracts independently, e.g., blood vessels, arrector pilli).

**Cardiac Muscle:** Branched, cylindrical fibres connected by intercalated discs. Located only in the heart. Involuntary but never fatigues. The least abundant muscle type.

**[Nervous Tissue]**
Nervous tissue controls, coordinates, integrates, and regulates the entire organism. It is ectodermal in origin and performs nerve impulse conduction. Two cell types exist: **Neuroglia** and **Neurones**.
> [VISUAL: Electric pulse animation running down a myelinated neuron with saltatory conduction at Nodes of Ranvier, then branching into a neural network.]

**Pedagogical Mode: Descriptive & Spatial:**

**Neuroglia** — supportive cells that can divide (mitosis). In the CNS: astrocytes (regulate neuro-environment, heal injuries), oligodendrocytes (form myelin sheaths), microglia (phagocytic, mesodermal origin), and ependymal cells (secrete CSF). In the PNS: stellate cells (nourish soma) and Schwann cells (form myelin, regenerate nerves).

**Neurones** — the structural and functional units of the nervous system. They cannot divide after birth. Their two parts: **Soma** (cell body containing the nucleus and Nissl's granules) and **Neurite** (nerve processes: dendrites receive signals; the single axon transmits signals away). Classified by:
- Myelin: myelinated (faster, saltatory conduction via Nodes of Ranvier) vs. non-myelinated.
- Structure: unipolar, bipolar, multipolar, pseudopolar, apolar.
- Function: sensory (afferent, receptors → CNS), motor (efferent, CNS → effectors), intermediate (adjustor, within CNS).
- Location in brain: grey matter (soma-rich) vs. white matter (axon-rich, myelinated).

**[RECAP]**
To summarise this chapter: **Epithelial** tissue covers all body surfaces and is avascular, with simple, compound, and modified forms. **Connective** tissue binds, supports, and transports — from soft areolar tissue to hard bone and fluid blood. **Muscular** tissue contracts — skeletal is voluntary and fast-fatiguing, smooth is visceral and involuntary, and cardiac is the tireless heart muscle. **Nervous** tissue conducts impulses — neuroglia support the neurons, and neurons themselves are the irreplaceable signal carriers classified by structure and function.
