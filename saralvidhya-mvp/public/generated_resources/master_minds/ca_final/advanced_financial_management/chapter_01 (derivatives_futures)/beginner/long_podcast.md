The underlying asset is critical because it is the actual source of market risk. Without it, the derivative contract has absolutely no value or meaning. Got it. Now, who are the people trading these instruments? I know there are different types of market participants. Who are they, and what are they trying to achieve?

There are four primary players: Hedgers, Speculators, Arbitrageurs, and Dealers. Let us look at Hedgers first. Their main goal is to reduce or eliminate risk. Think of a jewelry manufacturer who needs to buy gold in three months. If the price of gold rises, their costs will shoot up, and they might lose money. Speculators are the exact opposite. They do not have any physical asset to protect. They are in the market purely to make a profit by taking on risk. They form a view on the market. If they think a stock will rise, they buy futures. If they think it will fall, they sell futures. They use leverage to make large gains, though that also means they can face large losses.

Arbitrageurs are the market opportunists. They look for price differences for the same asset in different markets. For example, if a stock is trading at five hundred rupees in the cash market and its futures contract is trading at an overpriced five hundred and ten rupees, they will buy the stock in the cash market and simultaneously sell the futures. This locks in a riskless profit. By doing this, they help bring prices back into alignment and provide liquidity to the market.

Finally, the Dealers. They are the market makers, right?

Yes, Dealers provide buy and sell quotes to ensure that anyone who wants to trade can find a counterparty immediately. They facilitate trades and hedge their own risks.

There are several big differences. First, let us look at the trading unit. In the cash market, you can buy just one single share of a company. Now let us talk about pricing. How do we determine what a futures contract should trade at? I know there is something called the Cost of Carry model. How does that work?

The Cost of Carry model is based on the idea of arbitrage. It says that the futures price should equal the spot price plus the cost of holding the asset until the delivery date. Think about it: if you want to own a stock three months from now, you have two choices. You can buy the stock today in the cash market, pay the full price, and hold it for three months. Or, you can enter a futures contract to buy it in three months.

If I buy it today, I have to fund the purchase. If I borrow money to buy it, I have to pay interest. That interest is my cost of carrying the stock, right?

Precisely. The interest on the borrowed funds is the carrying cost. Therefore, the theoretical futures price must equal the spot price plus this interest. Under simple interest, the formula is the spot price plus the spot price times the interest rate times the time to expiry divided by twelve hundred.

What if the stock pays a dividend during those three months?

If you own the physical stock, you receive the dividend. But if you hold the futures contract, you do not receive the dividend. The seller of the futures contract is the one who holds the physical stock and gets the dividend cash. Because the seller gets this extra cash, they are willing to charge you less for the future delivery. Therefore, we subtract the dividend from the futures price. The formula becomes Spot price plus Interest minus Dividend.

That makes sense. The dividend is a benefit of holding the physical stock, so not getting it reduces the value of the future. What about Index Futures? An index is a basket of fifty or more stocks, so how do we handle dividends there?

For an index like Nifty, we cannot easily track every individual dividend payment. Instead, we use an annualized dividend yield, which is expressed as a percentage. In this case, we calculate the net cost of carry, which is the interest rate minus the dividend yield. Under simple interest, the formula is Spot price times one plus the net cost of carry times time divided by twelve hundred.

And if we use continuous compounding, which is common in professional finance, the formulas use the exponential function, e raised to the power of the interest rate minus the dividend yield times time in years.

Yes, that is the continuous compounding model. It is mathematically elegant and widely used by institutions, though the basic economic principle remains exactly the same: futures price equals spot price plus carrying cost minus dividends.

That is a wonderful summary of the pricing. Now, I often hear the terms Contango and Backwardation. Can you explain those simply?

Of course. These terms refer to the relationship between the futures price and the spot price. In a normal market, the futures price is higher than the spot price because of the positive cost of carry, like interest rates. This state is called Contango. Futures price is greater than Spot price.

And Backwardation is the opposite?

Yes, Backwardation is when the futures price is lower than the spot price. This can happen if there is a severe shortage of the physical asset in the spot market, making people willing to pay a high premium for immediate delivery. It can also happen in stock futures if the expected dividend yield is very high, which drives the futures price down below the spot price.

Excellent. Finally, let us compare Index Futures and Individual Stock Futures. How do they differ in terms of risk and trading?

Index futures, like Nifty or Sensex futures, represent a diversified basket of stocks. Because they are diversified, they carry less volatility and help manage systematic risk, which is the risk of the overall market. Individual stock futures, on the other hand, represent a single company. They are much more volatile because they are subject to company-specific news, like earnings reports or management changes.

That is correct. With index futures, you cannot easily deliver a fraction of fifty different stocks, so the contract is settled in cash based on the final index value. With stock futures, at expiry, you may have to deliver or take delivery of the physical shares, which requires significant capital.
