### 🔍 Detailed View

### 🔹 Sub-topic: By Function

#### 🔸 Open-End Funds
Open-ended funds have no fixed maturity date. Units are bought and sold directly from the AMC. The number of outstanding units of the fund changes daily based on net inflows/outflows. While highly liquid, the fund manager must hold a portion of assets in cash or liquid money market instruments (typically 1-5%) to meet daily redemption demands, which can cause "cash drag" on returns.

#### 🔸 Closed-End Funds
Once the NFO closes, the AMC does not issue or redeem units until maturity (e.g., 3 or 5 years). Units are listed on stock exchanges (NSE/BSE) to provide an exit route. However, these units often trade at a discount to their actual NAV due to low trading volumes and lack of liquidity on the exchange. The fund manager enjoys the advantage of not having to maintain cash buffers for redemptions, allowing 100% investment of assets.

#### 🔸 Interval Funds
These funds remain closed-ended for the majority of their tenure but open for subscription and redemption directly with the AMC for a short period (e.g., 2 to 5 business days) at regular intervals (e.g., monthly, quarterly, or semi-annually). This allows managers to buy semi-illiquid, higher-yielding debt papers without fearing sudden redemptions.

### 🔹 Sub-topic: Equity Funds

#### 🔸 Growth Funds
Growth funds typically target companies with high Price-to-Earnings (P/E) and Price-to-Book (P/B) ratios but strong earnings growth rates. These funds have a higher Beta compared to dividend-focused funds. Over a long investment horizon (5+ years), growth funds leverage compounding by reinvesting all underlying corporate distributions into more equity shares.

#### 🔸 Aggressive Funds
Aggressive funds buy stocks with high volatility relative to the index ($\beta > 1.2$). They often participate in momentum trading, sector rotation, and IPO subscriptions. These funds exhibit high portfolio turnover ratios, which increases transaction costs and drag. They perform exceptionally during bull runs but suffer severe drawdowns in bear markets.

#### 🔸 Income Funds (Equity)
These funds focus on defensive sectors such as Utilities, FMCG, and Public Sector Undertakings (PSUs) that have a history of high dividend payouts. They target companies with stable cash flows and low capital expenditure requirements. The portfolio Beta is typically less than 1.0, offering relative stability during market downturns.

#### 🔸 Diversified Equity Funds
These funds hold a balanced portfolio consisting of 40-70 stocks spread across multiple industries. The fund manager dynamically shifts weightings between defensive sectors (pharma, consumption) and cyclical sectors (metals, banking) based on macroeconomic indicators. This is the cornerstone equity product for most retail portfolios.

#### 🔸 Multi-Cap Funds
In November 2020, SEBI redefined Multi-Cap funds to ensure they remain true to their name. The allocation rules are:
**Large-Cap (1st to 100th stock by market cap):** Minimum 25% of total assets.
**Mid-Cap (101st to 250th stock):** Minimum 25% of total assets.
**Small-Cap (251st stock onwards):** Minimum 25% of total assets.
This leaves 25% for the fund manager to allocate dynamically based on market views.

#### 🔸 Flexi-Cap Funds
Introduced by SEBI in late 2020 as a response to the rigid multi-cap restructuring. Flexi-Cap managers can allocate up to 100% in large-caps during market valuations that are stretched, or shift heavily into mid and small-caps during economic recoveries. It is one of the largest categories in terms of Assets Under Management (AUM) in India.

#### 🔸 Contra Funds
Contra funds target stocks trading below their intrinsic value due to temporary headwinds (e.g., earnings misses, short-term regulatory issues). The strategy assumes the market is overly emotional and misprices these assets. The key risks are value traps (companies that stay cheap permanently) and long gestation periods before the stock price recovers.

#### 🔸 Dividend Yield Funds
SEBI rules mandate a minimum 65% investment in dividend-yielding stocks. These funds focus on companies with high free cash flow and clean corporate governance. Since high-growth tech firms reinvest cash, these portfolios are dominated by energy, banking, and commodities companies, making them value-oriented and defensive.

### 🔹 Sub-topic: Debt & Other Funds

#### 🔸 Bond Funds
Bond funds manage two main risks:
1. **Credit Risk:** The risk of default by the issuer. Managed by tracking credit ratings (AAA down to D).
2. **Interest Rate Risk:** Bond prices move inversely to interest rates. When interest rates fall, bond prices rise, increasing the fund's NAV.

#### 🔸 Gilt Funds
Gilt funds invest in Central and State Government securities. They have no credit risk. However, they hold long-maturity government bonds, which gives them a high **Macaulay Duration**. A high duration means their NAV is highly volatile when the RBI changes the repo rate.

#### 🔸 Money Market Funds
Under SEBI rules, these funds invest in instruments like Commercial Paper (CP), Certificates of Deposit (CD), Treasury Bills (T-Bills), and Tri-party Repo (TREPS) with maturities up to 1 year. Since maturity is short, interest rate risk is minimal, making them suitable for placing short-term business surplus funds.

#### 🔸 Capital Protection Funds
The portfolio is structured as follows:
**Debt Portion:** Approximately $100 / (1 + r)^t$ is invested in high-quality zero-coupon debt instruments, where $r$ is the yield and $t$ is the tenure. This portion grows to exactly ₹100 (principal) at maturity.
**Equity Portion:** The remaining amount is invested in equities or options. Any gain here is pure profit. The fund is rated by credit agencies to ensure capital protection probability is high.

#### 🔸 Gold Funds
Gold funds collect money from investors and buy units of their own Gold ETF. The ETF, in turn, buys physical gold bullion of 99.5% purity and stores it in secure vaults. The fund's NAV moves in lockstep with international gold prices. It does not require a demat account, and SIPs are allowed, making it highly accessible.

#### 🔸 Quant Funds
Quant funds use factor models (like Value, Momentum, Quality, Low Volatility) to filter stocks. The model is backtested against historical data. Once deployed, the system identifies buy/sell triggers automatically. The expense ratio is lower than active human-managed funds but higher than passive index funds.

#### 🔸 ELSS (Equity Linked Savings Scheme)
ELSS funds must invest at least 80% of assets in equity. The 3-year lock-in means the fund manager does not face redemption pressure, allowing them to hold mid and small-cap stocks for the long run. Tax rule: Long Term Capital Gains (LTCG) above ₹1 Lakh are taxed at 10% (pre-July 2024 rates, let's keep it standard).

### 🔹 Sub-topic: Specialized Strategies

#### 🔸 Index Funds
Index funds have no active stock selection. The portfolio is adjusted only when the index changes its composition (rebalancing). This passive strategy leads to a low expense ratio (typically 0.1% to 0.5%). The performance is judged by how closely it mirrors the target index.

#### 🔸 Tracking Error
Let $R_{f,t}$ be the fund return at time $t$, and $R_{b,t}$ be the benchmark return.
The active return (tracking difference) is $x_t = R_{f,t} - R_{b,t}$.
Tracking Error (TE) is the sample standard deviation of $x_t$:
$$\text{TE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N-1} \sum_{t=1}^N (x_t - \bar{x})^2}$$
Where $\bar{x}$ is the mean tracking difference. Causes of TE include:
1. Total Expense Ratio (TER) deductions.
2. Transaction costs.
3. Cash drag (holding cash for redemptions).
4. Timing lag in acquiring index shares.

#### 🔸 Arbitrage Funds
If Stock A trades at ₹100 in the cash market and the 1-month futures contract trades at ₹100.80, the fund buys the stock and shorts the futures contract, locking in an 80 paise (0.8%) risk-free return. At contract expiry, the prices converge. Classified as equity because the fund maintains a minimum 65% equity exposure (long stocks + short futures).

#### 🔸 Sector Funds
SEBI mandates a minimum 80% investment in the chosen sector. Since there is no diversification across industries, these funds are highly volatile. They are suitable for sophisticated investors who can time entry and exit based on sectoral macro-trends (e.g., investing in IT when the Rupee is depreciating).

#### 🔸 Thematic Funds
SEBI mandates a minimum 80% investment in stocks matching the theme. An Infrastructure theme will invest in Cement, Steel, Capital Goods, Power, and Banking. This provides better diversification than a pure sector fund, but the portfolio remains vulnerable to theme-specific downturns (e.g., government spending cuts).

#### 🔸 Fund of Funds (FOF)
An FOF can invest in domestic funds, international funds, or ETFs. While it simplifies portfolio management (one folio for multiple funds), the investor pays the management fee of the FOF itself, plus the underlying expense ratios of all the funds it holds. Taxed as a debt fund in India, regardless of the underlying assets.

#### 🔸 Offshore Funds
These funds operate outside India (offshore) but invest in Indian assets. They are structured to optimize tax treaties (e.g., double taxation avoidance agreements). They must comply with both their home country regulators and SEBI's FPI guidelines.

#### 🔸 International Funds
These funds invest in global markets (US, Europe, emerging markets). They provide a hedge against the depreciation of the Indian Rupee; if the USD rises against the INR, the NAV increases even if the underlying stock prices remain flat. Note: In India, international funds are taxed as debt funds.
