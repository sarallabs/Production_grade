# Sub-Topic 1.2: Market Types

> **Reference:** Page 2, Table, Page 3

---

### 📋 Summary View

#### 🔸 Trading Venue and Structure
- **Concept:** Forwards are private deals signed directly between two people, usually over the phone or email. Futures are bought and sold on official stock exchanges, like the NSE or BSE, just like shares.
- **Analogy:** A forward is like buying milk directly from a local dairy farmer down the road. A future is like buying a packaged, standardized carton of milk from a supermarket.
- **Key Takeaway:** Forwards are private and informal; Futures are public and highly organized.

#### 🔸 Contract Standardization
- **Concept:** Forwards let you customize everything: the quantity, quality, and delivery date. Futures are rigid; the stock exchange decides the exact contract sizes, quality, and expiry dates, and you must accept them as they are.
- **Analogy:** A forward is like getting a customized suit stitched by a tailor to fit your exact measurements. A future is like buying a medium-sized t-shirt from a store; you have to choose from the available sizes (M, L, XL).
- **Key Takeaway:** Forwards are tailor-made; Futures are ready-made.

#### 🔸 Liquidity Profiles
- **Concept:** Futures are highly liquid, meaning it is very easy to buy or sell them instantly because thousands of people are trading them. Forwards are illiquid; once you enter a contract, it is very hard to exit or sell it to someone else before the contract ends.
- **Analogy:** Futures are like trading gold coins in a busy market—anyone will buy them from you instantly. Forwards are like selling a second-hand custom wooden wardrobe—you have to search for a specific buyer who wants that exact size.
- **Key Takeaway:** Futures can be entered or exited in seconds; Forwards trap you until the contract ends.

#### 🔸 Margin Requirements
- **Concept:** In futures trading, you must deposit security money (called margin) with your broker before you can trade. In forward contracts, you usually do not pay any margin upfront; the entire payment is made at the very end.
- **Analogy:** Futures are like booking a hotel room online where you must pay a security deposit. Forwards are like booking a local taxi where you pay the driver only after you reach your destination.
- **Key Takeaway:** Futures require security money upfront and daily adjustments; Forwards are based on trust and pay at the end.

#### 🔸 Counterparty Credit Risk
- **Concept:** If you trade a forward and the other person goes bankrupt, you lose your money. In futures, the stock exchange's Clearing Corporation guarantees the trade. If the other person defaults, the exchange pays you.
- **Analogy:** If you buy a phone from a stranger on the street, they might run away with your money (forward risk). If you buy it through Amazon, Amazon guarantees you will either get the phone or a refund (futures safety).
- **Key Takeaway:** Futures have zero counterparty default risk because the exchange acts as a guarantor; Forwards carry high default risk.

#### 🔸 Settlement Mechanisms
- **Concept:** Forward contracts are settled only on the final agreed date. Futures contracts settle profit and loss every day (Mark-to-Market), and the final trade completes on the expiry date.
- **Analogy:** Like a tab at a local tea shop. In a forward, you pay the total bill at the end of the month. In a future, you pay for your tea and snacks at the end of every day.
- **Key Takeaway:** Forwards settle once at the end; Futures settle daily and close on the final expiry day.

#### 🔸 Asset Type and Underlying
- **Concept:** In the cash market, you buy the actual, physical asset (like the real stock certificate or physical gold). In the derivatives market, you only trade contracts that track the price of those assets.
- **Analogy:** Buying milk from the store (cash market) versus signing a contract with a dairy to buy milk next month (derivatives market).
- **Key Takeaway:** Cash market is for buying real assets; Derivatives market is for trading contracts linked to those assets.

#### 🔸 Minimum Transaction Quantities
- **Concept:** In the cash market, you can buy just one single share of a company. In the derivatives market, you must buy in fixed bundles called 'lots'. You cannot buy less than one lot.
- **Analogy:** Buying a single apple from a fruit vendor (cash market) versus buying a crate of 50 apples from a wholesale distributor (derivatives market).
- **Key Takeaway:** Cash market allows you to buy any quantity; Derivatives market requires buying in large, fixed lots.

#### 🔸 Ownership and Voting Rights
- **Concept:** Buying shares in the cash market makes you a part-owner of the company, giving you voting rights. Trading derivatives does not give you any ownership or voting rights.
- **Analogy:** Buying a house (cash market) versus renting a house for a month (derivatives market). The tenant has a contract but does not own the house and cannot vote in the society meetings.
- **Key Takeaway:** Cash buyers are owners; Derivative traders are contract holders.

#### 🔸 Holding Periods
- **Concept:** You can hold shares bought in the cash market for as long as you want—even for decades. Derivatives have an expiration date. You must settle or close the contract before that date.
- **Analogy:** Buying a house (cash market) versus leasing an apartment for 11 months (derivatives market). At the end of the lease, you must vacate or sign a new contract.
- **Key Takeaway:** Cash assets can be held forever; Derivatives have a strict expiry date.

#### 🔸 Dividend Entitlements
- **Concept:** If a company pays cash dividends to its shareholders, only cash market investors receive that money. Derivative traders do not receive dividends.
- **Analogy:** Owning a cow (cash market) gives you milk (dividends). Owning a contract to buy a cow later (derivatives market) does not give you any milk in the meantime.
- **Key Takeaway:** Dividends are paid only to stock owners, not to derivative contract holders.

#### 🔸 Investment Objectives
- **Concept:** People buy in the cash market to invest their savings for the long term or to own the asset. People use the derivatives market to protect against risk, make short-term guesses, or profit from market inefficiencies.
- **Analogy:** Buying a house to live in (cash market investment) versus buying a home insurance policy to protect against fire (derivative hedging).
- **Key Takeaway:** Cash market is for long-term wealth creation; Derivatives market is for short-term risk management and trading.

#### 🔸 Payment and Leverage
- **Concept:** In the cash market, you must pay the full 100% price of the stock to buy it. In the derivatives market, you only pay a small fraction (around 10% to 20%) as a security deposit, allowing you to trade larger amounts with less money.
- **Analogy:** Buying a car. In the cash market, you pay the full price of the car upfront. In the derivatives market, you pay a small booking amount to secure the car, intending to settle the rest later.
- **Key Takeaway:** Cash market requires full payment; Derivatives market uses leverage through margin payments.

### 🔍 Detailed View

#### 🔸 Trading Venue and Structure
Because forwards are private agreements (Over-the-Counter or OTC), they are not visible to the general public. Only the two parties know the terms. Futures, on the other hand, are traded on a centralized exchange platform. Anyone with a trading account can see the live prices, buy orders, and sell orders, making the process transparent.

#### 🔸 Contract Standardization
In a forward contract, if a company wants to buy exactly 14,350 kg of grade-A basmati rice on October 12, they can write that down. In futures, the exchange might offer rice contracts only in lots of 10,000 kg with expiry on the last Thursday of October. You cannot buy fractional lots or change the expiry date.

#### 🔸 Liquidity Profiles
Because futures are standardized and traded on an exchange, you don't need to know who the buyer or seller is. You just click 'Sell' on your broker's app and it is sold. In a forward contract, if you want to back out, you must negotiate with the original partner, who might demand a heavy penalty to let you go.

#### 🔸 Margin Requirements
The margin in futures acts as a safety net. Since prices move daily, the exchange uses your margin to cover any losses you make. If your losses eat into your margin, you will get a 'margin call' asking you to deposit more cash. Forwards don't have this daily check, which means no cash changes hands until the final day.

#### 🔸 Counterparty Credit Risk
The Clearing Corporation (like NSCCL for NSE trades) acts as the seller to every buyer, and the buyer to every seller. This is called 'novation'. Even if the person who took the opposite side of your trade defaults, the clearing house uses its settlement guarantee fund to pay out your profits. In forwards, you have to sue the defaulting party in court to get your money back.

#### 🔸 Settlement Mechanisms
In a forward, no cash is exchanged during the life of the contract. On the final maturity date, the buyer pays the seller the full price, and the seller delivers the asset. In futures, cash is exchanged daily based on price movements. On the expiry day, only the final day's price movement is settled, and the contract is closed.

#### 🔸 Asset Type and Underlying
When you buy 10 shares of Reliance in the cash market, you are purchasing a piece of the company. Your name is registered in their books. If you buy Reliance futures, you own a contract that says you will buy or sell Reliance shares later. You do not own the company; you own an agreement.

#### 🔸 Minimum Transaction Quantities
If you want to buy Reliance shares, you can buy exactly 1 share for around ₹2,500. But if you want to trade Reliance futures, you must buy at least 1 lot. The exchange sets the lot size (for example, 250 shares). So, one contract of Reliance futures represents 250 shares, and you must trade in multiples of 250.

#### 🔸 Ownership and Voting Rights
When you buy equity shares, they are deposited into your Demat account. You become a shareholder and receive invitations to annual general meetings (AGMs), where you can vote on company decisions. If you hold futures or options, you only own a contract with a third party. The company does not recognize you as a shareholder, and you have no say in how the company is run.

#### 🔸 Holding Periods
If you buy shares of SBI, you can pass them on to your children. There is no time limit. But if you buy SBI futures, the contract will expire, usually at the end of the month. On the expiry day, the contract is terminated. If you want to keep your position, you must 'roll over' to the next month's contract by closing the current one and buying a new one.

#### 🔸 Dividend Entitlements
When a company like TCS declares a dividend of ₹10 per share, that money is credited to the bank accounts of shareholders who own the stock. If you only hold TCS futures or options, you will not receive a single rupee. However, the price of the futures contract will drop on the ex-dividend date to reflect the dividend payout, keeping things fair.

#### 🔸 Investment Objectives
If you want to save money for your retirement 20 years from now, you buy mutual funds or stocks in the cash market. You hold them and let them grow. If you want to protect your portfolio from a market crash next week, or if you want to make a quick trade because you think the market will fall tomorrow, you use the derivatives market.

#### 🔸 Payment and Leverage
If you want to buy 100 shares of Reliance at ₹2,500, you need ₹2,500 * 100 = ₹2.5 lakh in your account. In the futures market, you only need to pay the margin (say 15%), which is ₹37,500. This is called 'leverage'. While leverage can increase your profits, it also increases your losses if the market moves against you.
