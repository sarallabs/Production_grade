# Sub-Topic 1.2: Market Types

> **Reference:** Page 2, Table, Page 3

### 📋 Summary View

#### 🔸 Trading Venue and Structure
- **Concept Summary:** Forwards are bilateral Over-the-Counter (OTC) transactions, whereas Futures are standardized contracts traded on regulated centralized exchanges.
- **Formula/Rule:** Trading: Forwards (Over phone/OTC) vs. Futures (Exchange).
- **Application Context:** Traded on Indian exchanges (NSE/BSE/MCX) with standard contract specifications (Nifty lot size of 25, Bank Nifty lot size of 15).

#### 🔸 Contract Standardization
- **Concept Summary:** Forwards allow fully customized terms, while Futures feature standardized lot sizes, quality specifications, and fixed expiration dates defined by the exchange.
- **Formula/Rule:** Contract Terms: Forwards (Customized) vs. Futures (Standardized).
- **Application Context:** Traded on Indian exchanges (NSE/BSE/MCX) with standard contract specifications (Nifty lot size of 25, Bank Nifty lot size of 15).

#### 🔸 Liquidity Profiles
- **Concept Summary:** Futures contracts benefit from high secondary market liquidity, while forward contracts are generally illiquid due to their customized, non-transferable nature.
- **Formula/Rule:** Liquidity: Forwards (Less liquid) vs. Futures (More liquid).
- **Application Context:** Traded on Indian exchanges (NSE/BSE/MCX) with standard contract specifications (Nifty lot size of 25, Bank Nifty lot size of 15).

#### 🔸 Margin Requirements
- **Concept Summary:** Futures require initial and maintenance margin deposits subject to daily marking-to-market, whereas forwards typically involve credit extensions with no upfront margin.
- **Formula/Rule:** Margin: Forwards (No margin) vs. Futures (Requires margin).
- **Application Context:** Traded on Indian exchanges (NSE/BSE/MCX) with standard contract specifications (Nifty lot size of 25, Bank Nifty lot size of 15).

#### 🔸 Counterparty Credit Risk
- **Concept Summary:** Futures carry negligible counterparty risk due to clearinghouse novation and the settlement guarantee fund, while forwards expose parties to direct counterparty default risk.
- **Formula/Rule:** Counterparty Risk: Forwards (Borne by client) vs. Futures (Shouldered by clearing corporation).
- **Application Context:** Traded on Indian exchanges (NSE/BSE/MCX) with standard contract specifications (Nifty lot size of 25, Bank Nifty lot size of 15).

#### 🔸 Settlement Mechanisms
- **Concept Summary:** Forwards are settled in a single cash flow at maturity, whereas futures are marked-to-market daily and settled at contract expiry.
- **Formula/Rule:** Settlement: Forwards (Agreed date) vs. Futures (Daily basis/Expiry).
- **Application Context:** Traded on Indian exchanges (NSE/BSE/MCX) with standard contract specifications (Nifty lot size of 25, Bank Nifty lot size of 15).

#### 🔸 Asset Type and Underlying
- **Concept Summary:** The cash market involves trading tangible financial assets, while the derivatives market trades contracts derived from those underlying assets.
- **Formula/Rule:** Asset Type: Cash (Actual assets) vs. Derivatives (Derivatives of underlying).
- **Application Context:** Traded on Indian exchanges (NSE/BSE/MCX) with standard contract specifications (Nifty lot size of 25, Bank Nifty lot size of 15).

#### 🔸 Minimum Transaction Quantities
- **Concept Summary:** The cash market permits trading of single units, whereas the derivatives market enforces standardized lot sizes defined by the exchange.
- **Formula/Rule:** Quantity: Cash (Single share possible) vs. Derivatives (Minimum one lot).
- **Application Context:** Traded on Indian exchanges (NSE/BSE/MCX) with standard contract specifications (Nifty lot size of 25, Bank Nifty lot size of 15).

#### 🔸 Ownership and Voting Rights
- **Concept Summary:** Purchasing shares in the cash market grants equity ownership and voting rights, whereas derivative contracts convey no ownership claims or corporate governance participation.
- **Formula/Rule:** Ownership: Cash (Results in ownership) vs. Derivatives (Need not result in ownership).
- **Application Context:** Traded on Indian exchanges (NSE/BSE/MCX) with standard contract specifications (Nifty lot size of 25, Bank Nifty lot size of 15).

#### 🔸 Holding Periods
- **Concept Summary:** Spot assets can be held indefinitely, while derivatives have finite life spans and must be settled or rolled over at contract expiration.
- **Formula/Rule:** Holding Period: Cash (Indefinite) vs. Derivatives (Must settle at contract end).
- **Application Context:** Traded on Indian exchanges (NSE/BSE/MCX) with standard contract specifications (Nifty lot size of 25, Bank Nifty lot size of 15).

#### 🔸 Dividend Entitlements
- **Concept Summary:** Shareholders in the cash market are entitled to corporate dividends, while derivative traders do not receive dividend distributions, though derivative prices adjust to reflect them.
- **Formula/Rule:** Dividends: Cash (Entitled to dividends) vs. Derivatives (Not entitled).
- **Application Context:** Traded on Indian exchanges (NSE/BSE/MCX) with standard contract specifications (Nifty lot size of 25, Bank Nifty lot size of 15).

#### 🔸 Investment Objectives
- **Concept Summary:** The cash market serves long-term investment and capital appreciation goals, while the derivatives market is used for risk hedging, directional speculation, and arbitrage.
- **Formula/Rule:** Investment Objective: Cash (Investment/Consumption) vs. Derivatives (Speculation/Hedging/Arbitrage).
- **Application Context:** Traded on Indian exchanges (NSE/BSE/MCX) with standard contract specifications (Nifty lot size of 25, Bank Nifty lot size of 15).

#### 🔸 Payment and Leverage
- **Concept Summary:** Cash market transactions require full capital outlay, while derivatives use leverage, requiring only a fraction of the contract value as margin.
- **Formula/Rule:** Payment: Cash (Full value) vs. Derivatives (Margin/Part of value).
- **Application Context:** Traded on Indian exchanges (NSE/BSE/MCX) with standard contract specifications (Nifty lot size of 25, Bank Nifty lot size of 15).

### 🔍 Detailed View

#### 🔸 Trading Venue and Structure
The trading venue dictates regulatory oversight and transaction mechanics:
**Forwards (OTC)**: Bilateral contracts negotiated directly between counterparties (often via banks or brokers). There is no centralized order book, leading to search costs and opaque pricing.
**Futures (Exchange-Traded)**: Traded on exchanges like the National Stock Exchange of India (NSE). Trading occurs via an electronic limit order book, providing price transparency, instantaneous execution, and public data reporting.

#### 🔸 Contract Standardization
Standardization is a prerequisite for exchange trading:
**Forwards**: Parties negotiate contract size, underlying quality/grade, delivery location, and maturity date to fit their exact commercial needs.
**Futures**: The exchange defines all specifications:
**Lot Size**: Nifty 50 is 25, Bank Nifty is 15.
**Expiry Cycle**: Weekly or monthly (always the last Thursday of the month for equity derivatives on the NSE).
**Tick Size**: The minimum price movement (₹0.05 on NSE).

#### 🔸 Liquidity Profiles
Liquidity refers to the ability to transact quickly with minimal price impact. Futures contracts are highly fungible, allowing traders to close a position by executing an equal and opposite trade (e.g., buying 1 lot of Nifty futures to offset a short position of 1 lot). This keeps bid-ask spreads very tight. Forwards cannot be easily offset because there is no public secondary market. To exit, a party must write a canceling contract or pay a settlement fee to the counterparty.

#### 🔸 Margin Requirements
Margin systems protect the financial system from default risk:
**Forwards**: Generally rely on mutual credit lines. No money changes hands at inception. The performance risk is carried until the settlement date.
**Futures**: Requires an **Initial Margin** (usually 15-20% of contract value) to open a position. If the equity in the account falls below the **Maintenance Margin** due to adverse price movements, the broker issues a margin call to restore the account balance to the initial margin level.

#### 🔸 Counterparty Credit Risk
In the OTC forward market, if party A defaults, party B suffers a credit loss. In the futures market, the Clearing Corporation performs **Novation**:
$$\text{Party A} \longleftrightarrow \text{Party B} \quad \text{becomes} \quad \text{Party A} \longleftrightarrow \text{Clearing Corp} \longleftrightarrow \text{Party B}$$
By stepping into the middle of every trade, the clearinghouse guarantees performance. It mitigates this risk by enforcing daily MTM and maintaining a Settlement Guarantee Fund (SGF) financed by member deposits.

#### 🔸 Settlement Mechanisms
Comparison of settlement mechanics:
**Forwards**: Settle on the maturity date. Settlement can be physical (delivery of the asset against cash) or cash-settled (payment of the net price difference). No cash flows occur prior to maturity.
**Futures**: Settle daily via MTM. On the expiration date, the contract is settled. In India, equity futures are settled by physical delivery of shares, while index futures are cash-settled against the index closing price.

#### 🔸 Asset Type and Underlying
The cash market (also called the spot market) is where real assets are bought and sold for immediate delivery and payment. The derivatives market trades contracts. The pricing of these contracts is mathematically linked to the spot price of the underlying asset. For example, if Nifty spot is at 22,000, Nifty futures is a contract whose price is determined by adding the cost of carry to the spot price.

#### 🔸 Minimum Transaction Quantities
This difference in transaction quantities affects the capital required to trade:
**Cash Market**: Investors can purchase a single share (e.g., 1 share of Tata Motors). This makes it accessible to retail investors with limited capital.
**Derivatives Market**: Trading is restricted to lot sizes. For example:
**Nifty 50 Futures**: Lot size is 25. At a spot price of 22,000, the contract value is $22,000 \times 25 = \text{₹}5,50,000$.
**Bank Nifty Futures**: Lot size is 15. At a spot price of 48,000, the contract value is $48,000 \times 15 = \text{₹}7,20,000$.
Traders must deposit the required margin to trade one lot, and cannot trade fractional lots.

#### 🔸 Ownership and Voting Rights
This distinction is critical for corporate actions:
**Cash Market**: Shares are held in the investor's Demat account (managed by NSDL or CDSL in India). The investor is registered in the company's register of members, granting them voting rights and the right to receive corporate communications.
**Derivatives Market**: The trader holds a contract. The company's registrar has no record of the derivative trader's position. Consequently, the trader has no voting rights and cannot participate in corporate governance.

#### 🔸 Holding Periods
The difference in holding periods impacts investment strategy:
**Cash Market**: Investors can adopt a buy-and-hold strategy. They do not face time decay or mandatory liquidation unless the company is delisted.
**Derivatives Market**: All contracts have an **Expiry Date**. For Indian equity derivatives, contracts are available for three concurrent months (near-month, next-month, and far-month). At expiry, the contract must be settled. To maintain a position, a trader must execute a **Rollover**, which involves closing the expiring contract and opening a new contract in the subsequent month, incurring transaction costs.

#### 🔸 Dividend Entitlements
Dividends are corporate actions that affect stock valuation:
**Cash Market**: Investors who hold the stock on the record date receive the dividend payment directly in their bank accounts.
**Derivatives Market**: Derivative contracts do not receive dividend payments. However, the expected dividend is priced into the futures contract. The futures price is:
$$F = S - D + \text{Cost of Carry}$$
where $D$ is the present value of the dividend. On the ex-dividend date, the stock price drops by the dividend amount, and the futures price adjusts to prevent arbitrage.

#### 🔸 Investment Objectives
The two markets cater to different financial objectives:
**Cash Market**: Designed for capital allocation. Investors buy stocks to share in the growth of companies over time. It is a long-term wealth creation tool.
**Derivatives Market**: Designed for risk transfer and short-term trading. It allows:
**Hedging**: Mitigating risk.
**Speculation**: Expressing a short-term market view with leverage.
**Arbitrage**: Exploiting price discrepancies between markets to secure low-risk profits.

#### 🔸 Payment and Leverage
Leverage ratio is calculated as:
$$\text{Leverage} = \frac{\text{Contract Value}}{\text{Margin Paid}}$$
**Worked Example:**
A trader buys 1 lot of Nifty futures at 22,000.
Contract Value = $22,000 \times 25 = \text{₹}5,50,000$.
Margin Required (15%) = $\text{₹}82,500$.
Leverage = $\frac{5,50,000}{82,500} \approx 6.67$ times.
If Nifty rises by 2%, the contract value becomes $22,440 \times 25 = \text{₹}5,61,000$. The profit is $\text{₹}11,000$.
Return on Capital (Derivatives) = $\frac{11,000}{82,500} \times 100 = 13.33\%$.
Return on Capital (Cash Market) = $2\%$.
However, if Nifty falls by 2%, the loss is also ₹11,000, representing a 13.33% loss of capital.
