And that's exactly why regulators and accountants created the Dividend Equalization Reserve, or DER. 

So how does it actually work in practice? How do they stop this dilution from happening?

Think of the Net Asset Value, or NAV, of an open-ended fund as having two distinct parts. One part is the capital component, which represents the value of the underlying securities. The other part is the income component, which represents the interest and dividends the fund has accumulated but hasn't distributed yet.

Ah, so when a new investor buys units mid-year, they aren't just paying for the capital. They also have to pay a premium to buy into that accumulated income pool.

Precisely. Let's use some simple numbers. Suppose a fund starts the year with one hundred units and an NAV of thirty rupees. By the end of January, the fund has earned fifteen rupees in dividend income. If we divide that fifteen rupees by the hundred existing units, we get an income component of fifteen paise per unit.

Okay, so if a new investor wants to buy units at the end of January, they can't just buy them at the opening NAV of thirty rupees. They have to pay thirty rupees plus that fifteen paise of accumulated income.

Spot on. And if there's an entry load, say one and a half percent, which is forty-five paise, that's added too. So the total issue price becomes thirty rupees plus forty-five paise load plus fifteen paise dividend equalization, making it thirty rupees and sixty paise.

I see! So that extra fifteen paise per unit paid by the new investor doesn't go into the general capital pool. It goes straight into a dedicated account called the Dividend Equalization Reserve.

Yes. It's like an entry ticket. The new investor is paying their fair share of the accumulated income up front.

And what happens when someone wants to redeem or sell back their units to the fund? Do they get that equalization back?

They do. If an investor redeems mid-year, they are paid their capital portion plus their share of the accumulated income up to that day. The fund debits their capital account by the face value and debits the Dividend Equalization Reserve by the current accumulated income per unit.

That is so clean! It means that when the fund finally declares a dividend at the end of the quarter or the year, every single unit holder gets paid the exact same dividend per unit, but nobody is diluted. 

Exactly. The long-term unit holders receive the income earned while their capital was working. The mid-year buyer gets a dividend check too, but because they paid that same amount into the equalization reserve when they entered, their net effect is zero. They are simply getting their own prepayment back as a tax-free or taxable distribution, depending on the country.

So it's a zero-sum game for the new investor, but it completely protects the older investors from dilution. 

That's the beauty of it. It preserves the integrity of the returns for everyone involved, regardless of when they buy or sell.
