Can we start by reviewing the organizational structure of mutual funds in India? I know it is set up as a trust, but how do the sponsor, trustees, AMC, and custodian work together?

That is a great starting point. The easiest way to visualize a mutual fund in India is to think of a three-tier structure. At the top is the Sponsor. Think of the Sponsor as the promoter or the parent company. They conceptualize the mutual fund, get it registered with SEBI, and set up the Trust. They are also required to fund at least forty percent of the net worth of the Asset Management Company, which must be a minimum of ten crore rupees.

Okay, so the Sponsor starts the ball rolling and creates the Trust. Who actually represents the Trust?

That would be the Board of Trustees. The Sponsor executes a Trust Deed in favor of these Trustees. The Trustees hold the assets and the cash of the mutual fund in trust for the benefit of the unit holders. Their job is strictly fiduciary. They ensure that the schemes launched are managed by the Asset Management Company in accordance with SEBI guidelines and the Trust Deed. To maintain impartiality, at least two-thirds of the trustees must be independent.

And the Asset Management Company, or AMC, is the company that actually manages the money?

Exactly. The AMC is the investment manager. They hire the fund managers, researchers, and analysts who study the markets and decide which securities to buy and sell. The AMC operates the daily business of the fund but is supervised by the Board of Trustees.

Where does the Custodian fit in?

The Custodian is an independent entity registered with SEBI that holds physical or electronic custody of all the securities bought by the AMC. This is a very important safety measure. The AMC cannot touch the actual shares or bonds; they can only place buy or sell orders. The Custodian handles the delivery and settlement of those securities.

I see. That makes it hard for anyone at the AMC to run away with the assets. What about the Registrar and Transfer Agent, or RTA?

The RTA handles the investor services. They deal with application forms, allocate units, handle redemptions, maintain the investor database, and issue statements or dividend payouts.

Excellent. Now let us talk about the different kinds of mutual fund schemes. The syllabus classifies them by function and by asset class. We already mentioned open-ended and closed-ended funds. What are interval funds?

Interval funds combine features of both. They are closed-ended for most of the year, but they open up for subscription and redemption at pre-determined intervals, like for five days every month or every quarter. This helps the fund manager manage liquidity while giving investors periodic exit windows.

That is clear. Now, what about the classification by asset class? Let us start with Equity Funds.

Equity funds invest primarily in shares of listed companies. Within equity, we have Growth Funds, which aim for long-term capital growth by investing in high-growth businesses. Then we have Aggressive Funds, which invest in high-beta stocks and initial public offerings, aiming for quick speculative gains. These typically have high portfolio turnover.

What is portfolio turnover?

It is a measure of how frequently the fund manager buys and sells stocks in the portfolio. A high turnover means lots of transactions, which can increase trading costs. A low turnover means the manager buys and holds stocks for the long term.

Okay. What are Income Funds under the equity category?

Equity Income Funds invest in companies that regularly declare dividends, such as stable, high-dividend-yield stocks. They are less volatile than growth funds and appeal to investors who want regular cash distributions.

And then we have Multi-Cap and Flexi-Cap funds, right? How do they differ?

That is a common exam point. Multi-cap funds are mandated to invest at least twenty-five percent of their assets in large-cap stocks, twenty-five percent in mid-cap stocks, and twenty-five percent in small-cap stocks. This means seventy-five percent must be in equities, with a strict distribution. Flexi-cap funds, on the other hand, have no such sub-limits. The fund manager can allocate anywhere between large, mid, or small-caps based on market conditions, as long as the total equity investment is at least sixty-five percent.

Flexi-cap sounds much more flexible for the fund manager. What about Contra Funds?

Contra funds take a contrarian approach. They invest in stocks or sectors that are currently underperforming or out of favor in the market. The belief is that the market has overreacted to temporary bad news, and once the underlying problems are resolved, these stocks will outperform the market.

And what about Sectoral and Thematic funds?

Sectoral funds invest exclusively in one specific industry, like banking, information technology, or pharma. They lack sector diversification, making them highly volatile. Thematic funds are slightly broader; they invest in several sectors that are connected by a common theme, like infrastructure or manufacturing. Still, they are considered high-risk, concentrated bets.

Let us shift to Debt Funds. How do Bond Funds and Gilt Funds differ?

Bond funds invest in corporate debentures, commercial papers, and bonds of various maturities. They carry two primary risks: interest rate risk, which is the risk that bond prices fall when interest rates rise, and credit risk, which is the risk that the issuer defaults on payment. Gilt funds, however, invest exclusively in central and state government securities. Since the government cannot default, gilt funds have zero credit risk, but they still have high interest rate risk because government bonds often have long maturities.

That is a key distinction. No credit risk, but interest rate risk remains. What about Money Market Funds?

These are also known as liquid funds. They invest in short-term debt instruments with maturities of ninety-one days or less, like Treasury bills, commercial paper, and certificates of deposit. They are extremely safe, have low volatility, and are perfect for parking surplus cash for short periods.

Let us talk about modern or special mutual fund schemes. What are Exchange Traded Funds, or ETFs?

ETFs are passive investment products that track an index like the Nifty Fifty. Unlike traditional mutual funds, which are bought or sold at the end-of-day NAV, ETFs are listed on stock exchanges and trade live throughout the day, just like shares. A Sponsor creates the ETF and works with Authorized Participants who exchange baskets of underlying stocks for creation units of the ETF.

So ETFs offer real-time trading and usually have very low costs?

Yes, the expense ratio of an ETF is typically much lower than that of an actively managed mutual fund.

What about Gold Mutual Funds?

Gold mutual funds are open-ended schemes that invest in units of Gold ETFs. This allows retail investors to invest in gold systematically without having a demat account or storing physical gold.

And Quant Funds?

Quant funds use quantitative analysis and mathematical models. Computer programs select the stocks based on historical data and algorithms, minimizing the emotional biases of a human fund manager.

Now, what about ELSS? I know it stands for Equity Linked Savings Scheme, and it has tax benefits.

Yes, ELSS allows individuals and Hindu Undivided Families to claim a tax deduction of up to one point five lakh rupees under Section 80C of the Income Tax Act. The most important feature to remember is the mandatory lock-in period of three years. Once you buy units, you cannot redeem them for three years.

What if you invest in ELSS through a Systematic Investment Plan?

If you invest via a monthly SIP, each individual installment has its own three-year lock-in period from the date of that specific allotment. So, the units bought in January are locked until January three years later, and units bought in February are locked until February three years later.

That is a very important point for investors to know. Let us discuss the Fund of Funds, or FoF.

A Fund of Funds is a scheme that invests in units of other mutual funds rather than investing directly in stocks or bonds. It offers great diversification, but the downside is double expenses because you pay the expense fee of the FoF itself, plus your share of the expenses of the underlying funds.

Now let us talk about Hedge Funds. How do they compare to Mutual Funds?

Hedge funds are private investment pools designed for wealthy investors or institutions. Unlike mutual funds, they are not highly regulated. They can use aggressive strategies, including short selling, high leverage, and derivatives, to generate positive returns in both rising and falling markets. They also charge performance-linked fees, which mutual funds are not allowed to do in India.

Now let us move on to some calculations. How is NAV actually calculated?

Net Asset Value is calculated daily. The formula is total assets minus total liabilities, divided by the number of outstanding units. Total assets include the market value of all investments, cash, bank balances, and any accrued income. Liabilities include accrued expenses, management fees, and other payables.

That is straightforward. How do entry and exit loads affect this?

Entry load is a charge when you buy units. It increases your purchase price, also known as the Public Offer Price. The formula is NAV multiplied by one plus the entry load percentage. Exit load is a fee charged when you redeem units, which reduces your payout. The redemption price is NAV multiplied by one minus the exit load percentage. In India, SEBI has banned entry loads, but the formula is still tested.

Okay. What is the Total Expense Ratio, or TER?

The TER represents the annual recurring expenses of running the fund, such as management fees, administrative costs, and audit fees, expressed as a percentage of the average daily net assets. The formula is total recurring expenses divided by average daily net assets, multiplied by one hundred. SEBI caps this ratio to protect investors. For instance, the maximum limit for equity open-ended funds is two point five percent, and it goes down as the fund's assets grow.

So a lower expense ratio is generally better for the investor because it leaves more returns for them?

Exactly. Expenses directly reduce the fund's NAV.

Let us discuss the three plan options: Growth, Dividend Payout, and Dividend Reinvestment. Do they perform differently?

Actually, the underlying investment portfolio and the performance of the fund manager are exactly the same. The difference is purely in the accounting treatment. In the Growth option, all profits are reinvested, so the NAV increases over time. In the Dividend Payout option, profits are paid out as cash, and the NAV drops by that exact dividend amount. In the Dividend Reinvestment option, the dividend is declared, which drops the NAV, but that money is immediately used to buy more units for the investor at the new, lower NAV.

So in Dividend Reinvestment, you end up with more units, but each unit is worth less?

Yes. The total value of your investment remains the same immediately after the transaction, but you now own a larger number of units.

What are SIP and SWP?

A Systematic Investment Plan, or SIP, is a method where you invest a fixed amount regularly, say monthly. It offers Rupee Cost Averaging, meaning you buy more units when the NAV is low and fewer units when the NAV is high. This averages out your acquisition cost over time. A Systematic Withdrawal Plan, or SWP, is the opposite. You withdraw a fixed amount regularly from your corpus, providing a steady stream of income while the remaining balance continues to grow.

That makes sense. Let us talk about valuing non-traded securities. What is the rule there?

Under SEBI guidelines, if a security is not traded on any exchange for sixty days prior to the valuation date, it is considered non-traded. The AMC must value it in good faith. Equity instruments are valued using capitalization of earnings or net asset value, using comparable P/E ratios with a liquidity discount. Debt and government securities are valued on a Yield to Maturity basis.

What are the rights and obligations of mutual fund investors?

Investors have the right to receive dividend warrants within forty-two days of declaration. They also have the right to receive annual statements and inspect documents. If seventy-five percent of unit holders agree, they can terminate the AMC or approve changes to closed-ended schemes. The main obligation is that investors must study the key risk factors in the offer document before investing.

Finally, let us touch on the performance measures. What are the main ratios we need to know?

Even at a beginner level, you should know the formulas for Sharpe, Treynor, Jensen's Alpha, and Tracking Error. The Sharpe Ratio measures excess return per unit of total risk. The formula is portfolio return minus the risk-free rate, divided by the portfolio's standard deviation.

And the Treynor Ratio?

The Treynor Ratio measures excess return per unit of systematic risk. The formula is portfolio return minus the risk-free rate, divided by the portfolio's Beta.

How does Jensen's Alpha work?

Jensen's Alpha measures the absolute return of the portfolio minus the expected return calculated using the Capital Asset Pricing Model. The formula is portfolio return minus the sum of the risk-free rate plus Beta multiplied by the market return minus the risk-free rate. A positive alpha means the fund manager outperformed the market benchmark on a risk-adjusted basis.

And what about Tracking Error?

Tracking error is the standard deviation of the difference in returns between a passive fund and its benchmark index. It shows how closely the fund manager is replicating the index. For index funds, we want this tracking error to be as close to zero as possible.
