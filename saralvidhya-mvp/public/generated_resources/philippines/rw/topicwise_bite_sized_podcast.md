# Topicwise Bite-Sized Podcast Scripts: Critical Reading as Looking for Ways of Thinking

**Subject:** Reading and Writing Skills | **Curriculum:** Week 2  
**Format:** 8 Modular Episodes (Micro-Concepts, ~2 Minutes Each)  
**Speakers:** Host & Specialist  
**Institution:** De La Salle University - Senior High School Department  

---

## Episode 1: The Four Kinds of Reading (Hirschberg Taxonomy)
**Duration:** ~2 Minutes | **Target Word Count:** ~270 words

Host: Welcome to SaralVidhya Micro-Podcasts! Episode 1: Did you know that not all reading is created equal?

Specialist: That's right! Stuart and Terry Hirschberg break reading down into four distinct categories. First is Casual Reading—skimming street signs, text messages, or news alerts out of pure daily necessity. Second is Reading for Pleasure—getting lost in a sci-fi thriller or romance novel at bedtime, where you intentionally relax and enjoy the escapism. Third is Reading for Information—studying a textbook to memorize facts, dates, and formulas for an exam.

Host: But what about the fourth type—Critical Reading?

Specialist: Critical reading is where intellectual maturity begins. It is active, not passive. You treat reading as a live conversation with the writer. You don't just ask, "What does this sentence say?" You ask, "Why is the author claiming this? What evidence supports it? What is being left unsaid?" The casual reader glances, the pleasure reader escapes, the information reader absorbs, but the critical reader interrogates. Resisting gullibility is your superpower!

## Episode 2: The Cognitive Triad — Analysis, Synthesis, and Evaluation
**Duration:** ~2 Minutes | **Target Word Count:** ~280 words

Host: Welcome to Episode 2! When we read critically, what cognitive gears are actually turning in our brains?

Specialist: Hirschberg outlines a brilliant three-step engine: Analysis, Synthesis, and Evaluation. Step one is Analysis: you dissect the text into its moving parts—its core thesis, supporting claims, evidence, and underlying assumptions. Step two is Synthesis: you take the ideas you've analyzed from multiple authors and blend them together to create an original perspective that belongs to you.

Host: And synthesis does something remarkable for a student's attitude, doesn't it?

Specialist: It humbles us! It tampers down intellectual arrogance by proving that on complex issues—like artificial intelligence or public policy—more than one valid viewpoint exists. Finally, step three is Evaluation: you assess the weight of the evidence and place your own weight on the seesaw. You take an educated, nuanced stand without falling into uncritical extremes.

## Episode 3: Demystifying the Author and the Power of Annotation
**Duration:** ~2 Minutes | **Target Word Count:** ~275 words

Host: Episode 3: Why should we stop treating authors like untouchable geniuses?

Specialist: Metherell and Winkler give us one of the greatest pieces of advice in academic history: "Demystify the writer." Kick the author off the throne of public esteem! Writers are regular human beings with personal biases, limited experiences, and flawed assumptions. When you realize authors can make mistakes, you feel empowered to engage with them as equals.

Host: And the best way to do that is marginal annotation, right?

Specialist: Absolutely! Don't keep your books clean just to resell them at the end of the trimester. Write in the margins! Underline the main thesis. Write "Is this really true?" or "What about this counter-example?" Jot down your immediate reactions, look up unfamiliar words, and note powerful metaphors. Annotating turns reading from a silent lecture into an interactive debate.

## Episode 4: Detecting Logical Fallacies and Bogus Claims
**Duration:** ~2 Minutes | **Target Word Count:** ~290 words

Host: Welcome to Episode 4! Today, we're putting on our detective hats to spot flaws in reasoning.

Specialist: Metherell and Winkler remind us that an argument can sound eloquent and still be completely invalid. Critical readers must be on high alert for logical fallacies. First is the Ad Hominem attack—attacking an author's personality or looks instead of their argument. Second is Either/Or reasoning—pretending complex problems only have two extreme choices.

Host: What about false analogies or circular reasoning?

Specialist: Exactly! False analogies compare apples to airplanes. Circular reasoning—or begging the question—just repeats the premise as the conclusion: "We must obey the law because the law must be obeyed." And always beware of "bogus claims"—vague statements like "Studies show most people agree" without naming the study, the sample size, or the source. If there's no verifiable evidence, don't buy it!

## Episode 5: Essay Architecture — Finding the Thesis and Signposts
**Duration:** ~2 Minutes | **Target Word Count:** ~285 words

Host: Episode 5: How do we navigate the blueprint of an essay?

Specialist: In *Writing and Reading Across the Curriculum*, Behrens and Rosen explain that every essay has a spine: the Thesis Statement. It's usually a single declarative sentence located near the beginning that asserts the author's focused stance on a debatable topic—like Mark Twain declaring in *The Lowest Animal* that humans are the only creatures driven by petty revenge!

Host: How do we trace the author's logic from there?

Specialist: Look for signposts! "Premise signals" tell you a reason is starting: words like *because, since, for, follows from, inasmuch as*. "Conclusion signals" tell you the author is arriving at their destination: words like *therefore, thus, hence, consequently, it follows that*. Spot the signals, track the thesis, and you'll never get lost in a complex essay!

## Episode 6: What is Intertextuality? Forms and Narrative Borrowing
**Duration:** ~2 Minutes | **Target Word Count:** ~280 words

Host: Welcome to Episode 6! Today, we demystify our diagnostic topic: Intertextuality!

Specialist: According to Dharwadker and his co-authors, intertextuality is the borrowing or application of a literary, media, or social "text" inside another text. No author creates in isolation; we build upon the stories that came before us.

Host: And it comes in two distinct flavors, right?

Specialist: Exactly. Form 1 is "Book in a Book"—a literary work referencing another literary work. It could be brief, like borrowing a character name or quoting a famous line from Shakespeare, or prolonged, like adapting an entire storyline. Form 2 is "Other Text in a Book"—referencing a media or cultural story, like a hit pop song, a movie, or a historic moment like Rosa Parks's bus protest. Because the referenced event has a narrative quality, it injects a whole second storyline into the text!

## Episode 7: The Three Functions of Intertextuality
**Duration:** ~2 Minutes | **Target Word Count:** ~275 words

Host: Episode 7: Why do authors use intertextuality? What work does it do?

Specialist: Dharwadker identifies three dynamic functions: Comparison, Dialogue, and Destabilization. First, Comparison: when an author references another text, you are asked to compare the original version with how it's being used now—spotting similarities and differences.

Host: And the second function is Dialogue?

Specialist: Yes! Because both the host text and the referenced text tell stories, they engage in a lively conversation. Sometimes they reinforce each other, and sometimes they clash over which worldview is dominant. Finally, function three is Destabilization: intertextuality can shake up your settled understanding! It can disrupt what you thought you knew about the original classic, or it can challenge the assumptions of the new book.

## Episode 8: Transformative Effects of Intertextuality and the Hypertext Link
**Duration:** ~2 Minutes | **Target Word Count:** ~285 words

Host: Welcome to our final bite-sized episode! What are the ultimate effects of intertextuality, and how does it relate to Hypertext?

Specialist: Dharwadker outlines three transformative effects. First, it transforms the primary book, adding layers of meaning. Second, it reflexively transforms the prior text—reading a modern retelling makes you re-evaluate the original classic. Third, it sparks a simultaneous reinterpretation of both texts at once!

Host: And how does this connect to our diagnostic exercise on Hypertext?

Specialist: Think of it this way: Hypertext is technological. It connects digital documents via clickable web links, allowing you to choose your own non-linear pathway across the internet. But Intertextuality is the cultural hypertext of the human mind! Long before the internet existed, authors used intertextual references as intellectual hyperlinks, connecting readers across centuries and cultures. Read critically, follow the links, and expand your thinking!
