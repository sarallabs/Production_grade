# Podcast Audio Script: Critical Reading as Looking for Ways of Thinking (Long-Form Masterclass)

**Subject:** Reading and Writing Skills | **Topic:** Critical Reading as Ways of Thinking  
**Format:** Deep-Dive Academic Seminar Dialogue (Host & Guest)  
**Target Duration:** ~30 Minutes (~4,500 words)  
**Academic Reference Matrix:**  
- Hirschberg & Hirschberg (2009), *The Millennium Reader* (5th ed., pp. 1-3)  
- Metherell & Winkler (2007), *Readings for Writers* (2nd ed., pp. 1-10)  
- Behrens & Rosen (2000), *Writing and Reading Across the Curriculum* (7th ed., pp. 45-55)  
- Dharwadker, Dharwadker, Harrison, Lemaster, & Bourenane, *What is Intertextuality?* (p. 70)

**Institution:** De La Salle University - Senior High School Department  

---

Host: Welcome back to SaralVidhya's Masterclass Series, tailored specifically for Senior High School scholars at De La Salle University. Today, we are exploring one of the most foundational modules in the 2nd Trimester Reading and Writing curriculum: "Critical Reading as Looking for Ways of Thinking." My name is Marcus Vance, and I am joined once again by Dr. Aris Thorne, Senior Fellow in Rhetoric and Textual Hermeneutics. Aris, welcome.

Guest: Thank you, Marcus. It is an absolute privilege to be here. This unit is truly the hinge upon which a student's intellectual life turns. In high school, students are frequently taught to view reading as an act of obedience—an exercise in passive reception where one extracts information to reproduce on a multiple-choice quiz. But what our curriculum asks students to do is radically different: it asks them to treat reading as an intellectual confrontation, an investigative art, and a methodology for examining how human minds construct reality.

Host: Let's begin right at the foundational taxonomy. Stuart and Terry Hirschberg open *The Millennium Reader* with a provocative classification of the reading act. They identify four distinct kinds of reading. Could you unpack these four categories and show us how critical reading separates itself from the others?

Guest: The Hirschbergs provide a very illuminating spectrum. The first category is what they call "Casual Reading." This is the involuntary reading we do every single day. When you glance at a road sign that says "No Left Turn," when you scroll through push notifications on your phone, when you scan email subject lines or skim newspaper headlines, you are reading casually. You don't read because you are yearning for deep truth; you read because modern urban survival necessitates it. It requires minimal cognitive investment.

Host: And right above that is reading for pleasure.

Guest: Precisely. The second kind is "Reading for Pleasure." Think of picking up a gripping murder mystery, a romantic novel, or an epic science fiction saga before bed. What is the reader doing here? The reader is intentionally relaxing their critical faculties. They want to be transported to an imaginary universe. They do not interrogate whether the dragon's biology makes aerodynamic sense, or whether the detective's legal procedure aligns with constitutional law; they surrender to the narrative spell. The reading is uncritical, pleasurable, and soothing.

Host: Which brings us to the third type, the one most students mistakenly believe is the pinnacle of academic reading: Reading for Information.

Guest: That is a critical distinction, Marcus. "Reading for Information" is what students and professionals do as an occupational tool. When you read a biology textbook to memorize the stages of mitosis, or when an accountant reads a tax regulation, the objective is utility: completing a task, executing an assignment, or scoring well on an exam. It demands high attention, literal comprehension, and memorization. But—and here is the crucial limitation—it remains essentially submissive. The reader treats the text as an authoritative repository of raw data.

Host: And that is where the fourth kind—Critical Reading—enters like an intellectual thunderbolt.

Guest: Exactly. Critical reading is active reading. In Hirschberg's evocative phrasing, you engage in a continuous "mental dialogue" with the author. The writer asserts that such-and-such is an undeniable reality, and the critical reader immediately retorts: "Maybe so, but what about this exception? What evidence supports that claim? What hidden interest does this author represent?" The critical reader annotates the margins, tracks unstated assumptions, anticipates counter-arguments, and deduces the real-world consequences of the author's ideas. 

Host: And Hirschberg makes a blunt statement: "Critical reading is not gullibility."

Guest: Yes! Gullibility is the default state of the uncritical mind—the belief that if something is printed in black ink on glossy paper or published on an official-looking website, it must represent objective truth. Energetic curriculum developers designed freshman composition and senior high school reading courses specifically to abhor that cognitive vacuum. When you read critically, you refuse to be intellectually manipulated.

Host: Now, Laurence Behrens and Leonard Rosen, in *Writing and Reading Across the Curriculum*, link this inquiring spirit directly to the historical origins of the essay as a literary genre. Let's talk about Michel de Montaigne.

Guest: What a marvelous historical context! In the late sixteenth century—around 1580—the French nobleman Michel de Montaigne began writing what he called *essais*, from the French verb *essayer*, meaning "to try" or "to attempt." Prior to Montaigne, writing was dominated by formal philosophical treatises or dogmatic theological tracts that claimed absolute, divine certainty. Montaigne said, "No. These are not accounts of objective, settled dogma. These are personal disclosures of a mind exploring its own attitudes, values, prejudices, and assumptions."

Host: The essayist speaks directly to us, without fictional characters or narrative disguise.

Guest: Without shields! When you read an essay, you are invited to witness a sensitive, observant human mind wrestling with conflicting impulses. The writer is trying to reconcile empirical facts with their own subjective values. And because an essay is an "attempt," the critical reader is not required to bow down to it. You are invited into the laboratory of the writer's thought as an intellectual equal.

Host: That brings us directly to the first major pillar of our curriculum: Metherell and Winkler's 14 Guidelines for Critical Reading from *Readings for Writers*. And guideline number two is legendary: "Demystify the Writer."

Guest: It is the single most liberating concept for a young scholar. Most students are conditioned to view authors as intellectual deities. But Metherell and Winkler urge us to "kick the writer off the throne of public esteem." An author is a human being with a mortgage, political biases, blind spots, emotional wounds, and limited perspective. Once you recognize that writers are fallible, the text transforms from sacred scripture into a human product subject to audit and scrutiny.

Host: Let's systematically walk through these 14 guidelines, because they provide the practical toolkit our students need. Guideline one: Read Actively.

Guest: Reading actively means never reading passively in a comfortable haze. You read with a pencil or digital stylus in hand. You underline the thesis. You trace the supporting evidence. If the author makes a factual error or a leap in logic, you flag it immediately in the margin.

Host: Guideline three: Understand What You Read Before Passing Judgment.

Guest: You cannot critique what you do not comprehend. Metherell and Winkler advise students to re-read difficult paragraphs. If you hit an unfamiliar term, look it up in a dictionary immediately. A brilliant technique they recommend is summarizing difficult concepts aloud in your own words. If you cannot explain the author's argument to an empty room, you do not yet understand it well enough to disagree with it.

Host: Now consider Guideline Four, which touches on true intellectual empathy: "Imagine an opposing point of view for all opinions."

Guest: This is the acid test of a critical mind. When an author argues passionately for a position, you must mentally reverse the equation. Metherell and Winkler give the compelling example of animal testing. If an essayist passionately condemns using dogs in medical research, the critical reader must immediately force themselves to contemplate the counter-perspective: What about the millions of human lives saved? Medical research on dogs led directly to the discovery and refinement of insulin, without which millions of diabetics would perish. The critical reader asks the agonizing ethical question: Does a puppy have the exact same moral standing as a human child? Forcing yourself to inhabit the counter-argument prevents intellectual dogma.

Host: That links directly to Guideline Five: "Look for biases and hidden assumptions."

Guest: Every writer writes from an ideological coordinate system. A devout Roman Catholic and a militant atheist will approach the moral status of an unborn fetus from fundamentally irreconcilable metaphysical assumptions. A free-market Libertarian will assume that private corporate management of municipal services is inherently superior to state control, whereas a socialist progressive assumes state welfare is moral duty. Metherell and Winkler encourage students to investigate the author's biographical headnotes: What is their educational background? Who funds their research institute? What is their socio-political allegiance?

Host: And Guideline Six warns us against the great manipulator of public discourse: "Separate emotion from fact."

Guest: Oh, especially in our digital era! Talented rhetoricians routinely drench complex policy disputes in emotionally charged language. They engage in what Metherell and Winkler call "sloganeering" and "bumper-sticker philosophizing." Complex ethical questions like capital punishment are reduced to simplistic slogans: vengeance versus bleeding-heart mercy. Critical readers must strip away the emotional veneer, dismiss the demonization of the opposition, and evaluate the bare logical architecture beneath.

Host: What if the student encounters an issue they know nothing about? That's Guideline Seven.

Guest: "If the issue is new to you, look up the facts." Don't guess, and don't accept the author's selective framing. If an author writes an editorial proposing that foster parents should pay higher insurance premiums because foster children cause more household property damage, a critical reader must contact or research the Department of Social Services, insurance actuarial tables, and child welfare advocacy groups. You cannot pass critical judgment in an empirical vacuum.

Host: Guideline Eight introduces a thrilling interdisciplinary principle: "Use insights from one subject to illuminate or correct another."

Guest: This is pure liberal arts brilliance. Metherell and Winkler illustrate this with a psychological claim: suppose a psychologist asserts that oppressed peoples develop a passive, defeatist psyche that makes them naturally submissive to tyrants. If you know American history, you can immediately demolish that claim! You can cite the Battle of Fallen Timbers in 1794, the Battle of Tippecanoe in 1811, and the Black Hawk War of 1832—where Native American tribes fought desperately against overwhelming odds rather than surrender their sovereignty. Your knowledge of history corrects an empirical distortion in psychology!

Host: Guideline Nine is all about the rigorous evaluation of evidence, and it highlights a fascinating scientific example regarding the recency of data.

Guest: Yes! Metherell and Winkler cite Dr. Susumu Tonegawa at MIT. Before 1976, medical science considered it an absolute mystery how the human body could manufacture millions of distinct antibody variations from a limited set of genes. If you read an immunology paper from 1975, it would authoritatively state that this mechanism was completely unknown. But in 1976, Tonegawa discovered how our genes rearrange themselves to synthesize diverse antibodies—a discovery that won him the Nobel Prize in 1987. If you cite the 1975 paper today, your evidence is hopelessly outdated! Critical readers must check the timestamp of scientific claims.

Host: Guideline Ten asks us to examine the foundational values behind a claim.

Guest: All arguments rest on value hierarchies. When Thomas Jefferson drafted the American Declaration of Independence, his entire argument was anchored in the moral premise that "all men are created equal." When Karl Marx wrote *The Communist Manifesto*, his argument was anchored in the premise that labor is society's supreme moral good. When someone argues that convicted murderers should be publicly executed to satisfy society's thirst for retribution, they are prioritizing vengeance over human dignity. You must ask: What underlying philosophical values is this author asking me to endorse?

Host: Now let's examine Guideline Eleven, which is perhaps the most practical weapon in a reader's armory: "Recognize logical fallacies."

Guest: Logic does not concern itself with whether a premise is morally noble; it concerns itself strictly with whether the conclusion genuinely follows from the premises. Metherell and Winkler catalogue the classic flaws in reasoning:
First, the *Ad Hominem* attack—attacking the author's character, appearance, or background instead of dissecting their argument.
Second, the *Ad Populum* appeal—relying on mass popularity, patriotic slogans, or bandwagon emotions.
Third, the *False Analogy*—comparing two situations that possess superficial similarities but fundamental structural differences.
Fourth, *Begging the Question* or circular reasoning—where the premise simply restates the conclusion in different words.
Fifth, *Ignoring the Question*—distracting the audience with irrelevant red herrings.
Sixth, *Either/Or Reasoning*—the false dilemma that presents a nuanced controversy as a stark choice between two extreme polarities.
Seventh, the *Hasty Generalization*—drawing a universal conclusion from an unrepresentative or microscopic sample size.
And eighth, the *Non Sequitur*—drawing a conclusion that has no logical bridge to the evidence presented.

Host: And Guideline Twelve warns: "Don't be seduced by bogus claims."

Guest: Beware of phantom authorities! Phrases like "Recent studies prove beyond doubt that..." or "Leading scientists agree that..." without citations, peer-reviewed data, or methodological transparency are red flags. Demand verifiable citations!

Host: Guideline Thirteen is the art of annotation. Why is scribbling in books so essential?

Guest: Many students hesitate to annotate because they want to resell their textbooks at the end of the trimester. Metherell and Winkler call this "penny-wise and pound-foolish." The educational value you gain from annotating a text far outweighs a few hundred pesos in resale value. When you annotate, you write down your immediate visceral impressions, note apt stylistic metaphors, flag vocabulary, and record questions you would ask the author if they were sitting across the table from you. It transforms reading into a live seminar.

Host: And finally, Guideline Fourteen: "Understand the writer's opening context."

Guest: This is crucial, Marcus! Writers rarely write in a historical vacuum. An essay is usually an intervention into an ongoing conversation that began long before you entered the room and will continue long after you leave. If you don't understand the opening context, you will be utterly bewildered—like hearing someone yell "Guilty!" without knowing what crime was committed.

Host: And Metherell and Winkler illustrate this beautifully with that wonderful essay by CBS commentator Andy Rooney!

Guest: That is such a brilliant case study. Rooney writes an essay wishing he had authored Henry Watson Fowler's *A Dictionary of Modern English Usage*. If a student reads Rooney's essay without understanding the context, it seems bizarre. But Rooney was invited by the Screenwriters Guild in 2000 to contribute to a special symposium called "A Writer's Fantasy: What I Wish I Had Written." Rooney uses the opportunity to playfully satirize grammatical pedantry—mocking the snooty prohibition against split infinitives, joking about arcane terms like zeugma and syllepsis, and lampooning the fusty rule that the verb "to be" cannot take an objective pronoun, which is why pedants say "It is I" instead of "It's me!" Without knowing the opening context, the satire is lost; with the context, it is rhetorical gold!

Host: Now, let's connect these guidelines to the second major theme in our curriculum: Hirschberg's Cognitive Triad—Analysis, Synthesis, and Evaluation. Aris, how do these three cognitive steps synthesize into a master workflow for high school writers?

Guest: Think of it as a three-stage rocket.
Stage One is Analysis. You break the text down into its constituent elements. You map the author's primary thesis, identify the supporting premises, uncover the underlying assumptions, and scrutinize the evidence.
Stage Two is Synthesis. Once you have analyzed multiple sources—say, three different perspectives on whether online dating strengthens human connection or produces superficial commodification—you synthesize. You don't just paste summaries side-by-side. You absorb the findings, cross-pollinate the ideas, and forge a new, coherent perspective that belongs exclusively to you. Hirschberg emphasizes that synthesis is the ultimate cure for arrogance because it forces you to acknowledge that reality is multidimensional.
Stage Three is Evaluation. You weigh the merits, identify fallacies, and place your weight on the seesaw. You take a reasoned stance. You don't retreat into lazy relativism by saying "everyone has their opinion"; you declare which argument is most coherent and grounded in truth.

Host: Let's also look at essay architecture through Behrens and Rosen's lens. How does a student hunt down a thesis, and how do premise and conclusion signals guide the reader's eye?

Guest: A thesis is the conceptual spine of an essay. It is typically a single declarative sentence that asserts the author's response to an issue that rational people might debate. Behrens and Rosen cite Mark Twain's famous essay, *The Lowest Animal*, where Twain states: "In the course of my experiments I convinced myself that among the animals man is the only one that harbors insults and injuries, broods over them, waits till a chance offers, then takes revenge. The passion of revenge is unknown to the higher animals." Notice how sharp, focused, and provocative that thesis is!
To trace how an author builds their argument toward or from that thesis, students must become sensitive to signpost words.
"Premise Signals" announce the starting points of reasoning: words like *since, because, for, as, follows from, as shown by, inasmuch as, in view of the fact that*. When you see those words, the author is laying down evidentiary track.
"Conclusion Signals" announce the destination: words like *therefore, hence, thus, so, accordingly, in consequence, it follows that, we may infer, consequently, proves that*. When you see those words, the author is cashing their logical check!

Host: Now, let's pivot to the final major intellectual territory of our Week 2 curriculum, which is also the focus of our Diagnostic Entry Essay: Intertextuality and Hypertext, grounded in the theoretical framework of Dharwadker, Harrison, Lemaster, and Bourenane. Aris, what is the precise academic definition of intertextuality?

Guest: In its broadest and most rigorous sense, intertextuality is the reference to or application of a literary, media, or social "text" within another literary, media, or social text. When an author writes, they do not create in an empty room; they create within a vast, echoing cultural library. Every text is a mosaic of quotations, allusions, and borrowings.

Host: Dharwadker and his co-authors divide intertextuality into two distinct forms. What are they?

Guest: The first form is what they call "Book in a Book"—the presence of a literary text inside a second literary text. This can be brief, such as an author mentioning the title of another book, adopting a famous character's name—like naming a brooding character Hamlet—or revisiting an iconic scene. Or it can be prolonged, such as adapting an entire storyline or reimagining a classic narrative from a different perspective, the way Jean Rhys reimagined Charlotte Brontë's *Jane Eyre* in *Wide Sargasso Sea*.
The second form is "Other Text in a Book"—the presence of a media or social "text" inside a literary work. An author might reference a song, a movie, or a profound cultural event like the assassination of John F. Kennedy or Rosa Parks's refusal to give up her seat on a segregated bus. What makes this intertextual—rather than mere background noise—is that the referenced cultural event has a distinct narrative quality. It brings its own pre-existing storyline, moral weight, and cultural resonance into the host book.

Host: And Dharwadker identifies three vital functions that intertextuality performs.

Guest: Yes! First is Comparison. By placing two narratives together, the author creates an implicit comparative engine. The reader is forced to contrast the original narrative with how it is reframed in the new work.
Second is Conversational Dialogue. Because both the host text and the borrowed intertext are dynamic narratives, they enter into a multi-vocal conversation. Sometimes they harmonize, but often they conflict, creating competing dialogues about whose worldview or morality is legitimate.
Third is Destabilization. This is the most subversive function. The entry of an intertext can shake up our comfortable assumptions about the host story, or conversely, the host book can expose disturbing, unexamined biases in the original classic narrative!

Host: Which leads directly to Dharwadker's three transformative effects!

Guest: Exactly.
Effect One: Transformation of the Primary Book. The intertext deepens our appreciation of the new book's themes.
Effect Two: Transformation of the Prior Text. This is what Dharwadker calls "reflexive re-reading." Even if the original text is not changed by a single syllable, the fact that you now see it filtered through a modern lens permanently alters how you understand the original classic.
And Effect Three: Simultaneous Reinterpretation. A reciprocal, back-and-forth dialectic where both texts interrogate each other simultaneously!

Host: Now, how does this intertextual matrix relate to the diagnostic exercise on Hypertext?

Guest: That is the brilliant connection our curriculum makes. Hypertext is the technological realization of non-linear reading. In a digital hypertext environment, web links allow readers to break free from sequential, top-to-bottom reading and forge their own exploratory paths across nodes. But intertextuality is the cognitive, hermeneutic precursor to hypertext! Long before Tim Berners-Lee invented the World Wide Web, human literature was already an interconnected, associative web of hyperlinks across centuries! Hypertext links documents electronically; intertextuality links human consciousness culturally.

Host: What an extraordinary synthesis. Dr. Thorne, as we close this masterclass, what final guidance do you offer to our Senior High School students preparing to write their critical critiques and diagnostic essays?

Guest: Demystify the text. Pick up your pencil. Hunt for the thesis and its premise signals. Uncover the author's hidden value systems. Check the timestamp on the evidence. And whenever you read, ask yourself: What prior stories, cultural echoes, and intertextual threads are conversing beneath this page? When you read like that, you are no longer just decoding letters—you are mastering ways of thinking.

Host: Splendidly articulated. Thank you, Dr. Thorne, and thank you to our scholars at De La Salle University. Until next time, read actively, think deeply, and question relentlessly!
